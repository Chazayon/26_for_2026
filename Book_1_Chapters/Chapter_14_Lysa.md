# Chapter 14 — *The Crash of Glass*
### Lysa | POV

Cyran looks like he’s made of glass that’s already shattered but hasn’t fallen yet. 

The Devotions Hall is still echoing with applause when I decide to break something.

Thessen basks in it.

He stands at the edge of the salt-ring, one manicured hand lifted, the other resting on Cyran’s bare shoulder as if he’s pinning a trophy to the hearth. His voice rolls over the crowd, praising the Vigil’s efficiency, the reliability of properly managed assets, the exceptional returns on soul futures. 

I hear none of it properly.

All my attention is on the man beside him.

From a distance, Cyran is flawless.

He’s holding the dueling blade low and casual, blood still painted neatly along the glass-steel tip, posture loose, head tilted just enough that the collar charms catch the light. His mouth is curved in a half-smile that says of course I did, what else am I for? 

Up close—which, unfortunately, I am—I see the cracks.

His hand on the hilt is trembling. Not much. Just enough that the reflection of the chandelier shivers along the blade. His chest rises and falls too fast for someone who barely exerted himself in that “fight.” His eyes are wrong—too bright, too wide, pupils blown in a way that has nothing to do with bloodlust and everything to do with panic. 

Hyperventilating, my mind supplies, clinically. Masked by performance breathing.

If he snaps here, in front of this crowd, they’ll see the fake.

If they drag the “corpse” back too soon and find it breathing, they’ll see the lie.

If Thessen asks for an encore—

I cannot let this continue.

The applause crests again as the guards haul the “body” away, limp and convincingly dead. Around me, lords and magistrates clap, some with the enthusiasm of people at a public hanging, others with the polite distance of men applauding a new ledger entry. 

My own hands stay still on the rail until it would be suspicious not to move them. Then I make them clap twice, precise and empty, and stop.

“A remarkable asset,” someone to my left murmurs. “Imagine if the Tribunal had a whole kennel of them.”

I don’t look at him.

Assets. Kennel. Trash. 

Words scrape bone.

Thessen lifts Cyran’s arm like a victor’s. The audience roars. Cyran’s fingers flex once on the hilt, a brief clench of white knuckles, then steady.

The silk over his ribs is still immaculate except for the deliberate smear where he wiped the blade. There should be more blood if that blow had done what it pretended to do. There should be a different kind of slump to the body. A certain weight to finality. 

I saw what he actually did.

He chose mercy when there was no room for mercy.

My job is to write down what happens, not how I feel about it.

Right now, I feel like if I don’t move, I’ll watch him shatter in front of everyone.

A hero waits for the right moment. I just break things. 

Thessen is already turning, drawing Cyran with him up a side stair toward the private boxes where contracts will be signed. Investors lean in, hungry, pulling them into a knot of velvet and bone-jewelry. For the first time since the duel started, the spotlight isn’t on him.

This is my window.

I push away from the rail.

The benches sway underfoot as I move, each step a careful dance around gowns and boots and the occasional carelessly extended leg. People are standing now, chattering, reliving the best moments of the show. Laus about the clean kill. Speculation about how many Marks that one will be worth.

No one is looking at the walls.

I am.

I trace the curve of the Devotions Hall with my eyes as I walk, mapping lines of bone and shadow. Power in a place like this doesn’t just sit in the circle. It needs infrastructure. Conduits. A spine.

There.

Half-hidden behind a sculpted screen of rib-bone near the rear exit, a crystal pillar rises from floor to ceiling, threaded through with sigils that glow a steady, impotent blue. The light from it feeds the chandeliers, the wards, the dainty floating candles that hover in little clusters above the aristocrats’ heads. 

The Node.

If I were a responsible Tribunal officer, I would note that such a critical, volatile piece of tech-magic is insufficiently shielded in a public venue.

Tonight, I’m going to take advantage of that.

“Investigator?” a voice says at my elbow.

I turn, heart jerking.

It’s only a footman. Young, pale with the strain of carrying trays through this crush. His livery is bone-white, already sweat-stained at the collar. He offers me a fresh glass of wine with hands that shake slightly. 

“Ma’am?” he prompts when I just stare.

“Thank you,” I say automatically, and take it.

He looks relieved to have one less glass to balance and slips away.

I stand there a heartbeat longer, the goblet cold in my ink-stained fingers, pulse thudding in my throat.

I’m not here to be charmed. I’m here to determine if you’re following your oaths. 

That was the job, once.

There’s no version of “following oaths” that includes what I just watched.

If I stay in my seat, if I take notes, if I file a beautifully composed report later while Cyran shakes apart or gets locked deeper into whatever cage Thessen has prepared—

That’s complicity.

Quiet, precise anger threads through my ribs, replacing the earlier nausea.

I set the untouched wine on the nearest flat surface and step around the rib-bone screen toward the Node.

Up close, the crystal is as tall as I am and twice as wide, its facets humming faintly under my palms when I lay a hand against it. The sigils inside swirl like trapped lightning. Someone with more training than me has laid careful latticework to distribute the load: clusters for lighting, clusters for temperature, clusters for barriers. 

I don’t need to understand all of it.

I only need it all to fail for long enough to steal one man.

Behind me, Thessen laughs. Cyran doesn’t.

My fingers find the seam of the curtain that hangs to one side of the Node, an ornamental scrap meant to hide the purely functional pillar from delicate eyes. I slip behind it.

The fabric muffles the hall slightly, like stepping into another room. It smells of dust and expensive dye. The Node’s glow paints the inside of the curtain blue-white.

I reach into my sleeve.

The stun-baton sits snug along my forearm, Tribunal-issue, rune-etched. I’ve used it to drop rampaging god-spawn constructs, to stun violent petitioners, once to knock a drunk junior magistrate off me when he mistook my silence for consent. 

There’s a small dial near the base. Official training says Never turn past setting three. Anything beyond that and you stop stunning and start frying.

I turn it to five.

“Sorry, equipment manual,” I murmur.

My hand shakes once.

I flatten it against the crystal.

The Node’s hum sharpens. Sparks shimmer briefly under my palm where the batons sigils answer the pillar’s. There’s a high, teeth-aching whine building in the air now, too high for most of the humans in the room to consciously notice yet.

“Lysa,” I tell myself, because if I don’t say my own name, I might forget I exist outside of scrolls and signatures. “If you’re going to break a law, do it properly.”

I shove the baton into the crystal.

For an instant, nothing happens.

Then everything does.

Light erupts from the contact point, a bloom of blinding white. The baton bucks in my grasp like a live thing; I clamp down, jaw locking. Heat flares across my fingers, racing up the bones of my hand.

The Node screams.

That’s the only word for it—the crystal’s hum jumps into a shrieking keen as sigils overload, flaring so bright the pillar goes white-hot. Cracks spiderweb out from where I’ve jammed the baton in. Tiny shards shear off, ringing against the floor like glass rain. 

Then the world explodes.

BOOM.

The sound hits my chest like a swung door. The pillar shatters outward in a spray of glittering fragments. The curtain takes some of it; the rest rips through into the hall, peppering the front rows with harmless, shocked starlight. 

The lights die.

Not dim. Not flicker.

Die.

The chandeliers go blind. The candles wink out. The wards stutter, their faint blue glows snuffed. For one beautiful, terrifying heartbeat, the Devotions Hall is plunged into absolute black.

The smell of ozone punches through the incense, sharp and clean and wrong. My hair lifts with the charge. 

People scream.

“Assassin!” someone bellows.

“Stay calm!” another voice tries, and immediately gets drowned in the rising wave of panic.

Perfect.

I rip the baton free of the jagged stump of the Node. It’s heiß to the touch now, the metal casing warm against my palm. I thumb the dial back down by instinct.

No time to check damage. No time to think about the level of treason I’ve just committed.

I know this room.

I counted steps when I came in. Mapped the benches, the rail, the path to the circle.

I close my eyes in the dark and see it all as a diagram.

Then I move.

The aisle that was clear a moment ago is now a crush of bodies, people trying to stand, to push toward doors that haven’t been opened yet. Hands flail; someone’s fan smacks my shoulder; a woman’s jeweled hairpiece catches my sleeve.

“Watch it!” she shrieks, more offended than afraid.

“Sit down,” I snap, because it’s the quickest way to get her out of my way.

Authority does what the dark can’t—she freezes in instinctive obedience, giving me just enough space to slip past. 

I drop down the narrow stair to the lower tier, counting the steps by feel. Five, six, seven, turn. The central aisle is a tangle of shadows and noise now, people stumbling toward the exit arch.

Over it all, I hear guards shouting.

“Seal the doors! No one in or out until—”

Until what? Until they figure out if this was an attack or a malfunction? Until Thessen can get his magic consultants to tell him whether the Prime Contract is offended?

I don’t intend to be here when they decide.

The circle is ahead.

I can’t see it, but I know where it is—the faint give in the floor where salt meets bare stone, the lingering tingle of warding around its perimeter. I let my boots scrape once, deliberately, feeling for the grind. 

There.

Inside the ring, empty air. No prisoner now. No blade. No Cyran—

No. He went up the side stair. Toward the private boxes. Toward the—

A hand slams into my side.

I twist on instinct, baton up.

“Move, godsdammit!” some lord shouts, shoulder-checking me as he barrels past. He’s all sweat and perfume and too much wine. His ring catches the edge of my ribs hard enough to bruise.

“Then don’t run blind,” I snap back, but he’s already gone, shoving at whoever’s in his way next.

I breathe.

Think.

Thessen led him up that stair. Right-hand side of the hall, three steps up from the lowest tier, then a corridor to the boxes. The Node explosion would have hit them with light, but not with shrapnel. They’re probably fine. They might already be moving.

I can’t see them from here.

But Cyran will come down.

He’s trained for darkness so deep even god-bloods can’t see. He grew up in rites where the lights went out and the knives came out with them. His body will move before his thoughts do. 

I need to be where he reaches ground.

The aisle to the boxes runs along the right wall. I change direction, elbowing back through the panicking crowd, using my baton as a sort of blunt plow to create space.

“Tribunal!” I shout once, projecting from my diaphragm. “Make way!”

The word still has enough weight in this city that people flinch aside even when they can’t see me. I slip through the gaps they leave.

Something flickers overhead.

Not full light—just the first tremor of an emergency charm trying to come online and failing, sputtering in weak blue strobe before dying again. Not long now before some redundancy system kicks in properly. 

I reach the base of the box stair just as someone comes down it at speed.

The impact nearly knocks me backward.

Hands close on me—hard, fast. One clamps around my upper arm, the other shoves at my shoulder, pinning me briefly against the wall with more force than the hall’s ornamental bone ever expected to bear.

For a split, dizzying moment, all I register is the closeness.

His body pressed along mine, silk and leather and the heat of him. The sharp, cold snap of his breath near my ear. The faint metallic tang of someone else’s blood still drying on his skin. 

Predator, something old inside me whispers. Run.

“Get off—” I start, baton already sliding up between us.

“Don’t,” he growls, low and dangerous. “I will break your—”

“It’s me,” I hiss.

His grip tightens reflexively at the sound of my voice, then slackens all at once.

“Lysa,” he says, like pulling himself out of water. The snarl bleeds out of the word, leaving it bare. “Of course it’s you. Who else would blow up a ballroom for fun?”

“I’m about to blow up more than that if we don’t move,” I say. “Come on.”

I grab his wrist this time.

His skin is too warm under my fingers, pulse a jittering staccato against my thumb. He’s vibrating with leftover adrenaline and whatever passes for terror in a body that doesn’t quite remember how to be mortal. 

He doesn’t hesitate.

He falls in step with me instantly, letting me lead through the dark as if I know this place better than he does.

We weave.

The crowd is thicker closer to the doors, a solid knot of people pushing toward what they think is safety. In the blackout, all their practiced elegance collapses—lords shouting, ladies cursing inventively when their skirts are stepped on, someone sobbing near the back. 

“Doors will be sealed!” a guard bellows. “By order of Lord Thessen—”

“The doors are blocked,” Cyran says in my ear, voice close enough that I feel it vibrate against my jaw. “Wards. Even if they’ve dropped with your little fireworks display, they’ll be checking everyone who goes through.”

“Alternative?” I ask.

“Terrace.”

Of course.

I felt the bite of night air on my face when we arrived, when the doors to the terrace had been open to show off the view. A suspended balcony, glass rails, a charming drop into nothing.

“Lead,” I say.

He doesn’t.

He matches my pace, but his hand stays where it is, curled around my wrist now instead of in my arm, as if he’s decided that letting go in this chaos would be worse than trusting my sense of direction.

We angle left, away from the main doors, toward the tall windows that line the far wall. They loom as darker rectangles in the general black, faintly backlit by the paler night beyond.

The emergency charms try again.

This time, a few manage to stick—thin, sickly-blue witchlights stutter to life along the ceiling, throwing the hall into a jerky, uneven strobe. The effect is disorienting: faces appear and vanish, frozen mid-shout, mid-laugh, mid-panic. The broken stump of the Node glows faintly where I left it, cracks pulsing like a weak heartbeat. 

We don’t have long.

“There,” Cyran says.

The terrace doors are just to the right of the fountain, glass from floor to arch, framed in ornately carved god-bone. In the strobe, I can just make out the outline of the latch. 

“Locked,” I say.

“Not for long.”

He lets go of me.

Cold hits my skin where his hand was.

He strides the last few steps alone, putting his shoulder to the glass as if he’s only intending to lean. Then his boot snaps up, precise and vicious, striking just above the handle.

The pane fractures.

For an instant, it looks like the ballroom floor—a spiderweb of cracks spreading in a beautiful, horrifying pattern. Then the whole thing gives, shards collapsing outward in a glittering waterfall. 

The wind slams in.

Cold, sharp, full of marrow-mist from the Ribcage far below. It tastes of damp bone and distant market bells and the honest, dangerous dark of outside. 

Screams spike again behind us.

“Absolutely not,” I mutter, as the full scale of the drop registers.

The terrace juts out from the Spine like a tongue of glass and stone. Beyond its broken rail, the city falls away in layers of rib and vertebra, lit in patches by marrow-lamps and shrine-fires. Directly below, maybe ten feet down, a narrower servants’ balcony clings to the underside of the Devotions Hall, half in shadow. 

“That one,” Cyran says, pointing. “I’ve used it to sneak out for less exciting reasons.”

“Ten feet is not a small fall,” I say.

“For me, no,” he agrees. “For you, it’s… negotiable.”

I give him a look I know he can see even in this light.

“Do you trust me?” he asks.

It’s the same question he asked in the ossuary, with my blood in his mouth and my life in his hands. 

I didn’t answer then.

I don’t have time to answer now.

“No,” I snap. “But jump.”

His grin flashes—sharp, brief, completely inappropriate.

“As you command, Investigator.”

He moves first.

One step onto the terrace, broken glass crunching under his boots, then he’s on the rail, balanced with obscene ease. For a heartbeat, framed by stuttering blue light and the black gulf beyond, he looks like the statues the temple used to carve of victorious saints. Beautiful. Wrong. 

He turns and holds out his hand.

“Come along, then,” he says. “Before someone decides to light me up like a target practice dummy.”

My stomach swoops.

I step out.

The night hits me full in the face now, cold and clean and laced with the faint ozone tang still leaking from the overtaxed wards. The Devotions Hall behind me is a jittering nightmare of half-light and chaos. The terrace ahead is a narrow strip over eternity. 

I put one foot on the rail.

My skirts catch, of course. The heavy Tribunal formal cut was not designed for vaulting off balconies. The hem tangles around my ankle at exactly the wrong moment.

Cyran’s hand closes around my waist.

“Got you,” he says quietly.

There’s no tease in it this time. No smirk. Just steady, anchoring pressure.

I breathe in once, sharply.

“I hate this,” I inform him.

“I know,” he says. “On three?”

“Do not you dare count—”

He doesn’t.

He just pulls.

We go.

For a split second, there’s nothing under my boots but air. The city opens below—a stomach-twisting drop past gleaming ribs, the distant glimmer of streetlamps like a scattering of embers. The wind tears the breath from my mouth. 

Then he twists in the air, turning us.

I feel the shift in his muscles where his body meets mine, the way he angles himself so he will hit first. One arm wraps more securely around my waist, the other extends, bracing.

We slam into the servants’ balcony.

The impact explodes up my legs. Pain flashes white behind my eyes. My ankle rolls; something in my dress gives with a sharp rip that would have been mortifying under any other circumstances. 

We tumble.

He takes most of it—he’s heavier, stronger, less breakable—but I still hit stone hard enough to bruise. My shoulder cracks against the balustrade; my hip smacks the floor. For a moment, all I can do is gasp.

Cyran’s body is over mine, shielding.

I become aware, in quick shards, of details:

His chest heaving against my ribs.

The silk of his vest under my scraped palms.

The fact that he’s missing one boot now, the other still laced impeccably up his calf. 

The wet trickle of blood—mine, not his—where a shard of glass has nicked my forearm.

I laugh.

It bursts out of me, half-hysterical, too loud for the tiny space. He startles, then huffs something that’s not quite a laugh and not quite a sob into my hair.

“Next time,” I say, once I can breathe, “we take the stairs.”

“Next time,” he says, voice rough, “I’ll request a quiet dinner.”

He pushes himself up, weight shifting away from me, and then offers a hand down.

His palm is still trembling.

I take it.

Pain protests in my ankle when I stand, a sharp, bright flare that tells me nothing is broken but some tendons will have words with me tomorrow. The rip in my dress climbs scandalously up my thigh, cold air licking bare skin. I drag the fabric down as best I can, aware in some distant, absurd part of my brain that this is the least dignified I have ever looked in front of an audit subject. 

He doesn’t comment.

He sways once, almost imperceptibly, and his hand shoots out to grip the low wall.

The mask is gone.

Up close in the weak spill of light from above, he looks wrecked. Eyes too wide, the veins in his throat standing out, lips parted around breaths that come a fraction too fast. The gold dust on his collarbones is smudged now; the smear of blood on his vest has dried to a dull brown.

“Cyran,” I say quietly.

He flinches.

Not from me. From the sound.

“We need to move,” he says, words clipped. “Before Thessen remembers that the terrace exists and sends his dogs sniffing along it.”

“Can you run?” I ask.

He huffs a humorless breath.

“Can you?” he counters.

“Argument later,” I say. “Stairs. There.”

A narrow service stair twists down from the balcony into the guts of the estate, barely wide enough for one person at a time. The stone is worn in the center from years of servant feet. It smells of cold grease and overcooked food.

I start down.

He follows, staying just behind me, close enough that if I misstep in my ripped dress and protesting ankle, he can catch me. Our shoulders brush the walls. The noise from the hall above fades with each turn, replaced by the distant clatter of a kitchen in chaos and the frantic whispers of staff trying to figure out if their wages will cover this mess. 

We pass one of them—a middle-aged woman in plain black, arms full of linens, eyes wide.

She stops dead when she sees us.

For a heartbeat, the three of us are frozen in a little pocket of shared understanding.

She takes in my robes, his collar, the fact that we are on a servants’ stair when we very much do not belong here. The faint smell of ozone still clinging to my baton hand. The smear of blood on his vest that shouldn’t be there if the kill were clean.

Her mouth opens.

“No,” I say, softly but firmly. “You didn’t see us.”

Her gaze flicks to my wrist, to the Tribunal mark there. Something like grim amusement passes through her expression. 

“No,” she agrees. “I didn’t.”

She steps aside, pressing herself tight to the wall to let us pass.

“Thank you,” I say.

She snorts.

“Get out before they decide to add you to the bill,” she mutters.

We do.

The stairs spit us out into a narrow back corridor that smells of damp stone and cooling food. The kitchen door is ajar; inside, I glimpse terrified scullions, a cook swearing in language that would make a floor-warden blush, pots left to smoke on the bone-stove. 

We don’t stop.

Cyran guides us through the warren with the surety of someone who’s explored all the exits long before he needed them. Left at the pantry full of preserved god-spawn wings. Right at the closet of cleaning sigils. Down a short ramp that opens into a servants’ courtyard.

The night hits us again, full and real.

The gardens out back are less impressive than the front ones—the donor-facing side gets the manicured trees and bone-sculptures. Here it’s mostly scrubby plants clinging to the Spine’s edges, a few bedraggled shrine-flowers, the looming shadows of the estate walls. 

“Gate,” Cyran says, pointing.

The servants’ gate is small, set in the stone at the far corner, just big enough for delivery carts. It’s warded, of course, but the sigils carved into its arch are faint, old. Thessen’s priorities are clear: he’s invested in keeping people out of his party, not in keeping his servants from escaping it.

Cyran presses his palm to the central glyph.

It flares weakly, then gutters.

“Push,” he says.

We push.

The gate grinds open with a protesting squeal.

Beyond it, the Ribcage waits—mist rolling slow between the bone-arches, the distant glow of marrow-lamps turning the air a sickly green-red. The street outside is nearly empty at this hour, most of the city either asleep or working in the kind of places that don’t ask why your clothes are torn and your collar is on too tight. 

We step through.

The gate clanks shut behind us.

The noise from Thessen’s estate is suddenly a muffled, faraway thing. The panic, the shouts, the frantic scramble to restore order—it’s all on the other side of a wall now.

We stand in the marrow-haze, breathing.

My ankle throbs. My shoulder aches. My hand still tingles faintly from the Node’s feedback. Cyran is shaking, just enough that, this close, I can feel it in the air between us.

“We’re out,” I say, mostly for myself.

“For now,” he replies.

His voice is hoarse.

I look up at him.

His hair is a mess—crystal dust and static have pulled it out of its careful styling. His eyes catch what little light there is in that inhuman way, reflecting it back pale. The collar at his throat gleams like a shackle.

“You blew up a magic node,” he says slowly, as if tasting the words. “At a donor gala. In front of half the people who sign your pay slips.”

“Yes,” I say.

“You are,” he says, and there’s something like wonder under the raggedness, “utterly, devastatingly foolish.”

“I prefer ‘thorough,’” I answer. “You were going to break.”

He swallows.

For a moment, I think he’s going to make a joke about that, some careless comment about what else breaks him, about softer things.

He doesn’t.

Instead, he says, very quietly, “You keep expecting cruelty. I’m not cruel. I’m careful. Remember?” 

“I remember,” I say.

The street yawns out ahead of us, dark and damp and blessedly empty.

Behind us, somewhere high above, Thessen’s pretty glass world is cracking.

I turn my back on it.

“Come on,” I say. “Before someone thinks to send wardens to the servants’ gate.”

He falls into step beside me.

Partners in crime. Or whatever we’re becoming.

The marrow-mist curls around our ankles as we walk, soft and greasy, carrying the city’s distant creaks and sighs. Above, the ribs of the dead god arch, their shadows like the remains of a cage. 

Tonight, we jumped.
