# Chapter 15 — *The Paint and the Scar*
### Cyran | POV

She watches me unravel. 

I am not graceful when I break.

By the time we reach the safe house—the cramped two-room nest in the Skull district with its crooked door and perpetually damp walls—I am held together by habit and silk thread. Mostly silk. Mostly cosmetic. 

The door bangs against bone as I shove it open with more force than necessary.

“Inside,” I say, or snarl, or something in between. My voice feels like it belongs to somebody else. “Quickly, darling, before your employer decides to chase his favourite toys through the streets.”

Lysa slips past me, limping slightly from the jump, the rip in her robes flashing pale skin at her thigh. She’s flushed from the run, hair coming loose from its severe knots, Tribunal blue smudged with street grime. 

She turns back to the door automatically, reaching for the bolt.

I beat her to it.

My hand slams it home with a sharp, final crack that echoes in the little room like the closing of a tomb. I stand there an instant, palm flat against the rough bone, as if I can brace myself with it, keep my insides from spilling out.

It doesn’t work.

The collar is still on. The cuffs. The gold dust. The smell of Thessen’s perfume and other people’s fear clinging to my skin. 

Get it off.

I pivot away from the door so fast the room tilts. Lysa takes a half-step toward me, then stops, eyes narrowing as she tracks the way I move: too fast, too jagged, the theatrical grace gone and something more feral underneath.

“Cyran,” she says.

I ignore her.

The vest has hooks down the front, clever little gold things that Mistress Vhane pressed together with such care earlier tonight. They do not survive my attention now. My fingers claw at them, scraping, tearing, ripping silk and thread and any pretense of elegance. 

Buttons ping off bone and vanish into shadows. The seam gives with a satisfying, ugly sound. I yank the fabric apart.

The gold dust on my chest catches the lamplight, glittering mockingly.

It’s in the pores. It’s in the blood.

“Get it off,” I mutter, and the words scrape out of my throat raw. “Get it off, get it—”

There’s a basin in the corner of the wash alcove, chipped stone, half full of water from when I tried to make myself respectable before the Gala. I lurch toward it, half-stumbling as my bare feet skid on the cold floor.

The mirror over it is a scrap of polished bone.

I don’t look at it.

I grab the little bar of cheap soap from the edge, nearly drop it, catch it with fingers that don’t feel like mine. My reflection wavers at the edge of my vision—kohl-smudged eyes, smeared blood on my vest, collar glinting. The face Vhane made. The face Thessen sold.

I hate this face.

I crank the tap.

Water coughs and then sluices into the basin in a rusty trickle, cold enough to bite. I plunge both hands in, slosh it up onto my chest, my throat, my face.

It’s not enough.

The gold dust streaks, turning the water in the basin cloudy and bright, whorls of gleam circling the drain. The kohl around my eyes runs, black rivulets down my cheeks, turning the whole mess into something sickly and theatrical.

I scrub.

Soap grinds against skin. My nails dig in, heedless, scratching for some deeper layer where the paint stops and I might start again. I can feel the skin heating under my fingers, the faint sting where I’ve already gone too hard.

Not clean enough.

It never comes off, not really.

You can scrape and scour and wash until you’re red and raw, and still the temple’s hands are on you. Still their eyes. Still the donations.

“Cyran,” Lysa says again, closer now.

The world shrinks to water and bone and my own ragged breathing.

I rake my nails down my chest, leaving angry red tracks through gold. The collar buckles under my fingers and I yank at it, not even at the catch, just anywhere, wanting the sound of metal breaking, the feel of it giving way.

It doesn’t.

Of course it doesn’t.

My hands skid off the links and slam back to my skin instead.

“Off,” I say, louder. My voice cracks. “Off—”

“Cyran.”

Her hands close around my wrists.

It’s not hard.

She doesn’t wrench or twist. She just… holds. Firm and steady, fingers wrapping bone, thumbs pressing lightly over the tendons where they jump beneath my skin. 

“Stop,” she says. “You’re bleeding.”

I freeze.

Because the word is a command phrase I know too well, and because her voice is not the voice I know it from.

I go very still.

My heart keeps trying to claw its way out of my ribs anyway.

I look down.

She’s right. Of course she’s right. There are small crescents where my nails have broken skin along my chest and collarbone, thin pearls of red welling up through smears of gold.

It looks like some obscene painter got bored halfway through the masterpiece.

“It’s still there,” I say.

My hands twitch in her grip, the urge to scrub, to tear, to erase burning in my fingers. If she weren’t holding me, I’d already be back at it. I’d peel myself down to meat if that’s what it took.

“No,” she says, calm and infuriatingly gentle. “It’s not. Not all of it.”

Her thumbs stroke once, soothing, then still.

“Let go,” I say, but there’s no teeth in it.

“No,” she says.

I drag in air.

It doesn’t feel like it goes anywhere useful.

My reflection in the bone-scrap mirror is a stranger: raked-raw skin, kohl smeared, gold dust clinging in patches like spores. Eyes wide and blown and young in a way I have spent decades trying to avoid.

“I need—” I start.

“I know what you need,” she says. “You’ll have skin left when we’re done with it.”

The we shouldn’t help.

It does.

She releases my wrists, slowly, like she’s testing whether I’ll bolt. I don’t. Partially because I’m tired, partially because some mutinous part of me is relieved to have someone else’s hands on the problem for once.

She reaches past me.

Takes the soap from my grip.

Her fingers brush mine briefly; the contact is small, ordinary, and it sends a stupid jolt through me anyway. She dips a cloth—where did she even get a cloth—into the basin, wrings it out deftly, and lifts it.

“May I?” she asks.

Consent, always. Even now, even with me half feral in a washroom, she asks.

I shut my eyes.

“Yes,” I say.

The cloth is warm where it first touches my face. She must have run it under the water long enough to take the bite off.

She starts at my jaw.

Slow, careful strokes, moving up along the line of my cheek. The cheap soap smells faintly of lye and something pretending to be lavender. Underneath it, still, sits the ghost of expensive perfume, powder, other people’s hands.

She wipes the kohl from under my eyes, the smear of it dragging down and then away. The cloth rasps lightly over stubble I didn’t have time to tame earlier. She follows the curve of my cheekbone, the bridge of my nose, the hollow at my temple.

I exhale without meaning to.

Somewhere in the back of my skull, ancient muscle memory panics.

This is a familiar posture: me, standing obediently in front of a mirror; someone else’s hands on my face; cloth, sponge, whatever tool they’ve decided to employ; my own breathing forced shallow so I don’t smudge their work.

Except the temple made things worse when they touched me.

Lysa is undoing.

She doesn’t tilt my head to suit herself. She tips it gently with a knuckle and waits for me to follow the hint. She doesn’t press hard enough to grind anything in. When the cloth comes dangerously close to the collar, she pauses.

“Collar first,” she says quietly. “If you want it off.”

I open my eyes.

Our faces are close—closer than is wise for anyone who knows what I am. The lamp behind her throws a halo of dirty light around her head, picking out loose strands of hair and the faint ink-smudges still on her fingers. 

I can see myself reflected in her spectacles.

I don’t like what I see.

“Yes,” I say again, weaker. “Please.”

She sets the cloth aside.

Her hands move to the back of my neck.

I tense automatically, shoulders jumping under her fingers. She feels it. I know she does. Her thumbs press, just slightly, into the muscles there, a wordless wait communicated through touch.

“It’s me,” she reminds me. Soft. Not soothing, exactly, but steady. “I’m going to unfasten the collar. Nothing else.”

“I know,” I manage.

I don’t.

But I want to.

Her fingers find the clasp. It’s a tiny hook, cunningly hidden, designed so it can be undone quickly by anyone who knows where it sits and not at all by anyone wearing it. I’ve felt variations of this on so many nights, at so many altars.

There’s a momentary resistance as metal protests movement, then a sharp little click.

The weight at my throat lifts.

The sensation is absurdly disproportionate to the physical object. The collar is light. The absence of it is a chasm.

I sag.

Just a fraction, but enough that my shoulder brushes hers on the way down. She catches the collar before it can clatter into the basin, sets it aside on the counter with more care than it deserves.

The cuffs go next.

Those are easier; I’ve taken too many of them off myself over the years. She still does it, though, fingers on the catches, splitting the circle of metal from around my wrists. The faint indentation they leave in my skin feels like it goes all the way to bone.

“Better?” she asks.

I swallow.

“It’s a start,” I say.

She picks up the cloth again.

This time, when she presses it to my chest, over one of the raw, reddened tracks I left with my own nails, I don’t flinch.

The water is cooler now. The gold dust at my throat has stuck in a film, reluctant to leave. She scrubs—not hard, not like I had been doing, but with those slow, inexorable circles that polish rather than scour.

The water in the basin goes murky.

Gold and black and a faint thread of pink where I’ve bled swirl together, a little galaxy of everything I was tonight spinning down the drain.

“I hate this face,” I whisper.

The words slip out before I can stop them.

Her hand slows.

“It’s a good face,” she says.

I laugh.

It comes out more like a gasp.

“No,” I say. “It’s a currency.”

Her eyes flick up to meet mine.

There’s no pity there.

I don’t know what to do with that.

“They call me a Relic,” I say, because if I don’t talk, I’ll start clawing at myself again. “Like I’m a holy weapon. The Blood Relic. The Vigil’s blade. Isn’t it all very dramatic?”

“It is,” she agrees, quiet.

“I wasn’t,” I say.

The words feel rusted.

I haven’t said them. Not like this. Not to anyone who wasn’t already part of the temple and their ongoing little farce.

“I wasn’t a weapon. I was an ornament.”

Her hand stills entirely.

“In the Temple,” I clarify, when she doesn’t immediately respond. “I sat on a pedestal and let them look. And touch. And decide how much a minute of my time was worth on the donation ledger.”

Silence stretches.

It’s not empty.

The cloth in her hand drips, a slow patter of soapy water into the basin. My own breathing is too loud in my ears. Somewhere down in the street, a cart rattles over bone, a dog barks once, the city goes on endlessly indifferent.

I keep my gaze fixed on a crack in the wall above her head.

“The fighting,” I say. “The patrols. The god-spawn. That came later. After a priest miscalculated and something with too many teeth made a snack of one of the donors in the middle of a rite. It was… bad for public relations.”

My mouth twists.

“They decided perhaps their favourite ornament should learn some teeth of his own,” I continue. “Self-defense, they called it. Protection. For the donors, of course, not for me.”

Her shoulders are very still.

“I learned,” I say. “I learned quickly. I learned to be very good at making monsters die in ways that looked impressive from the pews.”

I don’t say: I learned to enjoy it, sometimes. That one’s mine to wrestle.

“I learned,” I finish, “because I thought if I was sharp enough, vicious enough, useful enough, maybe they’d stop touching.”

The confession sits there between us, raw and ugly and strange.

If this were a temple rite, this is the moment someone would laugh.

Or tut.

Or tell me how lucky I was to have such a righteous, holy purpose.

“How noble,” they’d say. “How utterly foolish, to think it changes anything.”

I wait for her version.

Judgment. Disgust. That slight, familiar tightening around the eyes people get when they realize their beautiful thing is secondhand.

The cloth drops.

It hits the basin with a wet slap.

Her hands come up, empty.

For a half-second, my body seizes—the position too close to other nights, other hands, other intentions.

Then her palms cup my face.

It’s not like the temple.

She doesn’t arrange me.

She just… holds.

Her fingers are warm along my jaw, thumbs resting lightly below my cheekbones. The contact is firm without being forceful, her touch saying stay here, with me, not stay still, for them.

“They didn’t break you,” she says.

Her voice is steady, low.

“They just sharpened you.”

I make a sound.

I don’t know what kind.

My throat burns.

“I’m telling you I was a whore for the gods,” I say, because if I pierce it with enough acid, maybe it won’t hurt. “And you make it sound like I’m a particularly fine kitchen knife.”

“You were a person,” she says. “They made you a thing. That’s not your fault.”

The simple, brutal accuracy of it knocks the breath out of me more than any jump from any balcony.

I look at her.

Really look.

Her hair is a mess. There’s a bruise already blooming along her jaw from where someone caught her in the dark back at the Gala. Ink stains her fingers. Her spectacles sit slightly crooked on her nose.

She is the most terrifyingly beautiful thing I have ever seen.

“Lysa,” I say, and my voice doesn’t sound like mine. It sounds like that boy on the pedestal, wondering which part of himself he’s going to have to sell next.

She leans in.

For one foolish second, every muscle in my body prepares for her mouth on mine, for the inevitable transactional kiss, the exchange of want and duty, the only language I have ever spoken fluently.

She kisses my forehead.

Soft.

Lingering.

Her lips are warm and dry and chaste, placed dead center above the bridge of my nose like some reversed benediction.

I go motionless.

All the little acts I know how to perform—arched back, parted lips, wandering hands—evaporate. There is nowhere to put them. Nothing they can purchase here.

No one has ever kissed me there without wanting something.

No one has ever kissed me there and meant nothing but I see you and you are here.

Something in my chest gives.

It’s not a clean break.

More like a slow, unwilling crack spreading through hardened resin, letting air into places it hasn’t touched in years.

My eyes sting.

Of course they do. I scrubbed at them with soap and cloth and my own damned fingers. It has nothing to do with the way that simple contact untangles a knot in me I didn’t know was still tight.

One treacherous tear escapes anyway.

It tracks down my temple, cutting a clean line through the remaining smear of gold dust on my cheek.

She doesn’t wipe it away.

Of course she doesn’t.

She just stays there another heartbeat, her forehead almost touching mine now, breath mingling with mine. Then she lets go.

The sudden absence of her hands feels like a loss, but it’s not abandonment. It’s space.

I exhale.

“Thank you,” I breathe.

It feels inadequate.

It’s also the truest thing I’ve said all night.

My legs decide, belatedly, that they have had enough of this vertical nonsense. They fold.

I slide down the wall, back against bone, leaving a faint smear of damp where my shoulders drag. The basin gurgles softly as the last of the contaminated water sinks away, leaving a ring of dull gold around the edge.

Lysa hesitates a second, then sits too.

Not at a polite distance. Not as if she’s preserving some illusion of professional detachment.

She folds herself down beside me, skirts tangling inelegantly, one knee bumping mine. Her shoulder brushes my upper arm. She doesn’t apologize. She doesn’t shift away.

We sit.

The lamp burns low, wick guttering in the draft from the warped window. The room smells of cheap soap, residual perfume, and something warmer underneath that might be us.

My face is bare now.

No kohl. No dust.

No armor.

“Your file,” she says, after a while. Her voice is soft enough that the words barely disturb the air. “It says Blood Relic. Temple-forged weapon. Exemplary service record. It doesn’t mention pedestals.”

“Files rarely do,” I say.

She huffs once.

“I will write what happened,” she murmurs. “All of it. Not the marketing.”

“Dangerous habit,” I say. “Telling the truth.”

“I know,” she says.

Silence settles again.

Not comfortable, not yet. But less flayed.

“You can’t un-know this now,” I say eventually. “Any of it. I can’t pretend to be just your charming little monster with a few inconveniently sharp edges.”

She turns her head, meeting my gaze squarely.

“You were never just that,” she says. “You’re not what they made you, Cyran. That’s the point.”

I want to believe her.

I don’t know how.

But for the first time in a very long time, sitting here on a cracked bone floor with my skin red from scrubbing and gold dust drying in uneven streaks around the basin, I consider the possibility that I might someday learn.

I let my head tip sideways until it rests lightly against hers.

She doesn’t pull away.

We stay like that.

In the dim, in the damp, in the ruin of Thessen’s finery and the aftermath of my own performance. Clean, and not clean. Broken, and not broken.

Real.
