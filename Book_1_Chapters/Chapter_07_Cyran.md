# Chapter 7 — *The Alcove in the Ribs*
### Cyran | POV

The storm sounds like a thousand teeth chattering at once.

Which is impressive, considering the corpse we live in doesn’t technically have those anymore.

We’re halfway along one of the upper bone-tunnels when it hits. The air has been wrong for the last ten minutes—too dry, the marrow-mist thinning into a fine, prickling dust that gets up under my collar and into my nose. The lamps along the tunnel walls flicker as if something is passing over them in waves.

“Storm,” I say, before the first real shudder goes through the tunnel.

Lysa glances up from her notes. Only she would be writing mid-patrol. The light from her little hand-lamp halos her spectacles, throwing a faint reflection of script across the glass.

“Storm?” she echoes. “There’s nothing scheduled until—”

The bone under our feet groans.

Not the usual background creak of an ancient god settling. This is sharp, immediate, like a rib being snapped between two careless fingers.

“Down,” I snap.

To her credit, she doesn’t argue. The tunnel bucks; dust sifts from the ceiling in a sudden, choking curtain. I grab her on instinct—fingers locking around her upper arm where her robe is thinner—and haul.

The nearest shrine-alcove is three strides away. Old, long-disused, its god-face eroded into something more like a skull than a saint. We hit the lip of it just as the tunnel ceiling decides it’s had enough of pretending to be load-bearing.

Bone comes down behind us in a roar.

We slam into the back wall of the alcove together. Her shoulder crunches into my chest, my spine into the cold stone. For a heartbeat, space disappears; there is only dust and weight and the animal certainty that this is finally the collapse that kills me.

Then the rumble eases from catastrophic to merely dreadful. Chunks of rib and spurs of cartilage crash down across the tunnel mouth, a stuttering cascade that goes on far too long. Fine bone-dust billows in after it, turning the world into an opaque, acrid cloud.

Lysa coughs, body jerking against mine. The lamp in her hand dies, smothered. Somewhere outside, the storm wakes fully—howling down the length of the tunnel, driving more dust before it until the noise is all teeth and wind and distant, cracking impacts.

I keep my arm locked around her, more to anchor myself than her. Sharp little bones, these Tribunal types. She fits against me oddly well, which is not a thought I should be having while we might be about to suffocate.

Eventually, the thunder of falling debris slows. My ears ring in the relative quiet.

She pulls in a breath, coughs again, then wriggles. “I need—” Another cough. “Room.”

Right. Autonomy. People enjoy that.

I loosen my grip, hands reluctant in ways I pretend not to notice. She steps back just enough that we are two distinct bodies again instead of one startled, dust-coated shape.

The alcove is small. Old shrine bench along the back wall, shallow ledge carved overhead where offerings used to sit. The god’s relief on the rear wall is worn down to the barest suggestion of eyes and a mouth. Someone left dried flowers here, centuries ago by the look of them; their ghosts crumble under my heel when I shift my stance.

Outside the mouth of the alcove, there is no tunnel anymore. Just a jagged wall of tumbled bone-blocks and packed dust, wedged tight from floor to ceiling. Every now and then a spray of finer particles skitters down it, hissing in the wind.

I reach out, touch the nearest piece. It’s solid. Heavy. No give.

“Well,” I say, brushing grey powder off the shoulder of her robe with a little flourish I don’t quite feel. “Cozy.”

She looks past me at the blockage, jaw tight. There’s dust in her hair, pale against the dark braid; a smear of it across one lens of her spectacles.

“Is there another way out?” she asks.

“Not without burrowing like a particularly determined rat,” I say. “And even I don’t relish chewing marrow-dust for the rest of the night.”

She digs into the fold of her robe and pulls out a small, disc-shaped charm—a com-link. Its sigils are etched with that fussy Tribunal precision. She twists the edge. A faint greenish glow licks around the rim.

“Spine dispatch, this is Investigator Vaskin,” she says, voice clipped. “We have a collapse in upper Ribcage tunnel seven. Requesting warden response and structural assessment.”

The disc hisses back at her. Not words. Static, thick and grinding, like more bone being ground to powder.

She tries again. “Spine, confirm receipt.”

More static. The storm outside howls louder, like it’s laughing at her.

“Storm interference,” I say. “The dust eats sound-sigils when it gets bad enough. Charming, I know.”

Her mouth flattens. She tucks the useless charm away with a motion that is a fraction more forceful than necessary.

“So we’re trapped,” she says.

“Temporarily detained,” I correct. “Don’t look so grim. I’ve had far worse company in much smaller spaces.”

“That is not comforting,” she says.

“It wasn’t meant to be,” I murmur.

The temperature drops in increments.

Even inside the alcove, out of the direct lash of the storm, the air is changing—heat leached out by whatever petty little miracle of corpse-weather produces bone-storms. My breath doesn’t fog; I don’t breathe that way. But hers does. Little puffs in the thin light that seeps in around the edges of the blockage and from whatever residual glow my Marks give off under my skin.

She rubs her hands along her arms, friction warming through robe and under-layer. Practical boots, practical belt, practical everything. No cloak. Of course not. Auditors prefer thin armour and thicker walls.

I, on the other hand, am draped in one of the few luxuries the Vigil still sees fit to throw at me—a heavy cloak, dark wool lined in something softer, clasped at my throat with a bone brooch carved in the shape of the Vigil sigil.

I undo the clasp.

“Here,” I say, shrugging it off. Dust puffs up as the weight of it settles over my arms. “Take it.”

Her eyes flick from the cloak to my face, searching for the joke.

“You’ll freeze,” she says.

“Sweetheart,” I say, with more patience than I feel, “I’m dead. I run cold. That,” I add, lying with the ease of long practice, “has its advantages.”

The truth is that bone-storms go straight through me. They set off the old temple carving-sites under my skin, make the Marks ache like someone’s pressing ice into each little line. There is no reason she needs to know that.

She hesitates anyway, staring at the cloak like it might sprout teeth.

“Take it,” I repeat, softer. “You’re no use to either of us if you turn into a Tribunal-shaped icicle.”

A breath. Then, with a sharp little nod that says she has framed this as a matter of duty in her head, she accepts.

The cloak nearly swallows her. I have to bite back a comment about it; the sight of her cinching it close around herself does something stupid in my chest. She sits on the stone bench in the back of the alcove, pulling her feet up to keep them off the cold floor, lamp set dark beside her hip.

I stay on my feet, entirely because it looks better. Guard at the entrance, back to the blockage, eyes on the storm.

The storm howls. Shards of something harder than regular dust ping off the outer bone with a sound like thrown gravel. Every once in a while the whole wall of debris shivers, sending another fine cascade of powder inward. It settles on my hair, my shoulders, on the talons of the goddess carved behind us.

Time stretches.

The ache in my arm—the one the scavenger-beast tore into last night—wakes up as the cold deepens. The Marks had done their work; the skin is mostly knit, but the underlying bruise protests, a low, steady throb. My side twinges in sympathy where the claws raked there too. Lysa’s bandages are tight under my leathers, a dull reminder with every breath.

“Something wrong?” she asks quietly, from the bench.

It’s always a little disconcerting how quickly she notices.

“Only the usual,” I say. “Decades of poor life choices, several thousand Merit Marks’ worth of scar tissue, a divine corpse having a tantrum around our ears.”

Her gaze tracks down my posture, catches on the slight flex of my hand over my ribs.

“The wound?” she prompts.

“Your handiwork is impeccable, Investigator,” I say. “It only hurts when I breathe.”

She snorts, of all things. A soft exhale with the faintest suggestion of derision. Progress. I like it when she remembers I’m supposed to be ridiculous.

The cold continues to seep in. Stone does not care about our smaller, mortal discomforts. My fingers begin to tingle. I roll my shoulders against the stiffness settling there.

Minutes pass. Or hours. Hard to tell without a sky.

“Sit down,” Lysa says suddenly.

I blink. “Pardon?”

“You’re shivering,” she says. “Or is that another theatrical flourish I’m supposed to ignore?”

I glance down at my hands. They are, in fact, trembling slightly. Betrayal.

“It’s part of the ambience,” I say. “Adds to the whole tragic Relic aesthetic.”

“Sit,” she repeats. The word is a command, not a request. Her voice has gone very calm and very precise. I’ve heard that tone before; it usually means someone else is about to regret their life choices.

I look at the bench. At the narrow span of stone, already mostly occupied by Tribunal robes and my own cloak. There is room. Barely. It will put us hip to hip, shoulder to shoulder, pressed thigh to thigh whether we mean to be or not.

Which is, of course, the point.

“Trying to lure me into compromising positions, Investigator?” I murmur. “You really have embraced this audit whole-heartedly.”

“If I were trying to compromise you,” she says, “you’d know. Sit down. Share the cloak. Don’t be a martyr.”

The last word pricks more sharply than I expect.

Martyrs are honoured. Remembered. I am neither.

But I’m also cold. Bone-deep, Mark-deep cold that worms under armour and between ribs and makes the old scars sing.

I give up first.

With a little theatrical sigh, because I have a reputation to maintain, I cross the narrow space and lower myself onto the bench beside her.

Thigh to thigh is, if anything, an understatement.

The cloak smells faintly of my own cologne—fading incense and something resinous—as she lifts the edge and drapes it over my lap as well. The wool is rough against my palm when I help her pull it higher, tucking it around us like a makeshift cave.

Underneath the heavy fabric, the chill recedes a fraction. Not gone. Just pushed to the outside of the moment, where the storm can gnash its teeth and dream of us without reaching skin.

Silence unspools between us. Longer this time. Our breaths find the same slow rhythm without consultation.

Her shoulder is a solid weight against mine. Her hip presses along the length of my thigh. The heat of her bleeds through layers of cloth in a way that feels… indecent. Not for what it is, but for how much I want to lean into it.

I don’t. I sit very still. Movements small and precise, the way I learned in the temple when they painted sigils onto my skin and told me not to flinch.

“Why the jokes?” she asks at last, so soft I almost miss it under the storm.

I turn my head. In the faint light, her profile is all sharp lines softened by exhaustion: the slope of her nose, the point of her chin, the little tension at the corner of her mouth.

“Darling, if I stop, the city might collapse,” I say lightly. “Think of the civic duty.”

“Even when you’re bleeding,” she continues, as if I haven’t spoken. “When that thing hit you in the market. Your first instinct was to make a joke.”

“Flirtation is a perfectly respectable response to near-death,” I say. “Everyone knows that.”

“Everyone who taught you, perhaps.” Her gaze cuts to me, steady, unamused. “It’s… pathological.”

Ah. Diagnosed. Lovely.

I tip my head back against the cold stone, stare up into the darkness where the god’s eroded face should be. Dust motes drift like slow, lazy stars in the thin slice of light from the blocked tunnel.

“If I stop laughing,” I say, before I can talk myself out of it, “I might remember I’m screaming.”

The words come out flatter than I intend. No flourish. No pretty edge. Just truth dropped between us like a stone into still water.

She is quiet for a long moment.

My muscles tense, anticipating mockery, pity, some careful ethical observation about trauma responses.

“That’s… honest,” she says instead.

I huff a little breath that might be a laugh if you were generous.

“I have my moments,” I say.

Her shoulder shifts minutely against mine as she adjusts how she’s sitting. The movement brushes the side of her breast against my upper arm through layers of cloak and robe. It’s nothing. It’s everything.

I think of the temple revels, of hands that never asked, of faces that blurred together into one long, endless performance. I think of Thessens donors, whoever they end up being, and the way I will be paraded in front of them like a wine bottle with no cork.

Then I think of Lysa’s voice, cool and precise, saying, Your body isn’t payment I’ll accept.

“Why the audit?” I ask, before I can make that memory into something it isn’t. “You hate this work.”

She goes very still.

“I don’t hate the work,” she says eventually. “I hate the mistakes.”

“Such as?” I prompt, because if I keep the attention on her, perhaps I won’t have to think about the fact that my heart—whatever remains of it—has started a slow, steady ache that has nothing to do with Merit Marks.

She breathes out through her nose, fogging the faint light.

“I authorized a Redemption once,” she says. “Early in my years. The file was clean. The metrics fit. On paper, it was correct.”

“And in reality?” I ask, softly.

“In reality,” she says, “they had miscounted. Or lied. Or both. He hadn’t met threshold. They took his Mark anyway.”

“Ah,” I say.

She doesn’t look at me. Her hands have folded together in her lap over the cloak, fingers interlaced so tightly the knuckles pale.

“He’d been difficult,” she continues. “Sharp with wardens. Angry. We used words like non-compliant and volatile to make it sound inevitable.” A humourless half-smile ghosts across her mouth. “He screamed when they did it. It echoed up through the whole hall. I wrote Subject distress: High.”

“And then?” I ask.

“And then I went home and told myself that one error in a system this size was tragic but acceptable,” she says. “That the law still worked, in aggregate. That it was still worth serving.”

The storm outside surges, wind keening along the blocked tunnel. Bone-shards rattle against bone.

“And now you’re here,” I say, “auditing another volatile, non-compliant asset with a pretty file and ugly gaps.”

“Now I’m here,” she echoes. “Trying not to make the same mistake twice.”

It is a terrible thing, to see yourself through someone else’s fear. It is also, perversely, a relief.

“I feel safer when you’re the one judging me,” I say.

The line surprises even me, in its… softness.

It hangs in the air, warm and fragile and enormously stupid.

Her head turns slowly toward me. In the shadow, her eyes are very dark.

“You shouldn’t,” she says. But it sounds more like can’t than won’t.

“Too late,” I say lightly, because the alternative is to admit how right it feels. “Don’t let it go to your head. I have very limited safe people to choose from.”

Something eases minutely in her face. Not a smile. The ghost of an unwinding.

We lapse back into silence.

The storm grinds on. Time blurs. My eyelids grow heavier despite myself; vampiric or not, I keep the same rhythms I’m ordered to keep. It’s the deep hours now, when even the sinners who never sleep grow quiet.

Beside me, Lysa’s breathing lengthens, slows. Her head begins to tilt in that traitorous way that means sleep is stalking her.

I hold very, very still.

At some point, despite her obvious best efforts, gravity wins. Her head tips sideways until it meets the slope of my shoulder.

Every muscle in my body seizes.

This is not temple touch. There is no command in it. No demanded performance. She has simply… fallen.

I do not breathe. Which is convenient, because I forget how.

Her hair brushes my throat, a few stray strands escaping the neat braid. It smells faintly of sage and ink and whatever cheap soap the Tribunal issues its scribes. There is a streak of dust on her cheek that the cloak has not quite wiped away.

I want, with a sudden, vicious intensity that scares me more than the storm, to turn my face into her hair and breathe her in properly. To rest my cheek against the top of her head. To close my eyes and pretend, for the span of a single heartbeat, that this is a choice we both made instead of a weather-induced accident.

I do none of those things.

I sit as if carved from the same stone as the alcove, every line of me held rigid so as not to disturb the poor, foolish Tribunal woman who has thought it safe to sleep against a monster.

Her head grows heavier against my shoulder as she slides a little bit more into the warmth between us. A soft, unconscious sound escapes her on an exhale—something between a sigh and a hum.

My throat tightens.

She jerks awake a moment later, of course. Because the universe is cruel and never lets good things linger.

Her head snaps up. The sudden loss of contact is a physical thing, like someone has pulled a blanket away.

“Sorry,” she says, voice rasped with sleep, straightening her spectacles with an automatic gesture. “I didn’t mean to—”

“Sleep, Auditor,” I cut in, more brusque than I intend. If I let her finish that sentence, I might say something unsalvageable. “I’ll watch the rocks.”

“That’s not…” She trails off, studying my face. Whatever she’s looking for there, she either finds or decides it’s none of her business. Her shoulders drop a fraction. “You need rest too.”

“Need is a strong word,” I say. “Want, perhaps. But I’m used to not getting that.”

Her mouth tightens. “You’re healing.”

“I’ll be fine by dawn,” I say. “Go on. Consider it part of my community service.”

She gives me a look that suggests she knows there’s more under that sentence than I’m willing to unpack in this alcove. Then, cautiously, she lets herself lean back against the wall again. Her head tips to the side once more—but this time she catches herself before it finds my shoulder, angling away so there’s a handbreadth of space between us.

It shouldn’t sting. It does.

She sleeps.

Eventually. Her breaths even out, her muscles slacken, the little frown between her brows smoothing with each exhale. Up close, in the thin not-light of the blocked tunnel, she looks younger. Or perhaps it’s just that the armor of professionalism has finally gone off-duty.

I watch her.

Not because I am a romantic fool—which I am absolutely not—but because the alternative is staring at the rock wall and imagining every small shudder is the next collapse that will finally bury me properly.

Under the cloak, her hand rests near her knee. Ink stains the side of her smallest finger. The Tribunal mark at her wrist peeks out where the robe has ridden up, circle of ink dark against pale skin.

Without thinking, I mirror the position with my own hand, fingers curling against the wool a hairsbreadth from hers. Not touching. Close enough that if either of us shifted in our sleep—not that I will be—our skin might brush.

Outside, the bone-storm rages itself hoarse. Gradually, the ping of shards against bone grows less frequent. The wind drops from a scream to a low, tired moan.

Dawn is still a long way off. The tunnel will need clearing. Wardens will dig us out eventually, or I will dig us out myself when the dust has settled enough not to flay us from the inside.

For now, there is this: an alcove carved into ribs, a cloak that smells faintly of me and now of her, a woman asleep at my side who has told me no more honestly than anyone ever has and still chosen to share warmth with the monster she’s auditing.

I sit very still.

I do not kiss her hair.

I watch the rocks. And, when it gets too quiet, her face.
