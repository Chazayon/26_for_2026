# Chapter 23 — *The Scribe at the Gate*
### Lysa | POV

I am not built for war, but I am built for stubbornness, which is remarkably similar.

The Heart Temple looms ahead of me like a ribcage waiting to snap shut.

Sunset bleeds along the god’s bones, turning the white spires gold and pink, all very beautiful if you don’t know what they plan to kill inside. The streets of the Temple District are already jammed—donors in their finest bone‑silks, pilgrims in home‑mended robes, hawkers selling blessed trinkets and stale bread. The air buzzes with anticipation and incense. Drums beat somewhere deeper in, slow and steady.

The Great Pulse is close.

I don’t have time to shove reverence out of the way gently.

I shoulder through the crowd, using elbows, the set of my jaw, and the fact that I no longer care if anyone recognizes me. Faces blur. Snatches of conversation snag and tear as I pass.

“—Prime Contract delegation—”

“—Relic, have you heard—”

“—they say there’ll be a sign tonight—”

Yes.

There will.

If I get inside.

The main gate yawns open ahead, a grand arch of heart‑bone big enough to march an army through. It’s flanked by Vigil Wardens and Tribunal Guards in their best uniforms, spears upright, faces carved into impassivity. Beyond them, I catch a glimpse of the nave: candles, banners, a sea of people pressed together under the vaulted ribs.

There is no way they let me walk through that door.

Not in this stolen, too‑big coat, not with my hair falling out of its knot, not with prison dust still ground into my skin.

I stop in the shelter of a shrine alcove, press my back to cold bone, and close my eyes for one heartbeat.

Mental maps unfurl behind my eyelids.

Years of audits, walking every sanctioned corridor and a few that weren’t, reading grainy contractors’ diagrams by candlelight until the ink bled into my dreams. Vein ducts. Drainage channels. Maintenance hatches the donors pretend don’t exist so they can imagine their temple floats untouched above the city’s filth.

South Sector.

There was a notation in one report: ACCESS: DRAINAGE. Marked in a bored clerk’s careful hand.

I turn left, away from the main gate.

The side street is narrower, wedged between two lower shrines. Laundry lines sag above, half‑blocking the sky, catching the last of the light. The crowd thins to service workers heading home and the unlucky few trying to slip into the ceremony late.

I move faster.

By the time I reach the south wall of the Temple, my lungs burn. The coat sticks to my back with sweat. The drums have picked up a fraction—faster, more insistent. My ribs echo them.

The maintenance alley is almost empty.

Just stacks of broken stone, a few discarded incense braziers, and ahead, exactly what I hoped and dreaded: a bone grate set into the wall at ankle height, wide enough for a person to squeeze through if they don’t mind losing some skin.

Two Guards stand in front of it.

Not Vigil.

Temple Guard tonight—brighter armor, less sense.

The nearer one straightens as I approach, hand dropping to the baton at his hip.

“Restricted area, citizen,” he says. “Main ceremony is through the front—”

The other Guard squints at my face.

I see the moment recognition hits.

“That’s her,” he blurts. “The Auditor. The fugitive—”

Well.

So much for bluffing.

I don’t think.

There’s no time for a careful ethical calculus.

There is only the grate behind them, the drums above us, and Cyran’s voice in my head saying Don’t follow, which was, frankly, an invitation.

My hand goes to my belt.

I scoop up a handful of grit from the alley floor as I move.

The first Guard is still turning his head toward his companion when I fling the grit up and into their faces.

It’s not clever.

It’s not precise.

It works.

Both men flinch, hands coming up to shield eyes already watering.

I’m on the nearer one before he can curse.

My baton telescopes out with a sharp, familiar click.

I jam the tip into the seam between his breastplate and shoulder and thumb the trigger.

The stun‑charge snaps, a blue‑white crackle, the smell of singed air.

He spasms.

His eyes roll back.

He crumples sideways, armor ringing when it hits bone.

“Sorry,” I tell him, breath already ragged.

The second Guard recovers faster.

He’s already drawing his sword, blinking grit and humiliation out of his eyes.

“Drop it,” I say.

He doesn’t.

Of course he doesn’t.

He swings.

The blade whistles far too close to my arm; I twist instinctively. Fabric tears. A line of cold fire blooms along my upper arm where metal kisses skin.

The pain is clear, bright, shocking.

I am not built for war.

My training is quills and statutes and long hours hunched over transcripts, not this.

But I am built for stubbornness.

I duck under his next swing on pure fear and habit. The world narrows to the smell of his sweat, the rasp of his breath, the gleam of the sword’s edge.

I don’t have room for a proper strike.

Fine.

Improvisation, then.

My free hand dives inside my coat, fingers closing around the smooth, cool glass tucked against my ribs.

Second vial of acid.

I slam it against the center of his chestplate.

Glass shatters.

For a heartbeat, nothing happens.

Then the smell hits—sharp, metallic, wrong.

The liquid hisses as it eats through polish and metal alike.

A bloom of pale fumes curls up between us.

The Guard screams.

He drops the sword in his panic, clawing at his armor, trying to strip it off before the acid reaches skin.

“Off, off—”

“Hold still,” I start, and then realize I can’t help him without dooming someone else.

Merely choosing intends a cruelty.

I grit my teeth, step in, and drive my boot sideways into the back of his knee with everything I have.

Something in his leg gives with a sickening crack.

His scream pitches higher, then cuts off as he topples, cracking his head against the stone on the way down.

Silence slams into the alley in the wake of all that noise.

My ears ring.

My arm burns.

Acid still sizzles faintly on ruined armor, but it hasn’t eaten through to anything gore‑colored yet. I force myself to look long enough to be sure.

He’s breathing.

So is the other one.

They’ll hurt tomorrow.

They’ll live to file complaints.

“Sorry,” I whisper again, to both of them now, because I don’t know their names.

My hands shake as I turn to the grate.

Up close, it’s bigger than it looked from the street. Two hands high, three wide, bars thick enough that I won’t be bending them with willpower and adrenaline.

I glance at my belt.

One vial of acid left.

Save it for a lock, Lysa. Don’t waste it on a glorified window.

I drop to my knees, grit biting stone through stolen boots, and run my fingers along the grate’s frame. The bolts are old, rusted into the bone. The seam where metal meets god‑bone is flaking, mortar crumbling.

Good.

I wedge my baton through the bars, lever hard.

Muscles protest.

The wound on my arm screams.

The metal groans like a living thing.

“Move,” I hiss at it.

One bolt pops.

Another.

The grate tilts, leaving a gap just wide enough for a very stupid, very determined woman to slide through.

I yank it the rest of the way aside, lay flat on the cold stone, and shove myself headfirst into the dark.

The smell meets me halfway.

Sewage, old and new.

Rot and runoff and every bad decision the Temple has ever flushed down its sanctified throats. It’s a wet slap in the face, thicker than the prison mildew, cutting through the incense like a knife.

I gag.

Keep moving.

The duct is a tight, low tunnel, circular, its interior slimed with god knows what. My coat drags along the bottom, soaking up moisture I don’t want to identify. My wounded arm twinges every time I plant that hand to crawl.

Behind me, the drums continue, muffled now.

Ahead, under the drip and squelch, I hear something else: voices, stretched thin by stone. Chanting. A congregation’s murmur, rising and falling in practiced waves.

You’re late.

The thought is a whip.

I pick up the pace, knees jarring against the stone, palms slipping in the muck.

By the time I reach a junction—one narrow branch veering off, the main duct sloping slightly upward—my lungs burn and my shoulders ache from crawling.

I pause just long enough to wipe sweat from my eyes with the back of my hand.

The smear of filth it leaves on my skin is… motivating.

“This is disgusting,” I mutter. “You’d hate it.”

Saying it aloud makes it real: what I know about him, what I carry like fact.

Cyran likes baths too hot, soap that smells like sage, sheets that don’t itch. He pretends he’s indifferent, but he notices every texture, every scent. He would make some cutting, theatrical observation about this tunnel, then hold his sleeve over his nose and power through it anyway if he thought it would save someone.

Well.

We make an excellent pair of fools.

I follow the slope upward.

The air thins from sludge‑heavy to merely foul. The chanting grows clearer, words resolving into old prayers I’ve read on parchment a thousand times, now smeared together by too many mouths into a single, insistent noise.

At last, blessedly, my hand hits something different.

A rung.

Metal, slick with condensation, set into the wall.

I tilt my head back.

Above, shaft: narrow, dark, the faintest suggestion of light at the very top.

I suck in two deep breaths, wipe my palms as clean as I can on my coat, and start to climb.

Rung by rung.

My arms tremble from the prison, from the fight, from the tunnel, but fear is an excellent motivator. So is fury. So is the thought of walking into the nave and seeing him on that altar with no one between him and the knife.

Halfway up, the drums change tempo again.

Faster.

The Pulse is almost here.

My heart tries to match it, stuttering in my chest.

“No,” I tell it. “You beat for me. He gets enough hearts already.”

The shaft feels longer than it has any right to be.

By the time my hand hits the trapdoor at the top, my shoulders are a continuous throb and my injured arm is on fire.

I brace my back against one wall, both feet against the opposite, and shove up with my good hand.

The hatch doesn’t move.

Of course it doesn’t.

Why would anything be easy?

I set my jaw.

Reach for my last vial of acid.

It takes three awkward, fumbling tries to get the wax seal off one‑handed in this cramped space. When it finally cracks, I whisper a thank you to every chemistry‑obsessed sociopath and petty god who ever lived.

I pour a thin line of the liquid along the visible seam of the hatch.

It hisses and smokes, the fumes making my eyes water all over again. The metal of the latch, unseen on top, protests in faint, ugly squeals.

I count to ten.

To twenty.

To thirty, because if I rush and get this stuff on my face, I’ll be blind and he’ll win twice.

Then I brace and shove again.

This time, something gives with a wet, tearing sound.

The hatch pops up an inch.

Light knifes down.

Voices flood in, no longer distant.

“—rejoice, for the Heart remembers—”

“—miracle of the Pulse—”

A roar of applause.

My pulse spikes.

I shove the hatch the rest of the way open and haul myself up through the gap.

The first breath of Temple air is almost worse than the sewer.

Incense, thick and cloying, layered over too many bodies and too much expectation.

I blink against the sudden brightness.

I’m crouched behind a massive statue—some long‑dead saint of contracts, all flowing robes and uplifted hands, carved from the same pale bone as the rest of the nave. The plinth hides me from the bulk of the congregation.

Beyond it—

The Heart Temple opens like a wound.

Ribs arch overhead, carved and polished, lit by thousands of candles that throw trembling light up along their curves. Banners ripple between them in red and white. The nave is a sea of people packed shoulder‑to‑shoulder, donors in the front rows with their reserved benches, the faithful crammed wherever there’s standing room.

All eyes are on the altar.

The Heart itself is hidden, buried deeper in the god’s chest, but the place where its pulse echoes loudest is right there: an elevated platform of heart‑stone, shot through with faint red veins that already shimmer, ready. The clergy stand in a wide semicircle, white and red robes fluttering, heads bowed.

At the center of it all walks a figure in white.

Even from behind, he is unmistakable.

Too precise.

Too composed.

Cyran moves like a man walking a blade’s edge he knows will cut him either way.

The white robe clings to his shoulders, flaring just so with every step. His hair has been combed back ruthlessly, exposing the clean line of his throat. In his hand, held low and hidden from most of the crowd by the angle of his body, something glints black as void.

The obsidian dagger.

My stomach drops.

The sound in the nave blurs for a moment, smearing into a dull roar under the drumbeat and the faint, terrible knowledge that his legs are moving exactly as someone else told them to.

I can’t hear his thoughts from here.

But I know him.

I know that somewhere behind that beautiful, obedient mask, he is charting every angle, every weakness, every possible way this could go wrong for the man holding the strings.

I press my back to the cool stone of the statue, drag in a thin, shallow breath, and force myself to catalog my own surroundings.

I am inside.

That’s the victory.

Battered, filthy, bleeding into someone else’s coat, but inside.

To my left, a side aisle: lined with more statues, leading toward a small service door half‑hidden behind a pillar. To my right, a gap in the benches where latecomers stand pressed against the wall, eyes fixed on the altar, mouths moving in prayer.

No one is looking back here.

Why would they?

They think the story is all in front of them.

I curl my fingers around the baton at my side.

Its charge indicator glows faintly amber.

Low, but not dead.

Good enough.

My arm throbs in time with the drums.

The Heart Temple looms over me, a ribcage of stone and light and hypocrisy.

I am one exhausted, under‑armed, legally disgraced scribe with no backup, no badge, and no official standing.

Cyran is walking toward an altar with someone else’s hand on his spine.

I square my shoulders.

Storming the castle was never in my job description.

Neither was falling in love with the man they plan to sacrifice.

By the time the Great Pulse hits, they’ll have both.
