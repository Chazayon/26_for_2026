# Chapter 14 — *The Crash of Glass*
### Lysa | POV

There is a special kind of evil unique to bureaucracy—the ability to turn atrocities into neat, alphabetized columns.

The Marrow Archives smell like the Tribunal would if someone had killed it a century ago and forgotten to bury the body.

Cold seeps up through the soles of my boots as I descend the last of the Halo Ward’s spine-steps, hand skimming the rail. The corridor here is narrower than the public levels above, the bone-walls closer together, lamps fewer and farther between. The air is still, preserved by spellwork that slows decay in paper and, I suspect, people.

I’m running on tea, adrenaline, and about forty minutes of sleep stolen poorly between finding Cyran collapsed behind the Skull chamber and hauling him back to something like himself.

His hand in mine. His voice raw around the words Make it stop.

The image rides with me all the way down.

At the end of the corridor, an iron door squats like a bad idea. Heavy. Unadorned. A small, functional plaque beside it reads MARROW ARCHIVES in the same clean script the Tribunal uses to label cannulae and punishment sigils.

I slide my fingers into my inner pocket.

The brass key Meris pressed into my ink-stained hand yesterday is still there, surprisingly warm against my fingertips. The little jawbone etching along its head is worn, edges softened by decades of use. Or centuries.

“Don’t make me regret this,” she’d said.

I’m about to try very hard not to.

The lock resists at first, not from any real enchantment—just from disuse. It turns with a protesting groan, like the building itself is objecting on principle.

The door opens on a breath of cold that feels different from the city’s usual marrow-chill. This is curated cold. Intentional. Preservation, not neglect.

I step inside and let it shut behind me.

Lumen-spheres hover at irregular intervals along the ceiling, their pale light barely touching the ground between the towering shelves. Bone-wood stacks climb floor to ceiling, loaded with ledgers, scrolls, and glass jars that cradle pale things in yellowing fluid. Labels flank the aisles in tidy script: TEMPLE ASSETS, RITES DOCUMENTATION, RESURRECTION PROTOCOLS.

The air smells of formaldehyde, ancient dust, and that particular paper-ink scent that has been the background radiation of my adult life—only here it’s older, deeper. Like inhaling a library’s grave.

I stand very still for a moment, letting my eyes adjust, letting the sheer weight of it settle.

Every shelf is a ledger of choices. Men like Thessen have always needed people like me to make their appetites legible.

I’m here to weaponise that.

Footsteps echo softly somewhere to my left.

I turn, hand going automatically to the stun-baton at my hip, thumb hovering over the activation sigil.

A man steps out from between two shelves as if he’s been conjured by the thought.

He is tall. Slender. His white physician’s coat hangs perfectly on his frame, sleeves crisp, buttons small dark dots down the front. A silver tray rests balanced on one hand, the other tucked neatly behind his back.

His hair is silver, but not with age—too deliberate for that. Combed back with precision that makes my scalp itch in sympathy. His eyes are a pale, washed-out grey, irises almost blending into the whites.

He does not blink as quickly as he should.

“Investigator Vaskin,” he says.

His voice is warm. Cultured. A nice, expensive voice, like the ones advocates use to soothe witnesses right before they gut their testimony.

The tray bears a porcelain teapot, delicate cups, a small dish of herb leaves. Steam curls in graceful tendrils.

“Dr. Marrow,” I say.

It’s not a guess. His name is carved discreetly into the plaque beside the door: ARCHIVIST-MEDIC MARROW, MASTER OF HALO WARD RECORDS. I’ve read it every time I’ve passed this corridor and pretended I wasn’t curious.

He smiles.

It doesn’t reach his eyes.

“I had heard,” he continues, “that Voss’s favourite scribe had acquired a key. I wondered when you’d decide to use it.” He sets the tray down on the nearest reading desk with a soft clink of porcelain. “Tea?”

“No, thank you,” I say.

He pours anyway, the pale green liquid catching the lumen-light. It smells faintly of mint, clean and sharp, and under that something antiseptic that pricks at the back of my sinuses.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” he remarks conversationally, pushing one cup across the desk toward me with a fingertip. “But then, neither are half the subjects in these ledgers. The law is very flexible when it serves itself.”

My hand stays on the baton.

“I have authorisation,” I say. “Founder Clause Eleven-A. Coercion of a bonded asset.”

His brows lift a fraction. “How quaint,” he murmurs. “Someone remembered the Founders.”

He picks up his own cup, inhales the steam.

“You’re looking for something specific,” he says. It isn’t a question. “You smell like ink and incense and Vigil steel. And…” He leans in fractionally, nostrils flaring with a delicacy that makes my skin crawl. “Blood that is not your own.”

My jaw tightens.

“Do you make a habit of scenting your colleagues?” I ask.

“I make a habit of noticing,” he says, unbothered. “It keeps me alive.”

He straightens, sips his tea, then glances past me to the rows of shelves.

“Temple records are in Section Four,” he says. “Asset acquisition ledgers on the upper tiers. Rites documentation below.” His eyes slide back to me, bright and mild. “I would advise starting with the ledgers. The rites are… messy.”

“I’m not here for spectacle,” I say. “I’m here for precedent.”

“Of course.” He sets his cup down with surgical precision. “So was the last investigator who haunted this place. Ilan, I believe his name was. Halo Ward junior. Lovely boy. Terrible taste in cases.”

Something cold and curious stirs under my ribs. Ilan. The name is in Meris’s gossip. In the marginalia of a few old rulings. I file it away.

“Is he still on active duty?” I ask.

Marrow smiles.

“He is alive,” he says, which is not the same thing. “You people rarely ask the right questions.”

“I ask the ones relevant to my file,” I say.

“Mm.” He glances at the untouched cup in front of me, then reaches to straighten a stack of nearby folders that did not need straightening. His hands are steady. “Well then. I won’t keep you. Try not to get lost. The old records have a way of… keeping what they catch.”

He inclines his head, polite as any courtier, and moves past me toward the door.

I wait until the iron clicks shut behind him before I let my shoulders drop half an inch.

The tea steam curls between us still. I slide the cup aside with two fingers, away from my reach, and head for Section Four.

TEMPLE ASSETS. RELIC ACQUISITIONS.

The sign is small, carved neatly, as if what it labels is mundane.

The bone-shelves here are taller, the ledgers thicker. Red leather bindings glower from the stacks like fresh wounds amid the more modest browns and blacks. Gold letters pick out designations and dates along their spines.

A chill that has nothing to do with the preservation spells slides down my back.

I drag a ladder along the shelf, its wooden wheels squeaking softly, and climb.

Dust lies thick along the upper reaches; it coats my fingers when I run them along the spines, grey-black against my already stained skin. The cough that bubbles up is quickly suppressed. My head is aching again, a dull, persistent throb behind my eyes.

“Designation…” I mutter, squinting at the index plates. “Seventeen, Ribcage, Relic class…”

There.

C-17. BLOOD RELIC. RIBCAGE.

Red leather. Of course.

I curl my fingers around the spine and pull.

The ledger is heavier than it looks. The weight drags at my shoulders. I brace it against my chest, climb down carefully, and carry it to the nearest reading desk.

The cover is stiff with age, the red gone darker where hands have worn it over the years. A seal crusted at the bottom bears the old temple sigil, overwritten by the Tribunal’s—a neat little stamp of conquest.

I set it down.

For a moment, I simply look at it.

This is Cyran, as far as the institution is concerned. Everything they ever thought worth recording about him, bound up in calfskin and filed away in a room people like Marrow haunt on purpose.

“Whatever I find,” I whisper to myself, “he deserves to have someone see it as it is.”

I open the ledger.

The first page is intake: date of acquisition, original temple designation, basic physical description rendered in clinical shorthand. Height. Weight. Bloodline uncertain, notes of an experiment. Subject amenable.

Amenable.

My fingers clench around the edge of the parchment. It crackles faintly under the pressure.

I turn the page.

The entries march down in neat columns, each on its own line, terse and bloodless.

“Consecration of vessel successful,” I read under my breath. “Subject demonstrates high compliance under Rite Sequence Three. Initiate controlled predation; donor appeasement exceeds expectations.”

Consecration of vessel.

Compliance.

Appeasement.

I translate automatically.

They carved the sigils into his skin and bled him for show. He obeyed when the words hit that fresh-carved magic. They made him bite the donors and told everyone it was devotion.

I turn another page.

“Rite Variant Seven introduced to increase spectacle. Subject’s regenerative capacity confirmed via controlled termination-resurrection loop. No significant degradation noted after third resurrection.”

My hand flies to my mouth before I can stop it.

Controlled termination-resurrection loop.

They killed him.

More than once. On purpose. To see how far they could push.

The letters blur for a second. I blink hard, force them into focus. The ink is faded but still legible, each stroke of the quill a casual cruelty.

“Compliance adjustments administered after unauthorised hesitation during offertory segment,” the next line notes. “Subject reminded of his purpose. Subsequent performance exemplary.”

Hesitation.

Reminded of his purpose.

I taste bile.

Images flicker, uninvited: Cyran lounging with a glass of wine, joking about being the city’s favourite horror story. Cyran in the cistern, blood-spattered and very carefully not looking like a hero. Cyran behind the Skull chamber, hands at his throat, gasping that he did the rite, he drank the cup.

He has always described himself as an ornament. A weapon. A thing made for pleasure and violence.

The file backs him up, in language so detached it makes my teeth hurt.

I turn another page.

“Donor-Circle Seven reports increased tithe following intimate display of Relic’s obedience.” There is a margin note, written in a different, more hurried hand. “Consider extended use as focal point for penitent rites. His beauty is an asset; fully exploit.”

My vision goes white for a second.

I realise I’m gripping the edge of the desk so hard my knuckles have gone bloodless.

His beauty is an asset.

Of course he thinks that’s all he has to offer. The law told him so. Not metaphorically; literally, in ink, on official parchment, in a line someone was proud enough to initial.

I flip through faster now, breath shallow, skimming for the words that matter.

Compliance.

Appeasement.

Deviance corrected.

There’s an entry further on, years later, when the Tribunal has already sunk its claws into the temple’s carcass.

“Subject transferred to Vigil program under Contract Lord Thessen’s personal patronage,” it reads. “Temple designation retired; new parameters established. Public framing: redemption through service. Private function: donor appeasement and covert enforcement.”

The ink here is fresher. The hand is one I recognise from modern files—neat, precise, bureaucratic.

They didn’t just inherit him. They repackaged him.

Holy duty became civic protection. The cage changed shape. The lock stayed the same.

My stomach turns.

One paragraph near the bottom of the page is bracketed in thin red lines. Formal addendum. My eyes snag on it, reluctant.

“Note: Subject demonstrates habitual seduction behaviours toward authority figures,” it reads. “Utilise where useful for donor engagement. Monitor for attempts to manipulate evaluators. Do not mistake charm for autonomy.”

I sit back so fast the chair legs screech against stone.

Charm for autonomy.

They wrote that down as a warning.

They weaponised his survival strategy. Codified it. Wrote it into instructions for future handlers so none of them would ever be fooled into thinking the pretty monster had a will of his own.

My throat tightens.

The flare of anger is so sharp it almost reads as nausea. It’s directed nowhere I can hit—not at him, never at him—but at the entire edifice that made this possible.

Choices.

My eyes sting. I squeeze them shut for a heartbeat, fighting the urge to let anything spill. Tears are no use to him. They add nothing to the argument I’ll eventually have to make on paper.

I force them open again.

Keep reading.

One entry, three-quarters down the next page, nearly undoes me.

“During Rite Iteration Twelve, donor expressed discomfort at Subject’s visible distress. Adjusted dosage of sedative in pre-rite wine; increased numbing agent to reduce overt signs of pain. Donor satisfaction restored.”

They didn’t make it less painful for his sake.

They made it less visible, so the donors wouldn’t have to confront the reality of what they were paying for.

My hand shakes as I trace the line.

“Consecration,” the temple called it. “Redemption,” the Tribunal claims.

What it really was, is, has always been: theft.

Another shadow falls across the page.

I freeze.

This one is too large, too close, blocking the light from the lumen-sphere in a way Marrow’s never did.

My hand flies instinctively to my baton again as I look up.

He stands at the end of the aisle like the ghosts the citizens whisper about: pale, too still, eyes catching the light in ways that don’t belong in living things.

Vigil leather, not ceremonial this time, but even that looks wrong on him here. Dishevelled. Collar half-undone. Hair loose around his face instead of carefully styled. His fangs aren’t fully dropped, but they hover on the edge of it, visible with every breath.

His gaze is locked on the open ledger.

On the page where I have my finger braced.

I see the exact moment he understands what it is.

His whole frame goes rigid. The brightness in his eyes gutters down into something hard and flat.

He crosses the distance between us so fast the air snaps.

“Don’t—” I start.

Too late.

His hand slams down on the ledger, snapping it shut with a crack that ricochets down the aisle like a gunshot. Dust leaps from the pages. The force of it jars my elbows, shoves me back against the edge of the desk.

For a second, the whole archive seems to hold its breath.

He’s close.

Much closer than anyone should be in a room like this. The desk digs into the small of my back; there’s nowhere to step without climbing onto it or into him.

He looms, deliberately, using every inch of height, every line of predatory grace. The latent threat in his body is not subtle—the set of his shoulders, the angle of his jaw, the way his hand still pins the ledger to the desk as if it might sprout legs and run.

Vigil training tells me I should reach for my baton. Institutional conditioning whispers that I should step aside, let him pace, let him have the illusion of control so he doesn’t do something rash.

I do neither.

I plant my palms on the desk behind me, anchoring myself, and look him dead in the eye.

“Move,” he says.

His voice has lost its music. It’s rough, raw around the edges, scraped over something jagged on the way up.

“No,” I say.

His pupils blow wide.

He takes one more step, closing the last of the distance between us. The cold from his armour seeps through the thin wool of my robes where his chest nearly touches mine. The ledger between us is the only thing keeping us from full contact.

“Hungry for scandal, are you?” he hisses. “Can’t resist a bit of archival voyeurism? How many times did they kill me, Investigator? How many donors did they sell tickets to? Did you get to the part where they catalogued which screams the high priests preferred?”

His breath hits my face, hot with rage and the faint trace of the myrrh and wine from last night. It makes me want to cough.

“I got to the part,” I say evenly, “where they wrote you down as an asset to be fully exploited.”

His grip tightens on the ledger. The leather creaks ominously.

“You read my owners manual,” he spits. “Without asking. Without—”

“Yes,” I cut in. “I did.”

He actually flinches at the baldness of it.

“Do you want an apology?” I ask. My voice comes out quieter than his, but the words are sharper. “Do you want me to cry and tell you I didn’t mean to pry? Because I didn’t come down here to satisfy my morbid curiosity, Cyran. I came because if I don’t understand exactly how they used you, I can’t stop them from doing it again.”

His eyes flick to my face, searching for pity. For horror. For whatever reaction he’s braced himself against.

All he finds is fury.

“Atrocities,” I say, jerking my chin toward the ledger under his hand, “don’t stop being atrocities just because they’re written in pretty script. You know that better than anyone. But the Tribunal will argue those words mean you enjoyed it. That you were amenable. That you were doing holy work. If I let that stand unchallenged, Thessen wins.”

“Congratulations,” he snarls. “You’ve found yourself an excellent angle for your closing argument. ‘Ladies and gentlemen of the Tribunal, let me list off all the ways my client was decoratively brutalised.’ I hope the shock value is worth it.”

“That’s not what this is,” I say. “And you know it.”

“What I know,” he bites out, leaning in until his nose is almost touching mine, “is that you saw everything they did to me laid out like a menu, and instead of walking away, you turned the page.”

His words hit me like a slap.

What I did.

What I chose.

He isn’t wrong.

“I did,” I say. “Because I owe it to you to know. The file they gave me at intake was a lie. This is closer to the truth, but it’s still their version. I can’t fix what I don’t look at.”

His laugh is a short, vicious bark, nothing like the lazy chuckles he uses on donors.

“Fix me,” he mocks. “Is that the plan, Investigator? You see a nice broken thing in a cabinet and decide you’re going to patch it up? How very noble. How utterly, devastatingly foolish.”

“I am not here to fix you,” I say, and I mean every syllable. “I am here to break the story they wrote about you. I can’t do that with half the facts.”

“Those aren’t facts,” he snaps, shoving the ledger a half-inch along the desk as if he wants to pitch it into the nearest preservation vat. “They’re fantasies. Convenient little narratives they crafted to salve their donors’ guilt. You want facts? Ask the pretty monster how many times he begged. Ask him how often he tried to bite his own tongue off before the rites so he wouldn’t give them the sounds they paid for.”

The words tear out of him like shrapnel.

He didn’t mean to say that last part. I see it the second it leaves his mouth.

His jaw slams shut.

Silence drops between us, heavy and thick. The drip of condensation somewhere in the stacks ticks the beats of his heaving breath.

“I can’t ask you any of that,” I say, as evenly as I can manage, “if you keep refusing to answer.”

His eyes catch mine, furious and cornered.

“You banned the notebook in the chapel,” I remind him. “You told me if you told me anything, it would not be for the record. I respected that. This,” I tap the ledger with one finger where his hand doesn’t cover it, “is the record they made. If you want to correct it, you have to tell me where it lies.”

He stares at me.

For a moment, I think he’s going to grab the ledger and walk out, leave me here with nothing but the afterimage of his anger.

Then he steps even closer.

The desk digs into my spine. The space between our bodies is gone. His free hand comes up, slamming down palm-first on the wood beside my hip, caging me in.

“Or what?” he demands quietly. “You’ll write me up as non-compliant? Difficult asset? Prime candidate for donor engagement due to… what was it?” His mouth twists. “Habitual seduction behaviours.”

He doesn’t know I’ve read that line.

It lands like a stone anyway.

“I will write,” I say, each word measured, “that the official record is incomplete. That there are significant omissions and euphemisms that materially affect the ethical evaluation of your case. That I lack your testimony because you refuse to give it.”

“Testimony,” he echoes, dripping contempt. “Such a lovely word. Rolls off the tongue while they’re carving it out of you.”

“Yes,” I say. “And it’s the only weapon we have.”

He sneers.

“You think they care what I say? They have ledgers full of holy duty and donor satisfaction.” He jerks his chin toward the shelves. Dust motes spin in the lumen-light. “Why would they listen to a Relic who decided to cry abuse after two centuries of performance?”

“Because I will force them to,” I say. “Because I will put it in language they can’t ignore. Because I will drag every euphemism they ever wrote into the light and label it correctly.”

He opens his mouth.

I don’t let him use it as a weapon this time.

“It wasn’t devotion,” I say. “It was coercion. It wasn’t redemption. It was slavery. You were a person. They made you a thing. That is theft, Cyran, not holiness, and I intend to write it that way.”

The words shake.

Not because I doubt them. Because I don’t, for a single second. They tremble with the force of it, with the rage I can’t direct anywhere useful yet.

He goes very still.

For the first time since he stepped out of the darkness, he looks… unsteady.

Like I’ve knocked his feet out from under him with a single sentence.

“You—” he starts, voice cracking, then stops. Swallows. Tries again. “You can’t… you can’t just say that.”

“Why not?” I ask, softer now. “Because no one ever has? Because they wrapped it up in scripture and law and told you it was the best you could hope for?”

His throat works.

My own hand has relaxed on the desk without my noticing. The knuckles ache where I’d been bracing.

“I am not going to apologise,” I tell him. “Not for needing this information. Not for reading what they did to you. If I hadn’t, I would still be operating under the lie that you were a willing instrument. That you were… amenable.”

The word tastes like poison.

His lips curl at it, reflexive.

“What I am going to say,” I continue, “is this: you have a choice I can’t make for you. Either you walk away now, and I go into that Tribunal chamber with nothing but their ledgers and my fury. Or you tell me your version, and I put it into the record alongside theirs.”

His eyes are bright. Too bright.

“And if I can’t?” he whispers. “If I open my mouth and nothing comes out but ash and jokes?”

“Then we work with that,” I say. “But you have to decide to stand on your own history instead of letting theirs swallow you. I can’t drag it out of you. I won’t.”

He stares down at me, close enough that I can see the faint tremor in his lower lashes. Close enough that I can smell the slightest hint of last night’s myrrh still clinging to his hair, fighting with the colder tang of archive dust.

“That’s convenient,” he says, though the bite has dulled. “You read my worst days and call it handing the pen back.”

“Yes,” I say. “Because it’s the only way the story changes hands. I can’t pretend this—” I tap the ledger again, very lightly “—doesn’t exist. Ignoring it doesn’t make it less real. It just makes it easier for Thessen to keep winning.”

His shoulders slump, the predatory lines softening in ways that make him look suddenly, achingly young.

The ledger beneath his hand is still closed.

“If I walk out,” he says, not quite looking at me, “will you keep reading?”

“Yes,” I say, because lying here would be a deeper betrayal. “But it will still be their version. And it will still hurt you.”

He exhales, a ragged rush.

“And if I… if I tell you?” he asks, voice roughening further. “The… details. The things they didn’t write because there aren’t pretty words for them?”

“Then I will close this,” I say, nodding toward the book, “and listen. No pen. No ledger. Just you and me. And afterwards, if you want any of it in the record, we decide together which words it wears.”

He finally looks at me.

Really looks. Past the robes. Past the mark on my wrist. Past the ink on my fingers.

The chill of the archive presses in around us, preservation spells humming in the bones of the shelves. The tea on Marrow’s abandoned tray has gone cold, a thin skin forming on its surface like congealed blood.

The Ticking Clock hums at the back of my mind. Forty-eight hours. Less now. Every minute here is a minute we aren’t drafting motions, canvassing allies, building the scaffolding of a defense.

I don’t move.

He pushes off the desk at last, taking a single step back.

The sudden space between us feels too big.

He drags a hand through his hair, composure sliding back into place in tatters. The ledger remains on the desk, his hand no longer pinning it, but close enough that I know he could snatch it up and hurl it into the formaldehyde jars if he wanted.

“Bone-chapel,” he says abruptly. “Lower Ribcage. The one with the cracked altar.”

I blink, thrown by the shift.

“What?”

“If I decide,” he says, carefully enunciating each word as if it might break in his mouth, “to hand you the ugliest parts, it won’t be here. It won’t be in front of—” he gestures vaguely at the shelves “—them. Tomorrow. After sundown. If you’re still determined to drag truth out of me, meet me there.”

Something in my chest loosens, painfully.

“All right,” I say. “I’ll be there.”

His mouth twists in something that might, in a kinder world, be a smile.

“Of course you will,” he says, half to himself.

He turns on his heel before I can answer, strides down the aisle with predatory grace carefully reined in. He does not look back. The door does not slam behind him, but it might as well.

Silence rushes in.

The ledger sits on the desk between my hands, closed, red leather dark against the pale bone.

I drag in a breath that shakes a little and let it out slowly.

My hands are trembling again. I flatten them on the desk until they still.

He’s right. I have violated his privacy. I have read the worst days of his existence as if they were just another case file. No amount of righteous intent softens that.

But he is also wrong.

I didn’t keep turning pages because I wanted to taste his pain.

I did it because someone has to look these words in the face and name them for what they are. Because if I flinch away, if I avert my eyes the way every donor and every priest and every judge has, then I’m just another name on the list of people who decided holy duty sounded nicer than slavery.

I rest my fingers on the ledger’s spine.

“Consecration,” I whisper. “Compliance. Appeasement.”

The words sit on my tongue like curses.

When I write this report, I will translate them. I will not let the Tribunal hide behind pretty language.

For now, I close the ledger and slide it back toward the edge of the desk, where the dust is already settling.

Tomorrow, in a chapel that smells of old incense and damp stone instead of formaldehyde and cold intent, he might choose to speak.

He might not.

Either way, I’ll be there.

Because I have turned the page, and there is no going back to pretending the margins are clean.
