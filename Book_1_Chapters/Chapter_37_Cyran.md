# Chapter 37 — *What Whole-Hearted Looks Like*
### Cyran | POV

Peace is harder than war.

In war, you know what to do with your hands.

You hold the blade.

You brace for the blow.

You count the exits.

In peace, your hands lie idle, traitorous, on a rug in a quiet room, pretending to hold a book you are absolutely not reading while you stare at a woman worrying her quill like it offended her ancestors.

Lysa chews her quill when she’s thinking.

It leaves little crescent dents in the wooden shaft. Ink stains her lower lip a faint grey where she’s touched it without noticing. A tiny, feral part of me wants to be that quill. To be the thing occupying her teeth and tongue and entire, formidable attention.

Instead, I am occupying the rug.

It’s a nice rug.

Warm, patterned in some abstract knot‑work that probably has profound cultural significance to someone less busy having a crisis. I’m stretched on my stomach, propped on my elbows, book open in front of me.

I have not turned a page in twenty minutes.

The room is… quiet.

Not Temple quiet—thick with held breath and sanctified expectation—but simple, domestic quiet.

Fire crackling in the hearth.

Clock ticking on the wall.

Lysa’s pen—she finally gave up on the chewed quill—scratching steadily over parchment at her desk.

No distant screams.

No divine heartbeat.

No Vigil boots in the hall.

It should be unbearable.

Somehow, it’s worse.

My spine itches.

My palms tingle, phantom memory of burned sigils and god‑light.

I stare at a sentence about… something. Bone‑market trade law, I think. The words swim.

My eyes slide sideways, of their own treacherous accord, back to her.

She’s in her chair by the desk, bare feet tucked up under her, Tribunal robe traded for a simple tunic and leggings rolled to mid‑calf. Her hair is down, ink‑dark waves falling around her face. There’s a smudge of soot on her cheek she missed earlier when she washed.

New quarters.

Spine‑adjacent, but not in the main Tribunal building.

A small, crooked‑ceilinged apartment tucked into one of the vertebrae outbuildings, with a narrow bone‑framed window that looks out over the lower ribs. When she took the key from Registration, she said it was for “efficiency.” Closer to the Ribcage. Easier to oversee reforms.

She did not say: also easier for a recently retired Relic to sneak in and out without giving the Tribunal conniptions.

I close my eyes.

Listen.

Tick.

Scratch.

Crackle.

Breathe.

My chest tightens.

There’s a moment—small, stupid—where the room tilts and all I can hear is the clock.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

The hair on my arms stands up.

Any second now, the scream will come.

The explosion.

The crash of bone.

Someone pounding on the door, yelling Relic, asset, back to your cage, time’s up, this was cute while it lasted, darling, but the Prime Contract has balancing to do.

My fingers dig into the rug.

The book’s spine creaks under my thumbs.

I stare at the unmoving door like I can will it to betray me early, get it over with.

It doesn’t.

Of course it doesn’t.

Torture is patient.

Peace is… whatever this is.

A sliver of ink blotches Lysa’s page.

Her scratching pen pauses.

“Cyran?” she says.

I realize I’m standing.

When did I stand?

The book slides uselessly to the floor.

I pace three steps across the rug, turn, pace back.

The movement doesn’t help.

There’s too little space to prowl properly, too few threats to map. The furniture is an insultingly domestic battlefield: desk, shelves, low table with two mismatched mugs, the cushioned chair by the fire where she reads.

My heart races.

Useless traitor.

“Cyran,” she says again.

Not sharp.

Not alarmed.

Just… calling me back.

I drag in a breath.

It hits oddly in my chest, snagging on old burns.

“I’m fine,” I say.

It’s the automatic answer.

The wrong one.

The pen sets down.

Wood on bone desk.

She stands.

The sound of her chair legs scraping lightly against the floor is somehow louder than the clock.

“What’s wrong?” she asks.

I laugh.

It’s a thin, brittle sound.

“Nothing,” I say.

I make a helpless gesture that almost takes in the whole room.

“Everything is… fine.”

She arches a brow.

Silence stretches.

She’s gotten very good at using quiet as a lever.

I hate and adore it.

“I’m waiting for the scream,” I blurt.

“Or the explosion. Or the divine reprimand, or the Vigil captain with bad news, or your superior with a writ for my arrest, or—”

I stop.

Breathe.

My hands are shaking.

Of course they are.

I curl them into fists.

Burn scars pull tight.

“There are no explosions scheduled for Tuesday,” she says, dry.

It cuts through the spiral enough that an unwilling bark of laughter punches out of me.

“Did they post a notice in the Public Accounting Square?” I ask.

“Because I seem to have missed it.”

She walks toward me.

Barefoot, quiet.

She doesn’t reach out.

Yet.

Her eyes track my hands.

My shoulders.

My too‑fast breathing.

“You know that’s not how life works,” she says.

“I am intimately aware,” I say.

“That’s the problem.”

I scrub my fingers through my hair.

It’s loose tonight, falling around my face, still stubbornly unwilling to be tamed by any combs she’s bullied into my hands.

“I don’t know how to do this part,” I admit.

The words drop out of me like bone slipping in a bad joint.

“The after. The… *nothing’s currently on fire and no one’s using my body as a bargaining chip* part.”

Her mouth softens.

It hurts, how much that helps and makes it worse at the same time.

“I wake up,” I go on, because now that I’ve started I can’t seem to stop, “and I wait for the weight. The orders. The—” I wave a hand. “The script. There’s always been a script. Even with the Vigil, there was a list. Patrol here, feed there, report at this time, be a good monster and we’ll keep you on a leash long enough to pretend you’re almost free.”

I gesture around the room.

At the graph of her life that has, somehow, made room for mine.

“Now there’s… this,” I say.

“You. A room. Dinner that smells like you’ve committed crimes against onions. And I don’t—” My voice cracks.

I force it back into something like lightness.

“Peace is boring,” I say.

“It’s supposed to be boring. I should be delighted. I *am* delighted. I don’t know what to do with my hands.”

Her gaze flicks down.

My fists.

My scars.

Her mouth twitches.

She steps in very close.

Close enough that the heat from her body registers as its own small hearth.

“You just… are,” she says.

The simplicity of it offends me.

“Is that a legal term?” I ask.

“Existence?” She tilts her head, pretending to consider. “I can draft a statute if it helps.”

She lifts a hand.

Waits.

I let her touch me.

Her fingers come to rest lightly on my chest, over the center of the half‑healed burn that lies where the Heart‑Node’s energy hit hardest. The skin there is a strange, mottled patchwork of old brand, new scar, Tribunal sigil half‑erased.

Her palm is warm.

Solid.

“Heart beating,” she says.

“Lungs breathing. You doing something ridiculous with your eyebrows. That’s the job description.”

I look down at her hand.

At the steady rise and fall under it.

“That can’t be it,” I say.

“It can,” she says.

“It won’t always be this quiet. You know that. There will be reforms. Trials. New Contract Lords trying to claw their way back to relevance. The Tribunal will fight me. The Vigil will sulk. You’ll have patients to help who make you want to tear your remaining hair out.”

“Charming,” I say.

“I look forward to the professional exasperation.”

“But,” she continues, “right now? Right now, the most urgent thing in this room is that I’m down one quill, and you’re pacing a groove into my rug because for the first time in centuries, no one has told you what you’re for.”

“That’s not—” I begin.

She lifts her gaze.

It pins me more effectively than any sigil.

“What if I mess it up?” I ask.

The question spills out before I can dress it in something wittier.

“What if I don’t know how to be… this? Whatever this is. A man. A partner. Not a weapon or an ornament. What if I drop it? What if I break it?”

“You will,” she says.

No hesitation.

Brutal.

Honest.

I flinch.

Then she adds, “So will I. Then we fix it.”

“You make it sound simple,” I say.

She shrugs.

“Orbiting stars is simple physics,” she says. “That doesn’t mean it isn’t terrifying when you’re the one trying not to crash.”

I stare at her.

Her hand on my chest.

Her ink‑stained fingers.

The faint chewed mark on her lower lip.

A few weeks ago, we were standing under a screaming god, arguing legal precedent with a man who thought carving up children made him holy.

Tonight, she is promising me the chance to ruin dinner and fix minor emotional catastrophes until we die.

My throat tightens.

“Lysa,” I say.

I don’t even put a pet name on it.

Her eyes flick up.

“Yes?” she says.

I step in.

Slide my arms around her, careful of my still‑tender hands.

She makes a soft sound—surprise, not objection—as I pull her against me and bury my face in her hair.

It smells like ink and soap and a faint thread of something herbal from the tea she favors now that coffee reminds her of late nights in a broken system.

Her arms come up around me a second later.

No hesitation.

She fits under my chin perfectly, as if the gods, in their one act of competence, sized us for each other before they died.

“Thank you,” I say, into her hair.

“For what?” she asks.

Her voice is muffled against my shirt.

“For waiting,” I say.

The words scrape their way out.

“For… staying. For not deciding I was too much trouble when I tried to martyr myself without your permission. For writing me into your life in ink instead of pencil. For—”

Her hands tighten.

“Cyran,” she says.

I shut up.

“I’d wait a long time for you,” she says simply.

The room tilts again.

Not in panic this time.

In something that feels like the floor shifting to make space.

It is ridiculous, that this—this small, warm, domestic absurdity—lands harder than a god offering me a throne.

I pull back far enough to look at her.

Her cheeks are a little pink.

Her eyes are steady.

The corner of her mouth twitches, like she’s daring me to turn this into a joke.

I don’t.

“Don’t,” I say instead.

“Ever… wait *instead* of living. If I vanish into some idiotic sacrificial plan again, I expect you to be very cross.”

“Oh, I will be,” she says.

“Good,” I reply.

“I find your anger… invigorating.”

“Another terrible kink to add to the list,” she mutters.

Her hands slide down my chest.

One of them catches on the hem of my shirt, fingers curling there like a hook.

A quiet gurgle from the direction of the hearth interrupts whatever I was about to say.

We both turn.

A pot hangs over the fire on the little swing‑arm bracket she installed her second day here. The contents are… bubbling in a way that suggests enthusiasm rather than culinary competence.

Smoke curls lazily toward the chimney‑vent.

Something smells… aggressively caramelized.

“I’m hungry,” I realize.

It sneaks up on me.

I haven’t been properly hungry in weeks. The aftermath of the Node left my system so flooded with energy that feeding was more an exercise in careful calibration than need.

Now, with the connection severed and my body settling into something like a new normal, want unfurls in my gut.

Lysa sighs.

“Good,” she says.

“Because I burned the stew.”

I stare at the pot.

At the small plume of smoke.

At her faint, beleaguered grimace.

“You… burned stew,” I repeat.

“In a city built inside the corpse of a god that has safe, reliable access to bone‑fire and a hundred priests who could sear flesh with a thought, *you* managed to burn a pot of onions and bone‑broth.”

“Your point?” she says.

I blink.

Twice.

Then laughter breaks out of me.

Real.

Full‑bodied.

Not the brittle, barbed thing I use as a weapon, but something softer, messier, that leaves my ribs aching in a not entirely unpleasant way.

She scowls.

It’s undermined by the way her mouth twitches.

“Oh, don’t you start,” she says.

“I was distracted drafting clauses that will keep you and your fellow monsters from being turned into decorative furniture again. I can’t do everything perfectly.”

“On the contrary,” I say, moving toward the hearth.

“I think this is perfect.”

I peer into the pot.

The top layer of stew is indeed tragically scorched, a thin black film clinging to the sides. Underneath, the rest seems… salvageable.

“It looks… crunchy,” I lie.

She snorts.

“That’s bone,” she says.

“It was always going to be crunchy.”

“Then we simply declare it intentional,” I say.

“Charred marrow is very fashionable this season. All the best smugglers are serving it.”

She pads over.

Leans around me to look.

Our shoulders brush.

Warmth flares low in my spine.

“We could get takeaway from Cairn’s,” she suggests.

“The Hollow King owes you at least three free meals.”

“Tempting,” I say.

“But if we start our hard‑won domestic bliss relying on someone else to feed us, we’ll never learn valuable life skills.”

“Such as?” she asks.

“How to choke down burned stew with grace,” I say.

She elbows me in the ribs.

Gently.

“Fine,” she says.

“We eat the stew. You pretend it’s delicious. I pretend not to notice you pretending. Then we go back to arguing over the best way to dismantle a centuries‑old legal framework.”

My chest does that stupid, painful warm thing again.

“Yes, love,” I say.

“That sounds… astonishingly like a life.”

She goes still for a heartbeat.

Then smiles.

Small.

Real.

“I suppose it does,” she says.

We rescue what we can of the stew.

It’s terrible.

We eat it anyway, sitting cross‑legged on the rug, bowls balanced in our laps, shoulders bumping now and then.

She walks me through her draft amendments.

I point out which clauses the Contract Lords will try to abuse, based on painful personal experience.

We bicker amiably over phrasing.

“You cannot put ‘absolutely not’ in a formal statute,” she says, exasperated.

“Why not?” I ask.

“It’s so clear.”

“Because language matters,” she says.

“You taught me that,” I remind her.

She rolls her eyes.

“Not like this.”

We talk until the fire burns low.

The clock ticks on the wall.

At some point, my hands stop itching for a blade.

They find hers instead, casually, on the rug between us.

Her fingers curl around mine without looking.

As if it’s habit now.

As if I am.

Later, when the bowls are empty and the ink on her statutes is drying and the city outside is quiet in a way that feels… earned, I stretch out on the rug again.

She gathers her papers into a neat stack, sets them aside.

Then, without ceremony, she abandons the chair and lies down beside me, head pillowed on my shoulder, one leg thrown over mine in a way that would have gotten her arrested for indecency in half the Temple’s old codes.

“Better?” she asks into my shirt.

Her breath is warm.

Her weight is heavy and anchoring.

“Yes,” I say.

I stare up at the ceiling.

At the faint cracks in the bone where the city itself is still adjusting to the lack of a god’s heartbeat.

“I’m still… scared,” I admit.

“Good,” she says.

“Means you know this matters.”

I huff.

“Wise and terrifying, as always,” I murmur.

She hums.

Sleep pulls at her.

At me.

As my eyes drift shut, that old, ugly voice in the back of my head whispers its familiar refrain.

This won’t last.

They’ll take her.

They’ll take you.

Peace is a lie.

For once, another voice answers.

Mine.

Maybe it won’t, I think.

Maybe it will.

Everything ends.

Contracts expire.

Cities fall.

Gods die.

But right now, in this one, absurd, borrowed evening, there is a woman drooling ink on my shoulder, a pot of ruined stew in the hearth, and a stack of draft laws that say I am a person, not a thing.

Right now is enough.

I tighten my arm around her.

She sighs in her sleep and burrows closer.

“Cyran?” she mumbles.

“Mm?”

“Don’t burn the city down while I’m napping,” she says.

Drowsy.

Serious.

“I’ve just fixed the paperwork.”

I smile into her hair.

“I’ll try very hard to resist the urge,” I say softly.

For the first time in a very long time, the urge doesn’t feel like the only thing I am.

Peace is harder than war.

But I think, with practice, I might be very good at it.
