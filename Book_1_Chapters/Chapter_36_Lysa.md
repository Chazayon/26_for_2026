# Chapter 36 — *The Bone Tunnel Reconciliation*
### Lysa | POV

His hands are shaking.

Mine are steady.

Between the two of us, we make one whole person.

The tunnel reeks.

Dust and blood and the sour tang of god‑ash cling to the back of my throat. Every breath rasps like I’ve been drinking sand. Bone‑walls loom close on either side, gleaming dull white in the guttering light of the sigil‑lantern tied to my belt. The floor under my boots is uneven, old marrow‑channels cut by water and time, now half‑blocked with fallen debris from the Chamber collapse above.

Behind us, the godsheart is finally, mercifully silent.

Ahead of us, the spine‑ward tunnel curves away into shadow, familiar in the wrong kind of way.

This is where it started.

Sub‑basement access. Smuggling route. The night I first saw him bleed for someone else’s convenience in a place no one was supposed to see.

Appropriate, I suppose, to end here.

If this counts as an ending.

Cyran leans heavily against me, most of his weight draped over my shoulders. His boots drag more than step. Each time his feet catch on a ridge of bone, his balance goes, and I have to brace us both.

I am not built for hauling unconscious vampires through subterranean mazes before dawn.

My legs tremble.

My back throbs.

My arm—sliced shallowly by Varos’s dagger—is sticky with drying blood under the torn sleeve.

But my hands are steady where they grip him.

One arm cinched around his waist.

One hand fisted in the front of his shirt, keeping him upright.

He breathes.

Shallow.

Raspy.

But steady.

That’s all that matters.

“You’re heavy,” I mutter.

My voice bounces weirdly off the curved bone.

He makes a sound that might be a laugh, or just air leaking out of a punctured lung.

“Apologies,” he croaks.

It’s more breath than word.

“If you… wanted someone delicate and portable, you should have… audited a pixie.”

“I’ll note that for next assignment,” I say.

We both know I won’t.

There won’t be a next assignment like this.

Not after tonight.

A fine crack snaps somewhere high above. Bone dust sifts down in a fresh curtain. I hiss between my teeth and angle us under a thicker spur of rib, just in case the ceiling decides to join the general trend toward collapse.

Cyran’s head lolls.

His temple bumps my shoulder.

I squeeze his waist.

“Stay with me,” I say.

“Tempting offer,” he mumbles.

“I am… exceptionally comfortable. But I think I’ll pass out now, if it’s all the same to you.”

“It’s not,” I say.

We take another three shuffling steps.

My thighs burn.

My lungs protest.

Adrenaline is gone; what’s left is stubbornness and paperwork waiting on the other side.

And him.

Always him.

He destroyed the Heart‑Node.

I watched him drive that dagger into the god’s throbbing crystal. Watched the light claw its way through his body. Watched the Prime Contract’s geometry flare and crack like glass dropped from a great height.

He made himself the lightning rod.

He could have made himself a god.

He chose mortal.

He chose… this.

Everything else I can argue with.

That, I cannot.

At the fork where the main tunnel curves up toward the Temple’s other sub‑levels, a figure detaches from the shadow—armor grey with dust, Redemption mark on his throat flickering uncertainly.

Warden Kerrin.

Of course.

He’s the sort of man who would stand near a collapsing sanctum entrance out of sheer stubborn duty and terrible planning.

His eyes go wide when he sees us.

Or rather, sees Cyran slumped half‑conscious over me like some ruinous, singed statue I decided to steal.

“Tribunal!” Kerrin blurts, then, “Rel—Cyran—what in the ribs—”

“Later,” I say.

I don’t slow.

If I stop now, I might not start again.

“The High Priest is back there, unconscious. Secure him. Call for Onas or any healer who isn’t in love with the Temple. And get someone from Registration down here who can take statements without creative editing.”

Kerrin steps aside automatically as I shoulder past.

The training wins over the shock. Good.

“Is the… the Node—?” he stammers.

I don’t look back.

“Consider this an early report,” I say.

“It’s gone.”

He sucks in a breath.

The sound of it echoes down the bone corridor.

“By whose authority?” he asks, because even now, even here, the Vigil has its scripts.

I shift Cyran higher against me.

His groan ghosts warm over my neck.

“Mine,” I say.

“And his.”

That will make for an interesting entry in tomorrow’s log.

If the Tribunal building is still standing.

If the city hasn’t decided to riot.

If.

Kerrin hesitates.

Then his boots scrape on bone as he heads up the side passage at a run, toward the wreck of the Heart‑Chamber and the High Priest lying on its floor.

Good.

Let someone else drag Varos’s dead weight for a change.

The sigil‑lantern at my belt gutters again.

I adjust it with my elbow, coaxing a little more light out of the tired glyph. It flares just enough to show the side alcove to the left—a shallow scoop in the tunnel wall where the bone juts back, dry and mostly intact.

We hid there once.

Night seven.

Trapped under the city with a man who wanted to pay me in his own body, because it was the only currency he believed he had left.

Different lives.

Same walls.

“Here,” I say.

I steer us into the alcove and let my back hit the curve of bone with controlled collapse.

The impact sends a jolt up my spine.

My knees try to buckle.

I take us both down slowly, sliding along the wall until I’m sitting on the cool, dusty floor, legs stretched out, Cyran half‑sprawled between them.

For a second, I just breathe.

The silence is loud.

No chanting.

No divine heartbeat.

No crackle of ritual sigils.

Just the slow, irregular drip of water somewhere down the tunnel, and the occasional distant rumble as the Temple settles, sulking, into its new, weakened shape.

Cyran’s head lolls back against my shoulder.

His eyes are at half‑mast, irises too bright in the dim.

Up close, he looks worse.

His clothes are scorched in a dozen places, blackened patches ragged around the edges where the Heart‑Node’s energy licked through. The skin of his hands—what I can see of it under the smeared blood and ash—is blistered, angry red shading toward white in the worst spots.

He smells like burned metal and singed silk and something underlying that is just… him.

He turns his head enough to squint at me.

“You look…” His voice scrapes. “Terrible.”

I huff.

“Thank you.”

He blinks slowly.

His mouth curves.

“You’re welcome.”

A beat.

“You also look… infuriatingly beautiful,” he adds, more quietly.

“And terrible. It’s a charming combination.”

“That’s the bone dust,” I say.

“And the blood.”

I glance down at myself.

My robe is no longer Tribunal black.

It’s mottled grey and brown and rust, torn at the sleeve where Varos’s blade caught me, hem ripped from scrambling, crawling, surviving.

My quill case is still clipped to my belt.

Somehow.

Ink leaked in spiderweb patterns across the leather where a crack hit it.

It feels like a metaphor for something.

I push the thought away.

One emergency at a time.

“Let me see,” I say.

I untangle one of his arms from where it’s draped across his stomach.

He makes a faint protesting noise, but doesn’t pull away.

His palm is a ruin.

Blisters thick and ugly, skin split in places where he gripped the dagger through all that divine current. Sigil‑scars that once marked his obedience are blackened to char, their edges flaked and crumbling, like old ink burned off parchment.

“Is it bad?” he asks.

He tries to make it light.

It almost works.

I swallow.

“It could be worse,” I say.

He snorts.

“Lysa.”

I sigh.

“It’s not good,” I correct.

He lies there, head tipped back against my shoulder, watching my face with that unsettling, unblinking attention.

I drop my gaze to his hand so I don’t have to look at the way he looks at me.

“We need to clean this,” I say.

“And cover it.”

“I’ll heal,” he murmurs.

“Eventually. Dramatic scarring suits me.”

“Scarring is earned,” I say.

“I’d prefer you not earn more tonight.”

I reach down and tear a long strip off the bottom of my robe.

The fabric gives with a dry rip, exposing dust‑marked ankles and the tops of my boots.

He makes a faint appreciative sound.

“Careful, Auditor,” he says.

“Any more of that and I’ll be forced to lodge a complaint about your indecency.”

“Submit it in triplicate,” I say.

“Right now, I’m short on proper bandages.”

There’s a thin trickle of water seeping down one wall, darkening the bone. I lean over, cupping my hand under it, ignoring the way my ribs protest the stretch.

The water is cold.

Tastes faintly metallic when I wet my lips without thinking.

God’s old lymphatic system.

Repurposed as emergency wash basin.

I wet the strip of cloth.

It goes pink in my fingers.

“This will hurt,” I warn, bringing his hand up again.

He swallows.

His throat works around burned skin and old brands.

“I deserve it,” he says.

The words are soft.

Automatic.

Too fast.

My hands still.

I look at his face.

“At least wait until I apply the salve before we get into self‑flagellation,” I say.

He blinks.

I can see the moment his brain catches up with his mouth and realizes what he’s just confessed.

He looks away.

Too late.

“For the burn?” I ask, quietly. “Or for leaving?”

Silence swells.

He flinches.

Just a bare tightening around his eyes.

“Both,” he says, eventually.

His voice is rough.

Honest.

I could let it go.

We are exhausted.

He nearly died.

The Node is dust.

The Temple is gutted.

We are sitting in a broken bone‑tunnel before dawn, held together with torn cloth and stubbornness.

This is not the ideal environment for a relationship post‑mortem.

But the thing about deferred maintenance is that it kills people sooner or later.

I press the damp cloth to his palm.

He sucks in a breath.

His back arches away from me, a little jerk he can’t quite suppress.

“Breathe,” I say.

“I am,” he grits.

“Unfortunately.”

I keep my touch as gentle as I can, dabbing away soot, ash, the worst of the blood. The blisters weep under the contact, clear fluid seeping around the edges.

“Lysa,” he says.

My name is a question.

A warning.

A plea.

“Yes,” I say.

I don’t stop what I’m doing.

“If I say I’m sorry,” he begins, “will you… stop… doing that?”

“No,” I say.

“Then I’m not sure I see the incentive.”

“For the burns,” I say, “you don’t have to apologize. You did what you had to do.”

My voice goes quieter.

“For leaving,” I add, “yes. You do.”

He shuts his eyes.

Long lashes against ash‑smudged skin.

“I thought you’d… be relieved,” he says.

“I thought—”

He breaks off on a hiss as I find a more tender patch.

I ease up.

He exhales slowly, deliberately, like he’s bracing for a different kind of pain.

“I thought I was saving you,” he says.

The echo of the Node’s offer lingers in his tone.

Power as protection.

Cage as kindness.

I wrap the cleaned hand in the torn cloth, fingers precise, careful not to bind too tight.

“From what?” I ask.

He huffs an incredulous, bitter laugh.

“From me,” he says.

He says it like it’s obvious.

Of course.

Old scripts.

Old lies.

“I left because I thought if I cut the tie, the Tribunal would stop looking at you like… like a hazard.” His jaw tightens. “I went back to the Temple because that was where all this always ends. With me,” he adds.

“Not with the woman foolish enough to stand beside me.”

I secure the bandage.

His fingers twitch under it.

“And did they stop?” I ask.

He barks out a laugh.

“No,” he admits.

“Thessen still circled you like a shark. The Tribunal still sniffed around for blood. The Temple still scheduled my little execution—pardon, ritual. All I accomplished was making you walk into hell alone.”

“Correct,” I say.

My chest feels tight.

The cloth is wet and slick under my fingers.

My voice stays even.

“You also accomplished making decisions on my behalf without consulting me.”

His eyes snap open.

“There it is,” he murmurs.

“You’re angry.”

“Yes,” I say.

The word sits between us, solid as bone.

He watches me.

Careful.

“Are you… still?” he asks.

I inhale.

Exhale.

The tunnel air is cold.

It feels good against the inside of my lungs.

“I was furious,” I say.

I don’t raise my voice.

It doesn’t need volume.

“You stood in my office and told me you were leaving so I’d be safe. You took the choice out of my hands. You made our relationship into an equation you could solve alone.”

I move to his other hand.

It’s in slightly better shape, but not by much.

He doesn’t pull away.

“You were following the same script everyone else in this city uses,” I continue, as I begin to clean. “Contract Lords, Tribunal, Vigil, Temple—they all think they know what’s best for everyone beneath them. They make choices, and people die, and they call it necessary.”

I look up.

My gaze catches his.

“I don’t need another man deciding what level of risk I’m allowed to take with my own life.”

Color drains from his face.

Even under the ash, I can see it.

“I—” he starts.

I cut him off with a small shake of my head.

“I know why you did it,” I say.

“I know it came from fear, not malice. I know you believed walking away from me was the only way to keep me out of the blast radius. And part of me…” I swallow. “Part of me understands. It mirrors my own worst instinct. To sacrifice anything, anyone, if it means the record stays clean.”

The cloth moves.

Dust comes away.

New lines of tender, burned skin reveal themselves.

“I don’t want you safe,” I say softly.

“Not like that. Not at the cost of being known only as someone you once loved enough to abandon.”

His breath shudders.

“I wanted you alive,” he says.

“Preferably with a career and your annoyingly rigid ethics intact.” A shaky smile ghosts his mouth. “I thought if I left, the Tribunal would let you stay unburned. That you could go back to your nice, tidy contracts and write angry notes in red ink about someone else’s monster.”

He licks his cracked lips.

“I didn’t stop to consider that maybe you didn’t want that,” he says.

“There’s the problem,” I say.

He winces.

“Do you want me to say I was wrong?” he asks.

“Yes,” I say.

He lets out a breath that’s almost a laugh.

“I was wrong,” he says.

The words cost him.

I can see it in the set of his jaw, the tightness around his eyes.

Good.

Important things should.

“For what it’s worth,” he adds, very quietly, “I regretted it before I reached the door.”

It hits lower than the ribs.

Something in my stomach twists.

“I know,” I say.

“It doesn’t change the fact that you still tried.”

He nods.

Slow.

Earnest.

“I won’t do it again,” he says.

I look at him.

Really look.

Blackened sigils.

Burned hands.

Eyes too bright from all that magic, now dimming slowly to something more human.

“You will,” I say.

He blinks.

“I beg your pardon?” he says, offended despite everything.

“This is who you are,” I say.

“Someone who has been taught that his existence ruins the people around him. That leaving first is an act of mercy. That making choices for others is safer than trusting they’d still choose you if they knew everything.”

He flinches.

I keep my tone gentle.

Uncompromising.

“You will have that instinct again,” I say.

“To walk away ‘for my own good.’ To throw yourself back into some burning altar rather than let me stand there beside you.”

He opens his mouth.

Closes it.

The bandage on his second hand is nearly done.

I tie it off.

“You’ll try,” I say.

“And I will stop you again. The same way you stop me when I start to disappear into my work, or into the Tribunal’s expectations, and forget I’m allowed to want anything else.”

He huffs out a breath.

Reluctant.

“Is this what partnership feels like?” he asks.

“Infuriating.”

“Yes,” I say.

“Also that.”

He lets his head fall sideways until it rests against my shoulder more fully.

The weight is heavy.

Comforting.

“I don’t want to keep you,” he says, after a moment.

His voice has lost its theatrical lilt.

It’s just… his.

Bare.

“I want—” He hesitates.

Searching for words that aren’t scripts.

“I want to… keep choosing you,” he finishes.

“If you’ll let me.”

“I will,” I say.

“As long as you remember I’m choosing you back. Not being… kept.”

He smiles.

I feel it, small and tired and real, against my neck.

“Noted,” he murmurs.

“I’ll have that engraved on something delicate, so I can forget and you can beat me with it later.”

“I’ll write it into the new statutes,” I say.

His laugh is a low rumble.

It vibrates through both of us.

Silence settles again.

Not the oppressive, ritual‑thick silence of the Heart‑Chamber. This is softer. Broken only by the slow drip of water and the distant creak of settling bone above.

My body is starting to ache in all the places I’ve been ignoring.

My cut arm throbs.

My knees complain about sitting on cold stone.

But Cyran’s breathing has evened out, deeper and less ragged, now that we are out of the immediate blast radius and I’ve given his nervous system a different kind of overload.

He shifts.

Carefully.

“I’m going to lean on you,” he warns.

He already is.

“Go ahead,” I say.

“Someone has to uphold my reputation as soft‑hearted and compromised.”

His head tips more fully onto my shoulder.

His hair is stiff with dust and ash, but still manages to feel softer than anything in this tunnel.

His newly bandaged hands rest in his lap, wrapped and useless.

He looks down at them with a faintly baffled expression.

“I hate this,” he says.

“Not the being alive. That part is… novel and surprisingly pleasant. The not being able to touch you properly.”

Heat crawls up my neck.

Ridiculous man.

“We’re both covered in blood and god residue,” I say.

“This is not the moment for ‘proper’ touching.”

He sighs.

“Tragic,” he says.

“We nearly die, save the city, dismantle an ancient power structure, and I don’t even get a celebratory grope. Truly, the Prime Contract was unjust.”

“You’re impossible,” I say.

My mouth twitches.

I let it.

He goes quiet for a moment.

When he speaks again, his voice has dipped lower.

Calmer.

“Are we…?” he starts.

Stops.

Rephrases.

“Did I ruin this?” he asks.

Just that.

This.

Us.

My anger flares, reflexive.

Not at the question.

At the fact that he has to ask.

At all the hands that taught him love is a fragile, conditional thing.

I slide my uninjured arm around his chest, under his, pulling him in closer until his side is firmly pressed against mine.

The movement drags at my cut.

I bite back a hiss.

He tenses.

“Lysa—”

“Stop,” I say.

He does.

I rest my cheek against the top of his head.

He smells like smoke and him and the awful, beautiful mess we just walked out of.

“We’re not unhurt,” I say.

“That’s not how this works. You left. I stayed angry. We both walked into the worst parts of ourselves and then had to walk back out.”

He is very still.

“But,” I continue, “you came back. You listened when I said I was furious. You didn’t try to charm your way past it. You didn’t ask me to pretend it didn’t matter because you saved the city. You apologized. You promised to try differently next time.”

I can feel his pulse against my ribs.

Fast.

Uneven.

“We are not… fine,” I say.

“Fine is for people who haven’t had their bones rearranged by gods and institutions. But we will be.”

His breath leaves him on a shaky exhale.

“Will be,” he repeats.

Like he’s testing the shape of it.

“Yes,” I say.

“On a good day, I would put us… somewhere between ‘functional’ and ‘dangerously codependent in a mutually negotiated way.’”

He snorts.

“Such romance,” he murmurs.

“If you wanted poetry, you should have seduced a bard,” I say.

“I seduced an Auditor,” he says.

His tone goes almost fond around the word.

“How utterly, devastatingly foolish of me.”

“Yes,” I say.

“And now you’re stuck with her.”

He tilts his head enough to look up at me.

The exhaustion in his face doesn’t hide the intensity.

“If that’s a promise,” he says softly, “I accept the terms.”

Warmth pools low in my chest.

Dangerous.

Welcome.

I lean down before I can overthink it.

Press my lips to the top of his head, in the tangle of ash‑stiff hair.

It’s not a kiss, not exactly.

More… a seal.

A quiet, exhausted punctuation mark.

He goes very still.

Then relaxes.

His shoulders sag, tension bleeding out.

“I’m taking that as your signature,” I say, when I pull back.

He closes his eyes.

“Always so formal,” he whispers.

“Someone has to be,” I say.

We sit like that for a while.

Bone at our backs.

Dust under us.

The city above rearranging itself around the absence of a god.

For the first time in too long, there is no deadline ticking in my head.

No urgent file waiting on my desk.

No command waiting for him in the next room.

Just the drip of water, the slow, human cadence of our breath, the ache in our bodies, the rawness in our throats.

And this.

Him, slumped against me, head on my shoulder, bandaged hands useless in his lap.

Me, spine straight even sitting, one arm anchored around him like he’s the only solid thing in a world that’s made of cracking bone and bad contracts.

“Lysa?” he says, after the third or fourth breath.

His voice is blurred at the edges.

Sleep is pulling at him hard now that the danger is past.

“Mm?” I say.

“If I fall asleep,” he murmurs, “don’t let the Tribunal… steal you while I’m unconscious.”

I huff a laugh.

“They don’t pay me enough to carry you *and* your paranoia,” I say.

His mouth twitches.

“Wake me when it’s time to… topple a few more institutions,” he adds.

“Ambitious,” I say.

He hums.

Goes quiet.

His weight gets heavier as he slips the rest of the way under.

I let my eyes close too.

Just for a moment.

The bone is cold at my back.

The silence feels heavy.

Like a blanket we both need.

We look like we crawled out of a grave.

Which, technically, we did.

The difference, this time, is that we chose to climb out together.
