# Chapter 13 — *The Performance of Blood*
### Cyran | POV

I am drowning in dry air and frankincense, smiling so hard my jaw might crack.

The Gods Skull is a terrible place to hold a party.

The chamber is carved into what used to be the hollow between titanic eye sockets, a vast dome of fused cranial plates overhead. Bone has been scrubbed and polished until it gleams like old porcelain, every curve etched with Tribunal sigils that mimic the old temple’s script just closely enough to be blasphemous.

Sound behaves strangely here.

Every whisper, every shuffle of boot on stone, comes back twice as loud. The applause from the gallery lands like blows. The echo of the bone-gong still vibrates in my teeth from the last commendation they handed out.

I stand at attention in the center of it all, a decorative spike in the ceremony’s arrangement.

Ceremonial armor plates my body in black and silver: pauldrons too broad, cuirass too tight, greaves polished so bright the marrowlight from the chandelier-ribs above skates along them in sharp, mocking lines. The collar rides high, stiff leather and bone pressing against the vulnerable column of my throat.

Not a leash, I tell myself. Not a chain.

My fingers twitch minutely against my thighs, desperate to slip between skin and leather, to tear the damned thing off before it bites.

Around me, other Vigil stand in their appointed places—hulking brutes in heavier plate, sleek god-blooded mages in less. They form a neat semicircle facing the dais, heads bowed in dutiful reverence.

I could not bow if I tried. If I defer to anyone now, it will not be to the Tribunal.

On the dais, Priest Kelton clears his throat.

He is a small man, balding, soft around the middle, wrapped in indigo robes heavy with stitched sigils. The censer he holds on its chain looks too large for his hand. It swings with a clumsy little hitch, as if the momentum keeps getting away from him.

The smoke does not.

Myrrh, thick and cloying, spills out in slow coils, exhaled through pierced bone vents. It crawls over the floor, gathers in the hollows of the carvings, winds itself around my ankles and up, up, up.

The first lungful is an offense. The second is a time machine.

Temple incense, my mind supplies, bright and certain as a knife to the ribs.

I keep my smile in place.

I have worn this exact expression while men opened my chest and called it devotion. It will hold a little longer.

“Honoured protectors,” Kelton intones, voice echoing far too well in the hollow Skull. “Tonight we gather to celebrate exemplary service to Sanctuary. To recognise those who have bled for this city, and to renew the sacred bonds that tie Vigil to Tribunal, Relic to Redeemer.”

Sacred bonds.

There’s a pretty euphemism for chains.

The collar scratches. Sweat slicks the back of my neck under my hair, trickling down my spine in slow, ticklish lines. My head feels full of cotton and knives. Every time I inhale, the myrrh digs deeper, layering itself over the lingering ghosts of lavender and wine from earlier.

Seventy-two hours, I think. Lysa’s voice saying we, the brass key in her pocket, the way she stood in the ruins of my control while glass lay at her feet and refused to move.

If Thessen sees me flinch now, he’ll call it malfunction.

Malfunction means expedited disposal.

I inhale again.

The smoke hits harder. The world narrows, the bright whiteness of the bone dome smearing at the edges as my pupils contract against the light. The sigils on the ceiling ripple, years folding over on themselves, Tribunal lines blurring into temple script.

High-Divine characters swim in my vision, carving themselves into the air between us.

“Priest,” Kelton corrects, mildly, as Voss steps back. “Will now lead the commendation rites in the tongue of the Founders, that all may be purified in purpose.”

Of course he will.

He turns back to us, raises the censer. His mouth forms the first word of the chant.

The sound that comes out doesn’t belong to him.

High-Divine isn’t meant for human throats. It’s an old, cruel language built from command phrases and flensing knives. The syllables don’t just pass through the ear; they dig in under it, hook themselves behind the bones of the skull, and pull.

The chamber’s acoustics amplify it, ricocheting each phrase off the curved plates overhead until it wraps around my spine like a hand.

My jaw clenches so hard my teeth ache.

You are in the Tribunal, I tell myself. It is year eighty-four. You are not there.

Kelton’s voice slurs into another man’s cadence. For a moment, I can’t see him at all.

High Priest Varos stands where Kelton stood: tall, immaculate, eyes like fresh wounds. The censer is the same. The smoke is thicker, hotter, tinged with something coppery and sweet. I know that smell. Blood bubbling on hot bone.

“Relic,” Varos says, and the word hits every carved sigil in my skin like a hammer. “Kneel.”

My knees unlock.

My body remembers faster than my mind.

For a heartbeat, everything in me tilts toward the floor—toward the clean line of submission I carved into habit centuries ago. My thighs tremble with the urge.

You are in the Tribunal, I repeat, vicious. It is year eighty-four. Lysa is in the shadows. There are no chains on your wrists.

I force my knees straight.

I do not kneel.

My claws bite into my gloves where my hands curl tighter against my thighs. The armour’s straps creak. No one notices; all eyes are on Kelton, on the pretty show of divine language.

He drones through the commendations, each phrase a barbed hook dragging through my nerves. I let my mind drift to anything else: the market smell of cumin on Lysas breath, the way her ink-stained fingers tapped her notebook against my chest at the old archway, the fierce light in her eyes when she told me she’d refused to sign.

Varos bleeds back into Kelton by slow degrees. The chamber resolves again into Tribunal faces and Vigil plate. My heart thuds like it’s trying to punch its way out of my ribs.

“And now,” Kelton says, “we honour our most devoted blade. The Blood Relic of the Ribcage.”

Ah. My cue.

He approaches, censer swinging, another object cradled in his other hand.

The chalice is gold—heavy, over-ornate, rim set with little teeth of onyx and bone. The liquid inside is a dark, viscous red, thicker than wine. Spiced steam wafts from it: cinnamon, clove, citrus rind.

And under that, like a handclap in a quiet room: copper and the bitter, throat-numbing tang of something alchemical.

Of course they matched the recipe.

He stops in front of me, robes swaying. Up close, I can see the damp patch under his arm where sweat has soaked through. His face shines in the god-light, soft and devout.

“Drink, Relic,” he says, nasal voice pitched to something he probably thinks is kindly. “And be renewed in your service.”

I can feel eyes on us from the gallery. Commanders. Nobles. Donors. Maybe Thessen himself, lurking in one of the private boxes with his ledger and his sweetmeats.

My hands shake as I reach for the chalice.

Not much. Just enough that the gold rattles softly against the armour when I take it.

I bare my teeth in my best charming monster’s grin.

“Always a pleasure, Father,” I purr, because if I say Varos’ old title in this room, maybe it will lose some of its power. “To service.”

A ripple of polite laughter scatters across the chamber. Kelton smiles, sheepish. He doesn’t hear the way my voice fractures at the edge.

I tip the chalice.

The first mouthful is spiced and warm, thick on my tongue. Cinnamon, yes. Clove. A hint of orange peel.

Under that, the drugged bite: sharp, metallic, blooming along my gums and down the back of my throat. It hits the old pathways in my blood like lightning, lighting up every carved sigil along my arms in phantom pain.

My body remembers this, too.

Rites wine. The temple’s favourite trick. Drink up, pretty Relic. It makes the knives feel like kisses.

For a fraction of a second, I am in two places at once.

Here: Gods Skull chamber, smoke and sanctimony, Tribunal sigils humming faintly under my boots.

There: old temple nave, arches slick with blood, Varos’ hand warm on the back of my neck as the drug crawls up into my brain and rearranges my obedience.

White bone. Red light. The sharp, copper-sugar smell of blood sprayed across carved script.

My hand nearly drops the chalice.

I drag it back to my lips, forcing another swallow down purely for the sake of the watching eyes. A drop escapes at the corner of my mouth, hot and sticky against my skin.

Someone in the gallery sighs audibly.

I lower the chalice with deliberate grace and pass it back to Kelton. He takes it, oblivious, turning to present it to the next poor creature in line.

The command phrases threaded through the chant tug at the places in my blood where Varos’ rites carved their own runes.

Down, something whispers in the back of my skull. Kneel. Obey.

No.

I lock my knees.

You are in the Tribunal. It is year eighty-four. You are not in the temple. Varos is not here. He is ash or hiding; either way, he is not here.

Lysa’s “We lose together,” from earlier, threads itself into the mantra. Her face in the candlelight of my quarters, not flinching when I raised my voice. The feel of the word together in my mouth, frightening and new.

I cling to that.

The rest of the rite passes in flashes.

My name rising and falling with the chant. Applause like rain on stone. The dull clang of the bone-gong that marks the end of the ceremony, reverberating in my ribs.

Then the Vigil are released back into the crowd like well-trained dogs at a garden party.

Nobles swarm.

They come down from their carved balconies in a rustle of silk and leather, smiles bright, hands outstretched. I am intercepted by a minor functionary whose name I never remember; his perfume is too strong, his compliments as rehearsed as my answers.

“Relic Cyran,” he gushes. “Such service. Such grace. The city owes you so much—”

“The city pays in Marks,” I say, because it’s easier to be flippant than to admit that my hands feel like they’re full of broken glass. “I’m sure the ledger and I will come to an agreement.”

He laughs sycophantically, already turning to signal to someone else that he’s spoken to the monster and survived. A woman in bone-lace gloves lifts her hand as if to touch my arm; I angle my body just enough that her fingers meet air.

I can’t breathe.

There’s too much noise, too much smoke, too many echoes of a voice that isn’t Kelton’s bouncing around the dome. My collar feels tighter by the second, the leather digging into the scar beneath like a brand being pressed fresh.

The exit behind the dais is a dark slit of velvet curtain, half-concealed by an ornamental skull standard.

I move toward it.

Not hurriedly; that would draw attention. A slow, measured drift, nodding and bowing and letting people bounce off me until I can slide behind the drape’s heavy fold.

On the other side, the sound drops like someone has shut a door.

The maintenance corridor is narrow and dim, the ceiling low enough that I feel the weight of the god’s bone directly overhead. Exposed pipes run along one wall, dripping condensation. The air here smells less of incense and more of stone dust and old oil.

I make it three steps.

Then my legs stop cooperating.

The armour that made me look like a beautiful statue in the chamber suddenly weighs twice as much. My knees buckle. I go down hard, gauntleted hands scraping uselessly at the stone as I try to catch myself.

The floor rushes up and hits me.

Pain registers. Then it’s obliterated by something worse.

The panic doesn’t arrive all at once.

It builds in strange increments: a hitch in my breath that doesn’t correct, a tightening in my chest that doesn’t ease, a high whine under the bones of my ears that might be the echoes from the Skull or might be something only I can hear.

My vision tunnels.

The corridor narrows, perspective skewing, until it feels like I’m at the bottom of a well. The walls press closer. The overhead plates curve in, closing, teeth clamping down.

My hands fly to my throat.

I claw at the collar, leather biting under my fingers. It will not give. The buckles are at the back, just out of reach; I know this; I have worn this ridiculous thing before, but knowledge does nothing to soothe the frantic animal in my ribs.

“Off,” I gasp. It comes out a rasp more than a word. “Off, off, off—”

It isn’t just the collar.

It’s the memory of metal links pulled so tight around my neck I saw stars. The remembered weight of Varos’ hand on the chain, the way he could tilt my head with two fingers and the right syllable.

I can feel the drug threading into my veins now, the temple mixture buried under Tribunal ceremony. It rides my blood like a rider breaking a horse, pressing at old habits: Yield. Present. Kneel.

No.

I jam myself back into the corner where the wall meets the floor, seeking something solid at my spine. Stone grinds against the plates of my armour, loud and too close.

My lungs forget how to work.

Air goes in, but it’s wrong, too shallow, too fast. I try to slow it. It speeds up. A ragged, high sound claws its way out of my throat—I don’t even recognise it as my own voice.

My fangs drop.

They punch through my gums without warning, a hot pinprick of pain, elongating past my lower lip. It forces my mouth open just enough that my breathing sounds even more frantic, every inhale a wet, gasping drag.

I curl my legs up reflexively, armour scraping, spine arching away from the imagined hand at my throat. My fingers keep scrabbling, seeking purchase on leather that will not tear.

Command phrases spin in the back of my mind, old and hideous.

Down. Still. Smile.

The darkness at the edges of my vision thickens.

Footsteps.

Sharp, quick, heels on stone, cutting through the cotton and echo like a knife through skin.

Every nerve in me shrieks.

I bare my teeth at the end of the corridor, a cornered thing. My claws leave shallow gouges in the stone as I drag myself tighter into the shadow, trying to make my body smaller, braced for touch I haven’t consented to.

“Don’t—” The word tears out of me, feral. “Don’t touch me. Get back. I did the rite, I drank the cup, I—”

The figure freezes.

The glow from the lumen-sphere in her hand paints her in soft, shifting light: indigo robes dark against the bone walls, hair pulled back, face pale with strain.

Lysa.

Of course.

She does not move closer.

She stands there, ten feet away, at the mouth of my little hollow, and does the strangest thing of all.

She lowers her hands.

The lumen drops a fraction, casting more shadow over me, softening the harsh edges of the corridor. She sinks slowly down to the floor where she stands, robes pooling around her knees, until she is seated. Not looming. Not reaching.

Her back rests against the opposite wall. Her knees draw up, not quite as tight as mine. She sets the sphere beside her on the stone, letting it glow between us like a small, domestic star.

“Cyran,” she says.

My name is not a command. It’s a question.

The sound threads through the roaring in my head more cleanly than the chant did.

“Go away,” I snarl. It comes out slurred around the fangs. “Get out. I did the rite, I drank—did what he—what you wanted, just—”

“Not temple,” she says, firm and precise. “Tribunal.”

Her voice is low, pitched to cut through panic without sharp edges.

“You’re in the upper corridor behind the Skull chamber,” she continues, as if she’s reading from a report. “It is Tuesday—day seventy-four of this Redemption year. You are wearing black leather ceremonial plate. I am wearing indigo.”

The words land oddly.

Upper corridor. Tuesday. Ceremonial plate.

They don’t match the images in my head—the slick temple floor, the gleam of Varos’ blade, the press of bodies in the nave. They collide with them, jarring.

“Stop,” I rasp. My hands are trembling so badly now that my claws click against the armour. “Make it—just make it stop—”

She nods once. Not in agreement, but in acknowledgement.

“I’m not going to touch you,” she says. “I’m not coming closer unless you ask me to.”

A bitter laugh claws its way out of my chest.

“Asking,” I manage. “That’s—not how this works.”

“It is now,” she says.

She shifts, just enough to brace her forearms on her knees. The movement is slow, telegraphed; I flinch anyway.

“You’re Cyran,” she says. “Blood Relic. Vigil designation seventeen. You are in the Tribunal, not the temple. High Priest Varos is not here.”

His name lands like a slap.

I suck in a breath that feels more like a sob.

“You don’t know that,” I hiss. “You don’t—you don’t know what he—”

“I know he is not in this corridor,” she says. “I know the only people here are you and me. And I know you can breathe.”

Her gaze drops pointedly to my chest.

I realise, in a distant, absurd way, that I am hyperventilating like a terrified recruit, shoulders jerking with each shallow drag.

“I—can’t,” I get out. “Can’t—”

“You can,” she counters, implacable. “It’s just difficult. I’m going to talk you through it.”

“You give the worst—” I break off as my lungs seize again. “Worst parties, Investigator.”

Her mouth twitches. It’s not quite a smile.

“Count with me,” she says. “In for four. Hold for four. Out for four.”

“No,” I growl. Numbers are for Marks, for ledgers, for the little notches they carve into my skin when I kill on command. “No counting.”

“Fine,” she says, not missing a beat. “Then listen to my voice. In while I speak, out while I speak. We’ll bully your lungs into remembering their job.”

“You’re bossy,” I mutter, because the alternative is keening.

“Yes.” She tilts her head. The lumen-light catches on the edge of her spectacles. “Breathe.”

I drag air in as she continues speaking, forcing my body to match her cadence.

“My name is Lysa,” she says. “Investigator of the Tribunal. I am sitting on the floor in a very expensive robe that will probably never forgive me for this. There is a lumen-sphere beside me. It smells of incense and cold stone. You are here. You are not on an altar.”

The air shudders out of me on the next sentence.

“Good,” she says. “Again.”

I do it again.

Each breath still feels wrong, as if the air is thickened, but it’s less like drowning and more like pushing through water now. The roaring in my ears shifts down a notch. The edge of the walls retreats a fraction.

The collar still bites. My fingers are still braced against it, knuckles white under the leather. I can’t seem to make myself let go. If I release it, it will tighten. That’s how this works. That’s how it always worked.

“Cyran,” she says, gentler now. “May I ask you something?”

I huff out something between a laugh and a sob. “That implies I have the capacity to say no, love.”

“You do,” she says. “You always do. May I hold your hand?”

The question hits me harder than any command.

Of all the things I expected to crawl out of this corridor—handlers, donors, priests—this was not on the list.

“Why,” I rasp. My throat hurts. “So you can pat the good hound? Tell him he’s very brave for not biting anyone in the nice chamber?”

“So you remember you’re not alone,” she says simply. “Only your hand. Nothing else. If you say no, I stay where I am.”

My fingers spasm around the collar.

The idea of touch makes every hair on my body rise. Every nerve remembers rough hands on my shoulders, on my face, on my hips. The temple used touch as leash and reward and threat. The Tribunal isn’t much better.

But the idea of sitting here, shaking alone in my armour, while the command phrases eat through my veins—

“Only my hand,” I echo, hoarse.

“Only your hand,” she confirms.

The drug hums in my blood. My heart thuds against the plates of the cuirass, frantic bird in a cage.

I nod.

It’s not a grand, cinematic gesture. It’s barely a jerk, more reflex than decision.

She treats it like consent.

“Thank you,” she says.

She moves.

Slowly.

She narrates every inch.

“I’m going to stand,” she says, and does, unfolding herself with care. My body tenses, expecting a rush; it doesn’t come. She stays where she is for a beat, letting me see she’s not about to loom.

“Three steps,” she says. “One.” She takes it. “Two.” Another. “Three.”

She stops just out of reach of my outstretched claws. The lumen casts her shadow across my boots, jagged and long.

“I’m going to kneel,” she says.

She does, lowering herself so that we are closer to eye level. I’m still curled in my corner, shoulder-blades pressed against stone, but I don’t feel quite as much like a rat trapped at the bottom of a bucket now.

“Left hand,” she says softly. “Reaching toward you now. You can pull away if you change your mind.”

My hand spasms.

It is a ridiculous act of faith, to hold still.

I do it anyway.

Her fingers brush the back of my gauntlet first—leather on leather. Then she shifts, finds the gap at my wrist where plate gives way to skin, and slides her hand in, wrapping around my bare, freezing fingers.

Her palm is warm.

Ink roughens the pads of her fingers, familiar grit against the back of my hand. Her grip is firm, not squeezing, not coaxing—just… present.

I inhale.

The urge to jerk away is immediate and violent. Every memory of being grabbed, yanked, manoeuvred flashes through my muscles.

She doesn’t tug.

She just holds.

“You’re here,” she says, soft and steady. “You’re in a maintenance corridor. The stone under your boots is cold. My hand is on yours. You can feel my pulse.”

I can.

It taps against my skin, a quick, mortal rhythm, out of sync with the drugged thunder of my own heart. Not commanding. Not demanding. Just… there.

My fangs ache. My throat burns. My lungs drag in another breath, this one a fraction deeper.

“You did the rite,” she says. “You drank the cup. It hurt you. It was wrong. But it is over. No one is going to ask anything more of you tonight.”

“Liar,” I whisper, but there’s no heat in it.

“If they try,” she says calmly, “they’ll have to get through me first.”

The image that calls up—a priest reaching for my chain and finding her hand instead—is absurd and terrifying and something else I don’t have a name for.

My shoulders sag.

Not all the way. The armour holds me upright whether I like it or not. But some of the impossible tension unhooks, just enough that my back flattens a little more against the stone, my head tipping forward to rest against the cool wall.

The world is still too bright, too loud, too close. But the edges are less vicious now. The corridor is a corridor again, not a collapsing skull. The air goes in and out, ragged but functional.

My hand is still shaking in hers.

“Look at me,” I mutter, disgust curling bitter on my tongue. “The great Blood Relic. Shivering in a hallway like a kicked dog.”

“I am looking,” she says.

I risk a glance.

Her eyes are very steady. There is no pity there. No disgust. Only a quiet, burning anger that has absolutely nothing to do with me, and everything to do with the people who thought this ceremony was acceptable.

“I am not leaving,” she adds.

That lands somewhere deep.

People always leave.

They leave when the show is over. When the teeth come out. When the blood dries. When the pretty monster reveals there is nothing under the ornaments but scar tissue and bad habits.

She is sitting on a cold stone floor in her best robe, hand wrapped around mine like it’s the most natural thing in the world, eyes saying I planned for this possibility and came anyway.

The drug’s grip loosens by increments.

The old command phrases ebb back into the parts of my brain I keep barricaded. Varos’ voice recedes into the marrow walls. My own thoughts seep back in, messy and human and infuriating.

I feel… wrung out. Empty and overflowing at once.

Slowly, very slowly, my breathing evens.

I realise at some point that my claws are no longer digging at the collar. My hand has loosened enough that I can move my fingers again. When I flex them, hers flexes too, a tiny answering pressure.

I’m not sure how long we sit there.

The bone-clock in the Skull chamber tolls once, muffled by walls and distance. The lumen-sphere hums softly between us, its light dimming as it burns down.

At last, when my heart is something closer to normal and my thoughts have strung themselves into lines instead of static, I exhale.

“I hate this,” I say.

“I know,” she answers.

“I hate that they can still do this to me with a few words and a bad vintage.”

“I know.”

“I hate that you saw it,” I add, quieter. “That you had to.”

I’d rather she remember me as the thing that drops off ribs into the mist and slaughters god-spawn with a smile. Not this—shaking and small and half-convinced I’m about to be dragged back onto an altar.

Her thumb strokes once, absently, along the side of my hand.

“I needed to,” she says. “If I’m going to fight for you, I need to know what they did to you. Not the version in the file. The version that lives in your bones.”

I close my eyes against the sting.

“That’s very noble,” I say. “Also deeply inconvenient.”

“How so?”

“It makes it harder,” I say, voice barely above a whisper, “to believe you’ll leave.”

There. The ugliest truth, laid bare.

She is quiet for a beat.

Then: “Good.”

I crack an eye open. “Good?”

“Yes.” Her chin lifts a fraction. “I would be very disappointed in myself if I made it easy for you to keep expecting the worst.”

“Careful, Investigator,” I murmur. “You’ll ruin my perfectly cultivated nihilism.”

“You can file a complaint,” she says. “After we’ve stopped them from auctioning you off.”

The words snag.

Them. Auctioning. We.

Seventy-two hours.

The bone dome overhead. The smell of incense. The weight of her hand in mine.

For the first time since the ceremony started, the thought of the coming days doesn’t feel like an unbroken stretch of inevitability.

It feels like a fight.

I swallow.

“Lysa,” I say.

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

She squeezes my hand once, brief and firm.

“Don’t thank me yet,” she says. “We still have to survive the next three days.”

“Always spoiling the moment,” I say, but the bite is gone.

We sit there a little longer, in the dim corridor behind the gods blind eye: a monster and his auditor, armour and ink, terror and its antidote.

Outside, the Tribunal toasts its own mercy.

In here, for the first time in a very long time, I begin to believe that when my mind drags me back to the temple, the person reaching for me might not be a handler.

She might be an anchor.
