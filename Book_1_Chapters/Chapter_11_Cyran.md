# Chapter 11 — *The Art of Being Decorative*
### Cyran | POV

Gold dust on the collarbones. Kohl on the eyes. A collar on the soul. 

That is the order of operations tonight.

I stand on the little raised podium in my quarters, bare feet on cold bone, and hold as perfectly still as I can while Mistress Vhane dusts me like an altar offering.

“Chin up,” she murmurs. “Higher.”

Her hands move with the absent confidence of long practice, fingers tipped in tiny rings that scrape lightly along my skin as she works. Each touch is precise, impersonal, and far too familiar.

She hums while she pins me into the silk.

Some lilting temple melody, all sliding vowels and easy devotion. My body remembers it before my mind does—those nights under red glass, incense thick enough to choke, donors swaying in perfumed crowds while the choir tried to convince them this was holy. 

I let my awareness skid sideways.

It’s an old trick. There are only so many hours of being handled like an object a person can stay fully present for before something cracks. Better to set myself gently on a shelf for a while and let the Ornament take over.

The Ornament likes this part.

The Ornament doesn’t flinch when Vhane brushes shimmering powder across his chest and shoulders, leaving a faint sheen that will catch every stray candle-flame at the Gala. The Ornament holds his breath when she leans in close to trace a fine gold line along the shallow dip of his throat, right over the place where, not so long ago, Lysa’s pulse hammered against my teeth. 

“You’ve been eating well,” Vhane notes.

Her voice is dry, professional. She clucks her tongue once, as if I’m a prize hound whose coat has finally come in properly.

“Skin is flush. No hollows under the eyes. Whatever the Vigil is feeding you these days, I approve.”

My body responds before I can think better of it.

I think of Lysa’s blood.

Hot and bright on my tongue, filling a hunger I’d kept leashed for so long I’d almost forgotten what sat at the end of the chain. The way her hand had fisted in my hair, not yanking me off, not pushing me down, just anchoring. The small, wrecked sound she’d made when the healing fire roared up my side. 

I think of the way she’d said I trust you and meant it.

“I aim to please,” I say aloud.

The words come out in the Ornament voice, light and edged, polished smooth to hide anything that cuts.

“Head still,” Vhane chides.

Her thumb presses under my jaw, tilting my face where she wants it. Her hands smell of rose oil and the faint acrid sting of hair lacquer. Up close like this, I can see the tiny lines at the corners of her mouth, the faint flecks of powder embedded in her own skin.

We’ve both been doing this too long.

“Lord Thessen has very specific notes,” she says, moving around behind me. I feel her lift the fall of my hair, fingers combing through the pale waves with professional indifference.

“Doesn’t he always,” I murmur.

“Less warhound, more showpiece, this time,” she continues. “He says you’ve been looking… coarse. Too much patrol. Not enough pampering.”

“Patrol does terrible things to a man’s complexion,” I say. “All that blood and civic duty. Simply ruinous.”

She sniffs, a little amused exhale.

“Arms up,” she instructs.

I obey.

The silk vest goes over my head in a whisper of fabric, sliding down my arms like water. It settles against my skin with the cling of something alive. The color is bone-white shot through with fine veins of gold thread, sheer enough that my Marks glow faintly underneath, the sigils along my ribs visible in suggestion rather than detail. 

The sides are cut scandalously low. The back is a network of straps that make me think of harnesses and shackles at the same time.

Vhane steps in front of me, fingers at the front closures.

“This will restrict your breathing,” she says, not as a warning, but as an evaluation. “Stand tall.”

She begins to tighten the vest.

It cinches in stages. Each tug compresses my ribs a little more, pushing my shoulders back, lifting my chest. I can feel my lungs recalibrating, learning to sip shallow instead of drag deep.

“There,” she says finally, tying off the last of the mirrored gold cords. “Now you wont slump.”

If I faint, I think sourly, it will look gorgeous on the floor.

“What about my work will be accessible tonight?” I ask, because I can’t quite stop myself.

She reaches past me to the table where she’s laid out the rest of the outfit. The movement brushes her shoulder against my bare arm, a fleeting contact as meaningless to her as breathing.

“Lord Thessen wants you…” she says, and there’s the barest pause—a tiny hitch, not of conscience, but of searching for the politest word—“…accessible.”

She lifts a panel of the vest I hadn’t noticed before.

It’s a triangular cut-out at the front, cleverly hidden in the seams. When she fastens the final set of hooks, the silk draws tight over my sternum but leaves a perfect, inviting window of skin over my heart and throat. Easy for a donor’s hand to slide in. Easier for a mouth.

Vhane adjusts the angle with a critical eye.

“There,” she says again. “A little temptation encourages devotion.”

“Of course,” I say, and smile the empty smile.

“Accessibility is my specialty.”

She does not hear the jagged edge of the joke. Or chooses not to. In her world, there are patrons and there are pieces. She is very good at making the pieces shine.

She moves down, smoothing the silk over my stomach, checking the fall of it against the tight black leather trousers someone has thoughtfully tailored a size too small. The material clings to my thighs and hips like a second, unforgiving skin, every line on display.

Jewelry comes next.

Not the tasteful rings and simple chain I sometimes wear by choice, small claims of beauty in a life built on violence. No. Tonight, Thessen has sent adornments that split the difference between ornament and restraint.

Vhane clasps a wide metal cuff around each of my wrists. They’re polished gold, studded with tiny bone-sigils that hum faintly when they touch my skin. Not enough power to bind, but enough to remind. Matching bands encircle my upper arms, my throat.

The collar is the worst.

It’s a delicate-looking thing, fine links and small charms, but it closes at the back with a solid, audible click. I feel it like the echo of a chain slipping through a ring in an altar floor.    

“Relax,” she says. “You’ll get a line if you tense.”

I let my neck loosen. The Ornament tilts his head just so, making sure the charms lie flat along the exposed tendon. Let them see the vein. Let them see the pulse. That’s what they’re paying for.

“Boot,” Vhane says briskly. “Right foot forward.”

I step down off the podium to sit on the edge of the nearby chaise, lifting my leg with an ease that is pure habit. She kneels—she always kneels for this part, and the irony of that has not diminished in thirty years—and slides the first boot over my foot.

The leather is soft, supple, cut to hug from ankle to mid-calf. She smooths it up my leg, fingers pressing along the muscle as if appraising a horse’s flank.

“Flex,” she says.

I do.

She nods in satisfaction. “Good. No swelling. Scarring minimized nicely, too.”

“Perks of divinity,” I say lightly. “We stay fresh.”

The second boot follows. By the time she’s finished, the line of me from throat to toe is exactly what Thessen ordered: a beautiful, lethal animal in a decorative harness.

“Stand,” Vhane says.

I do.

She steps back, surveying her work.

For a moment, the room holds its breath—the fire in the grate a low, obedient glow, the air thick with layers of perfume and hairspray and the faint metallic tang of the powders she’s used. 

“Look,” she says.

I turn toward the mirror.

It takes my brain a beat too long to process the reflection.

The man in the glass looks taller than I feel. Shoulders back, chest bare in all the right places, skin gleaming with gold dust. The Marks along his ribs cast an otherworldly glow through the sheer silk, a network of red lines that make him look like he’s been filigreed from the inside out.

His face is… stunning.

Vhane has sharpened my cheekbones with shadow, darkened my lashes and lids with kohl so my eyes look even paler, more inhuman. My mouth has been tinted a shade darker, lips bitten-rose, like I’ve just fed and enjoyed it.

There is nothing of the patrol in him. No scuffs, no scars, no hint of god-spawn ichor. Just polished, curated beauty.

He looks like a prize stallion. An expensive dog at show. A thing.

Perfect, Vhane says behind me.

The word used to give me a small, bitter flicker of satisfaction. At least, if I must be property, I am the best property.

Tonight, there’s only a wave of nausea.

“Lord Thessen will be pleased,” she adds, businesslike. “Remember your posture. And try not to smudge anything before you arrive; we don’t have time for touch-ups.”

“I’ll do my utmost to remain un-smudged,” I say. “Though the donors are notoriously enthusiastic.”

She smiles, distracted.

“They like you enthusiastic,” she says. “It’s part of the… charm.”

There’s no malice in it. No awareness.

She packs her kit with efficient motions, brushes clinking softly in their cases, glass vials of pigment and gloss sliding into padded slots. The scent of her perfume lingers, cloying, occupying every inch of air that isn’t already claimed by Thessen’s preferences.

At the door, she pauses.

“Good hunting, Relic,” she says.

And then she’s gone.

The latch clicks. The room exhales.

I let my shoulders slump for exactly three heartbeats.

The corset-vest makes that a mistake; my lungs protest, ribs digging into the unforgiving fabric. I force them still again, sucking in a shallow breath. The Ornament wants to take over entirely now—to stand in front of the mirror and practice poses, expressions, the exact angle of the head that says take me without the words.

I deny him that.

Instead, I reach for my boot.

The leather tugs where it’s tight around my calf, but there’s just enough give at the top seam. I slide two fingers inside, feeling for the small slit I cut there years ago.

It’s still there.

Some precautions never leave you.

The thin glass dagger nestles in its hiding place like a cold, sleek secret. No longer than my hand from wrist to fingertip, wrapped in a strip of cloth to keep it from cutting through. I worked its edge myself with stolen time and stolen tools, grinding the bone-sand and alchemical glass until it would slide through temple silk and mortal throat without a whisper. 

I pull it out just far enough to see the faint, wicked glint in the firelight.

“Just in case,” I whisper.

Not to the knife. To myself.

To the boy who lay on his back on an altar once and wished, with everything in him, for something sharp he could point the right direction.

My fingers itch to keep holding it. To let its presence sink into my palm like a promise.

Instead, I slide it back into its slot, cloth and glass snug against leather and skin. The weight is negligible. The knowledge of it is not.

A knock interrupts the moment.

Three precise raps. Not servant-quick, not donor-impatient. Measured. Professional. This close to the appointed hour, there is only one person in the city who would knock on my door that way.

I pull the Ornament’s smile on like a second collar.

“Enter,” I call.

The door opens.

Lysa steps into the room and for an instant, everything else—the mirror, the chains, the powder in my lungs—recedes.

She’s armored.

The severe Tribunal dress blues fit her like a commandment: high collar, long sleeves, the cut so modest it loops back around to a kind of austere elegance. Her spectacles catch the light, making her eyes briefly opaque. The only concession to anything resembling ornament is the silver of her seal at her belt and the ink stains on her fingers she’s forgotten to scrub fully clean. 

She looks like judgment and mercy went halfsies on a body.

“Relic—” she starts, then stops.

Her eyes travel the length of me.

It’s not a slow, appreciative rake. It’s a shocked inventory, as if she’s trying to tally, in one glance, all the ways Thessen has re-written me since she last saw me undressed in an ossuary for reasons that had nothing to do with spectacle. 

Her mouth tightens.

“What,” she breathes, “did they do to you?”

If I were a better man, I might look away. Spare her. Spare myself.

I am what I am instead.

I step off the podium with a little spin, letting the silk flare and settle, the gold chains at my throat chiming against one another.

“Do you like it?” I ask, striking a pose I know is flattering. One hip cocked, one arm half-raised so the cuff catches the light. “It’s the latest fashion in High-End Martyr.”

She doesn’t smile.

She walks toward me, slow and controlled, every line of her body saying careful in a way that has nothing to do with me and everything to do with whatever she is holding back.

Her hand lifts as she comes within reach.

Then stops.

Her fingers hover a breath from the sheer silk over my chest, close enough that I can feel the heat of her through the fabric. She doesn’t touch.

“You look…” she says, and the word frays on the edges.

“Expensive?” I supply.

Her gaze flicks up to mine.

“Like a target,” she finishes.

The word lands harder than it should.

I laugh, because that’s what I do when I don’t know what else to do.

“That’s the point, darling,” I say. “The donors get skittish if the prey doesn’t stand still and glow for them.”

“They’re not donors,” she says. “They’re clients.”

“Such an ugly word,” I say lightly. “And so accurate.”

Her nostrils flare.

She’s angry. Not the bureaucratic irritation I’ve seen her direct at careless clerks and smug Inquisitors, but something colder and far more dangerous. It’s not at me. At least, not primarily.

Her eyes keep flicking to the collar, the cuffs, the strategic cut-outs. To the faint shimmer of gold dust across my throat, which no one but Thessen and Voss and a few select patrons are meant to notice.

This is the first time she has seen, in daylight and with time to look, what they’ve made of me.

I find I am more afraid of her gaze than of Thessen’s.

“It’s just paint and cloth,” I say, softer than I intend. The Ornament falters for a breath. “Underneath, I’m still your favourite monster.”

Her head snaps up.

“My favourite—” She stops. Closes her eyes briefly, inhales. When she opens them again, they’re steady. “You are not a monster.”

I almost flinch.

“Disagreeing with the marketing now, are we?” I say. “Careful. The Tribunal might revoke your license.”

“The Tribunal can go hang from the tallest rib,” she says. “I’m not here as their envoy tonight.”

“No?” I lift a brow. “The robes are a little on-the-nose, if so.”

“I’m here as your escort,” she says.

Something in my chest does an unhelpful little flip.

“For the donors, it will look like oversight,” she continues. “A visible reminder that the Tribunal is… observing your deployment. For Thessen, it’s a message: he doesn’t get you alone.”

“And for you?” I ask, because I can’t not.

She hesitates.

Then, quietly, “It’s so you don’t walk into that chapel without someone at your side who sees you as more than a centerpiece.”

I go very still.

The room is full of mirrors, but in that moment, she is the only reflection that matters.

“You realize,” I say, when I can find the air, “that if you stand too close to a cursed object, people start to assume you’re cursed too.”

“I am aware,” she says. “I’m willing to risk the association.”

The corset makes it difficult to breathe. That’s my excuse for why it feels suddenly like my ribs are too tight for my heart.

“Dangerous woman,” I murmur.

“Efficient woman,” she corrects. “Who would like to get you out of this city alive.”

“Alive and with minimal smudging,” I add.

Her mouth twitches, unwilling, at the corner.

“Can you walk in that?” she asks, nodding at the trousers and the boots.

“I can do rather more than walk,” I say. “But for you, I’ll stick to the basics.”

She gives me a look that says now is not the time, and then, after a breath, steps closer.

“May I?” she asks.

It takes me a second to realize she means the collar.

Her hand is still hovering near it, fingers stained faintly with ink and sugar-water from this morning. The idea of anyone touching that piece of metal without being paid for the privilege does something strange to my equilibrium. 

“You may,” I say.

Her fingers brush the chain, skimming lightly along the fine links. There’s no ownership in the touch. No appraisal. Just a quick, precise assessment of tension and catch.

“Too tight?” she asks.

“It’s tolerable,” I say. “I’ve worn worse.”

A flicker of grief crosses her face at that.

She adjusts it a fraction anyway, tipping the clasp so it sits more comfortably against the back of my neck instead of digging into skin.

The difference is small. It feels enormous.

“Better,” she says.

“Thank you,” I reply.

The words feel strange in this outfit. Like speaking liturgy at a brothel.

A distant bell tolls, muffled through bone and mist. The kind the wealthy districts use to mark event hours, not prayer. 

“The carriage will be waiting,” she says.

“Yes,” I say. “Thessen waits for no one.”

I offer her my arm.

It’s a ridiculous gesture, given what I’m wearing. I look like I’ve been poured into silk and leather for the express purpose of being dragged somewhere and displayed. The courtly pose should clash. Instead, it feels like the only armor I have left.

She hesitates only a heartbeat before taking it.

Her grip is firm. Not possessive. Anchoring.

“I’m with you,” she murmurs, low enough that if there were any listening spells in the walls, they’d miss it.

“I know,” I think.

“That’s what terrifies me.”

Aloud, I say, “Shall we go feed the wolves, Investigator?”

She squeezes my arm once.

“Let’s go remind them who’s actually holding the leash,” she says.

We step out of my quarters together.

The corridor is quiet, lit by the soft glow of marrow-lamps. Somewhere below, in the courtyard, carriage wheels clatter against bone. Perfume from the upper levels drifts down, mingling with the ever-present scent of dust and despair. 

I straighten my shoulders. The corset bites. The collar rests heavier than its weight.

In the mirror’s last sliver of view, just before the door swings shut, I catch one final glimpse of myself.

Gold dust on the collarbones. Kohl on the eyes. A collar on the soul.

And, just beneath the edge of the boot, the faint suggestion of a hidden blade.

How much of me is left under the paint?

Enough, I tell myself, as Lysa walks beside me and does not let go.

Enough to remember which way the knife points.
