# Chapter 17 — *The Breath Between*
### Cyran | POV

A bed is usually a battlefield or a coffin. She makes it look like a cloud. 

Afternoon light spills through the warped shutters in long, dusty bars, picking out every crack in the bone-plaster walls of the safe house. The city hums faintly outside—cart wheels on rib-stone, a distant hawker calling about marrow-bread, somewhere a shriek and laughter that might be joy or a fight. 

In here, it’s too quiet.

Too still.

I sit on the rickety chair by the one tiny table, dagger in hand, whetstone in the other, and try to sandpaper my nerves down to something manageable.

Scrape. Scrape.

The blade is already sharp. It was sharp an hour ago. It was sharp before breakfast, when I pretended burnt coffee was enough to saturate the gnaw at the base of my spine. I’ve been over it so many times the edge sings when I draw it along my thumb’s pad, a high, satisfied whisper of steel. 

“Again,” I murmur.

Scrape.

The sound is a metronome for my thoughts.

Hollow Archive. Command Keys. The possibility that somewhere under all this bone, some priest’s neat hand has written a single word that can turn me back into a thing. 

I picture it: my name in the margin of a scroll, sigils around it like a cage. A phrase in some dead dialect that would slip between my ribs like a knife if someone spoke it.

Thessen’s smile when he bought that word.

Scrape.

“Cyran.”

I startle.

The dagger dips, nearly takes a divot out of my own thigh. I catch it, fingers clenching too tight around the hilt.

Lysa is watching me from the other side of the room.

She’s perched on the edge of the mattress-pile like it’s a perfectly ordinary piece of furniture and not a problem incarnate. Furs, old blankets, something that might once have been a feather mattress now beaten mostly flat. It takes up a third of the small back room, a nest pushed under the crooked window. 

The dust motes above it are doing an enthusiastic little dance in the sunlight.

My skin prickles.

“You’ll grind the blade away,” she says.

Her tone is mild, but her eyes are not. They flick from the dagger to my hands to my shoulders, reading tension like text.

“I have others,” I say.

My voice comes out too bright.

“Eventually you’ll run out of metal,” she replies. “Long before we run out of problems.”

“Morbid, love,” I say. “You’re starting to sound like me.”

She doesn’t rise to the bait.

Lysa is still in her Tribunal shirt, though she’s shed the outer robe. The sleeves are rolled to her forearms, ink-smudges dark against her skin. Her boots are off, bare toes curled slightly against the furs. There’s a faint crease on one cheek where she must have fallen asleep on her arm after we finished scrubbing treason off the floor. 

She looks tired. She looks stubborn.

“You need sleep,” she says. “Tonight will be hard.”

“Tonight is always hard,” I say lightly. “Hard nights are practically my specialty.”

“Cyran.”

My name, this time, is not a question, not a joke. Just a simple statement of fact: you are doing that thing again.

I rest the dagger across my knees and look everywhere but at the bed.

“I’m fine standing,” I say.

The room is small enough I can cross it in three strides. Two, if I’m annoyed. One, if I’m running for my life. Now, I don’t move at all.

Under my stillness, everything vibrates.

My bones buzz with leftover adrenaline from the Gala, from the escape, from the confession that still feels like a flayed nerve in my chest. My senses are too sharp—every creak of the building settling, every distant voice knifing in through the thin walls. 

Hyper-vigilance, the Tribunal pamphlets call it when they diagnose their own wardens like broken machines.

For me, it’s just… normal.

Lysa pats the space beside her.

“Come here,” she says.

Two words.

Command in the cadence. Not like temple orders; like Tribunal directives. But she isn’t my Warden. She isn’t my priest. She’s the woman who kissed my forehead last night and called me sharpened instead of broken.    

It’s deeply unfair that my body doesn’t care about the distinction.

“I don’t…” I start, and stop.

I don’t share well.

Beds are for work or sleeping alone.

Beds are altars, or stages, or examination tables. They are places where people decide what they want from me and then take it. Or they are narrow pallets in barracks where I lie very still, facing the wall, while someone in the next cot over cries into their blanket and pretends they’re not.

To share a bed for rest is… intimate.

Lysa tilts her head.

“We shared a cloak,” she reminds me. “In the tunnel.”

“That was different,” I say.

It was. It was necessity and bone-chill and the threat of god-spawn in the dark. The cold pressed us together as effectively as any command. Survival doesn’t ask for permission. 

“This is also necessity,” she says. “Just a different kind. If you go into the Archive like this—” she nods at my hand, still clutching the dagger—“you’ll jump at the first shadow and trip every ward in the place.”

“Insulting,” I say faintly. “But probably accurate.”

She lies down.

Not in some seductive sprawl, not arranged just so for an appreciative eye. She folds onto her side with the graceless sigh of someone whose spine has had enough. One arm tucks under her head. The other drapes in front of her, hand open on the furs. 

“I’m sleeping,” she announces, eyes closing. “Join me or pace. I won’t be offended by either. But I am not listening to three hours of you torturing that poor knife.”

The room goes quiet again.

The city hums on.

I can hear her breathing.

Slow, deliberate draws at first—she’s doing it on purpose, the way she did in the Skull when she sat with me through a nightmare. Then it evens into something less performative, her body settling into its own rhythm. 

I sit there.

My fingers tense on the dagger hilt.

In the strip of light that makes it past the shutters, dust swirls slowly, caught in little thermal currents I can’t see. For once, I don’t feel the need to count them. I just watch, caught between my chair and that bed and the doorway.

I am vibrating with the need to run, and she is an anchor of gravity.

This is absurd.

I hiss out a breath between my teeth.

“Fine,” I mutter, to no one.

Carefully, I set the dagger on the table. I unbuckle my belt—the one with the smaller, nastier blades—and coil it so I can grab it quickly if needed. I lean it against the wall within arm’s reach of the mattress. Old habits.

Then I stand.

Cross the three strides.

The furs dip under my weight as I lower myself onto the edge. I keep my back to her, body angled away, a respectful gulf of space between us. My muscles are so tight my spine could be carved from temple stone. 

I lie there, stiff as a board, staring at the cracked plaster, listening to the tiny sounds of the house. A drip in the wash alcove. The faint pop of the cooling stove. Her breathing.

My hands don’t know where to go.

If I put them under my head, it feels like posing. If I cross them over my chest, it feels like I’m preparing to die. I settle for one folded under the thin cushion they’re calling a pillow and the other resting very precisely on my own stomach, as neutral as I can make it.

I wait for my mind to start its usual parade.

It cooperates.

Exits: door, window (too small for a fast escape unless I want to lose more skin), under the bed if I need to roll. Weapons: dagger at the table, belt against the wall, my own teeth and hands, Lysa’s baton in easy reach by the door. Potential threats: whoever paid for this safe house in the first place, Thessen’s men if they track patrol routes faster than I expect, my own head if it decides to replay last night in vivid detail. 

The mattress shifts.

Her hand lands on my back.

Just… lands.

Warm palm, fingers splayed lightly between my shoulder blades, weight barely there. No grip. No digging in. No push or pull. Just contact, like she’s staking a small, quiet claim: you are here. I am here. This is a fact.

Every muscle under her hand goes electric.

My first instinct is to bolt.

My body tenses, a full-body flinch restrained to a twitch. Breath stutters. Old memories, old scripts, try to overlay themselves: hands on my back meaning get on your knees, arch, be pretty; hands on my back meaning hold still for the knife, for the sigil, for the collar. 

She doesn’t move.

“Okay?” she murmurs.

Sleep has crept into her voice already, softening the edges. It’s not a challenge. Not a command. Just a check.

I force air into my lungs.

Hold it.

Let it out.

“Yes,” I whisper.

And it’s true.

Once past the first electric jolt, something in me… unwinds.

The threat assessment loop that’s been spinning in my skull all day hiccups. Stalls. Her hand is steady, radiating a little circle of warmth through the rough cloth of my shirt. Each rise of my ribs presses me fractionally into her palm; each fall, I feel the tiniest drag of skin on fabric.

I realize, distantly, that I’m matching my breaths to hers.

In.

Out.

Slow.

The tension bleeds out of my shoulders in increments so small I barely notice, until suddenly I am not lying on top of the mattress like a corpse but sinking into it, the furs cushioning my hips, my spine.

I turn.

It’s not deliberate.

My body just decides that facing away from the most dangerous thing in the room—that would be her, for the record—is a bad idea. I roll gently, trying not to dislodge her hand.

She obligingly lets it slide, following the motion until it rests over my side instead.

We end up facing each other.

Her eyes are still closed.

She smiles anyway, the corner of her mouth tilting up as if she can feel me looking. A tiny furrow has smoothed from between her brows now that she’s horizontal. There’s a faint smudge of charcoal on her chin, from when she rested it on her hand this morning studying the map. 

A lock of hair has escaped its tie to curl along her temple. Her pulse flutters in the hollow of her throat, steady and human and so, so tempting in a way that has nothing to do with hunger.

I don’t want to bite.

I want to protect.

It’s ridiculous. I’m the one with the command spell potentially lurking in my head. I’m the one who might turn on her with a single word I don’t even hear. She should be wary of this proximity. She should be sharpening her own metaphorical dagger and sleeping with her back to the wall.

Instead, she’s giving me her throat.

Trust, offered without flourish.

“You’re staring,” she murmurs.

Her lashes flutter, but she doesn’t open her eyes.

“It’s customary,” I say softly, “to at least pretend you’re asleep if you’re going to accuse someone of that.”

“Occupational hazard,” she replies. “I’m good at noticing when I’m being observed.”

“Terrifying,” I whisper. “And here I thought I hid it so well.”

“You don’t,” she says. “You never have.”

There’s no judgment in it.

I shift a little closer.

Not enough to be obscene. Just enough that the space between us narrows, our foreheads almost touching. The furs rustle under the movement, a soft, scratchy sound. Her scent—sage from some cheap soap, ink, the faint metallic tang of Tribunal wards—wraps around me. 

I let my forehead brush hers.

A tiny contact, barely there.

She tilts her head the fraction necessary to meet it.

“If we survive,” I say.

The words surprise me as they leave my mouth.

It’s not like me to talk about afterwards. Afterwards is for people with pensions and retirement plans and a reasonable expectation of living past the next set of orders.

But the map on the floor. The way she said partner. The way she kissed my forehead as if there might be a future version of me worth blessing.

It’s all lodged in my ribs.

“If we survive,” I repeat, softer, “I’d like to try real wine.”

Her breath ghosts over my lips, warm.

“Real wine?” she echoes.

“Not the bribe kind,” I say. “Not the… payment kind. Not poured down my throat to loosen my tongue or my clothes. A bottle we chose ourselves. Somewhere that doesn’t smell like incense and blood. Possibly accompanied by food that isn’t screaming.” 

Her smile widens, slow.

“I’d like that,” she whispers back.

“Scandalous,” I murmur. “Next you’ll suggest we pay for our own drinks.”

“Incredible,” she says. “Autonomy and everything.”

I huff out a laugh.

It feels… strange.

Different than the brittle, performative laughter I’ve wielded for years. This one is small. Quiet. It doesn’t bounce off walls; it settles in my chest.

My eyelids feel heavy.

I fight it, at first.

Sleep with another person in the room is a risk. Sleep with another person sharing the bed is tantamount to signing a waiver that says yes, please, do whatever you like while my guard is down.

“Cyran,” she murmurs, barely a breath now.

“Yes,” I say.

“You’re allowed to rest,” she says.

Ridiculous.

Untrue.

Dangerous.

My body believes her anyway.

Hyper-vigilance unspools, thread by thread. The loop checking exits goes fuzzy at the edges. The list of threats shrinks, until all that’s left is the feel of her hand on my side, the warmth of her forehead against mine, the slow, steady drum of her heart.

I can still feel the potential for violence in my muscles. The readiness. But it’s a distant thing now, sheathed instead of bared.

I could kiss her.

The thought slips in, uninvited.

The angle is easy. Our mouths are a breath apart. All it would take is a tilt, a nudge, a small surrender.

I do nothing.

Because that’s not what this is.

For once in my very long, very bloody existence, a bed is not a transaction or a trap. It is a place where I’m allowed to lie down while someone keeps their hand on me not to use me, but to make sure I don’t drift away.

If I turn it into currency now, I will poison it.

Later, perhaps, there will be wine and choices and mouths and everything that comes with them. Later, when yes can mean something other than survival. When no is so solid between us that neither of us is afraid to lean on it.

For now, this is… enough.

More than.

My last conscious impression is of her exhale against my cheek, slow and even.

Safe.

I don’t remember dreaming.

I don’t remember the usual parade of temple corridors and red altars and hands and words that turn my spine to glass.

For the first time in decades, the dark behind my eyes is simply that: dark.

At some point, the sun moves.

I don’t see it trace its path across the warped shutters, don’t watch the dust change its dance. I only know that when I blink awake later—abrupt, alert, the way predators do when a sound changes—the light in the room has shifted to a softer, slanting gold.

My head is still pillowed against hers.

Her hand is still on my side.

Her fingers have curled in, loosely, holding fabric instead of skin, but the weight is there, a reminder, an anchor.

I am on my side, facing her, knees tangled with hers in a way that would have had the temple donors positively giddy if they’d paid for such a tableau.

No one paid.

We chose.

My body feels… different.

Not the tight, brittle edge of post-fight, where every tendon is strung and every nerve buzzing. Not the sluggish ache of hungover temple mornings. Just… rested. As if some part of me I’d forgotten needed tending has been very gently set down and told you can stop now.

Lysa’s eyes are open.

She’s watching me with that quiet, assessing gaze that first made me want to both flirt with her and bite her when we met. 

“How long,” I croak.

“A couple of hours,” she says. “You made an extremely undignified snore at one point, but I decided not to record it for the Tribunal.”

“Cruel,” I murmur. “To deny them such vital evidence.”

Her mouth twitches.

“You slept,” she says.

It’s not a question.

“So did you,” I point out.

“Yes,” she says. “But I didn’t think that was in doubt.”

I search her face for any hint of regret.

I find none.

“You didn’t… do anything,” I say slowly. “While I was out.”

It’s a stupid thing to say. Childish. As if she’d pull a ledger from under the mattress and start taking notes on the lines of my throat while I was unconscious.

Her brows tilt.

“I could have stabbed you,” she agrees. “Or counted your teeth. Or tried random syllables to see if any of them turned you into a puppet.”

“Tempting,” I say.

“Very,” she agrees. “I settled for sleeping. And not letting you fall off the bed when you sprawled.”

“I don’t sprawl,” I object.

She glances meaningfully at the way my leg is currently hooked over hers.

I consider the evidence.

“All right,” I concede. “Occasionally, under extreme duress, I… extend.”

“That’s one way to put it,” she says.

Her hand pats my side once, light, then withdraws.

The absence is sudden.

Not painful. Just… notable.

She sits up, stretching, hair coming more loose from its already-losing battle with its tie.

“Sun’s moving,” she says, glancing at the slant of light. “We should eat before we go. And you—” she points at me, precise “—will drink something with iron in it that does not come from a donor banquet’s decorative fountain.” 

“Yes, Investigator,” I say.

It comes out without the old sarcasm. Almost fond.

She notices.

Her eyes soften.

“Cyran,” she says.

“Yes.”

“Thank you,” she says quietly. “For trusting me with this.”

I blink.

“Trusting you with… what?” I ask.

“Your sleep,” she says simply. “Your back. Your… quiet.”

I look at her.

I think of the Gala, of being paraded and displayed. Of the temple, of pedestals and command words and the way my body was never mine. Of last night, gold and soap and her kiss on my forehead. Of this afternoon, her hand on my back and my own idiotic, miraculous ability to close my eyes. 

“You keep expecting cruelty,” she told me once. “I’m not cruel. I’m careful.” 

I believed her enough to sleep.

“Thank you,” I say back.

For once, I don’t try to make it clever.

We rise.

The bed is just furs and a thin mattress again, not an altar, not a coffin, not a cloud. But something in me has redrawn its edges.

Tonight, we go down into the bones of a god to steal the words that would make me a thing.

For the first time, I think we might actually walk back out.

Not because I am sharp.

Not because she is righteous.

Because, somewhere between the burnt coffee and the dust motes and the fur under my cheek, she made this ruin of a safe house into something I have never had.

A place where I can shut my eyes and still wake up myself.
