# Chapter 39 — *Domestic Grace*
### Cyran | POV

The whiskey tastes cheap and burns like heaven.

Which is to say: it’s real.

No temple nectar, no sanctified blood‑wine poured into crystal goblets by hands that expected you to bleed for the refill. Just gritty, honest liquor in a scuffed glass that Cairn hasn’t polished since the last bar fight chipped it.

I tip it back.

Let the heat slide down my throat, sit warm in my chest.

Beside me, Lysa holds her own glass with far more restraint. She sniffs it, tastes a careful sip, and gives Cairn a look that could be entered into evidence as scathing.

“This could strip ink,” she says.

Cairn leans his elbows on the bar, all bulk and black fur and casual menace. His amber eyes glint in the Hollow King’s stained glow.

“That’s the point,” he grunts.

“House special. Cleans out pipes. Wipes away bad nights.”

“We are celebrating, not committing chemical warfare on my taste buds,” she replies.

But the corner of her mouth is doing that thing it does when she’s secretly amused and doesn’t want to encourage us.

I swirl my glass.

Amber liquid, cheap and honest, catching the flicker of the bone‑lamps overhead. The Hollow King is all shadows and neon glyphs tonight: sigils stuttering blue and purple over the bar, throwing dancing light over faces, over smoke, over the worn grain of wood and bone.

I used to watch this room from the rafters.

Literally.

Perched up in the Shadow‑beams with a nice safe distance between me and all the warm bodies below, counting exits and weighing everyone’s blood like numbers in a ledger. Easy to play monster when you’re looking down on people from the dark.

The view is better from a stool.

From here, the bar is a battlefield of spilled drinks and elbows and half‑heard gossip. Laughter rolls in waves. Someone’s playing a lazy jazz‑blues riff in the corner on an old bone‑fret lute, the notes threading through the low murmur of conversation and the hiss of poured drinks.

Behind it all, there’s a hum I wasn’t sure I’d ever feel in this city:

Relief.

The kind that comes after a storm you didn’t expect to survive.

Cairn slides a second glass in front of me without asking.

“On the house,” he says.

“Try not to die again. I just got the good customers used to you.”

“Your concern is touching,” I say.

I raise my freshly filled glass.

“To survival,” I offer.

He eyes me.

Then, with a grunt that almost counts as approval, he lifts his own mug.

“To better uses for sharp things,” he says.

He clinks his cup against mine.

Lysa hesitates.

Then she raises her drink too.

“To accurate record‑keeping,” she says.

Cairn snorts, and I laugh so hard I almost inhale whiskey.

“Gods preserve us,” I cough.

“Only you would toast archival integrity at a bar, love.”

“You’re welcome,” she says.

The word *love* still sits strange and perfect in my mouth when I aim it at her like this.

No armor.

No deflection.

Just fact.

She pretends not to notice, but the tips of her ears go a little pink in the Hollow King’s shifting light.

The bar is crowded tonight.

Smugglers fresh from the Vein Markets.

Vigil wardens who haven’t yet figured out what to do with themselves now that the Prime Contract’s stranglehold has loosened.

Former Temple donors who’ve swapped incense for cheap perfume and relief etched into the lines around their mouths.

I recognize too many faces.

Old clients.

Old marks.

People I’ve saved, people I’ve hurt, people I’ve pretended not to see.

The difference, tonight, is that when eyes land on me, there isn’t that same flinch, that quick little tally of “asset, threat, owned.”

There’s curiosity.

Wariness.

The occasional nod.

Warden Kerrin raises his mug from a corner table.

I give him a small, theatrical bow from my stool.

He rolls his eyes and goes back to losing at cards.

“Famous, are we?” Lysa murmurs, leaning in so I can hear her over the room.

Her shoulder brushes mine. She smells like ink and smoke and the faint, herbal tang of the tea Cairn insists is good for her nerves.

“For tonight,” I say.

“Tomorrow they’ll find a new disaster to gossip about. The city’s attention span is merciful.”

“Mm,” she says.

Her thigh presses against mine under the bar.

She hasn’t moved away since we sat.

I can feel every subtle shift of muscle when she crosses her ankles, when she shifts her weight on the stool.

I am… obscenely grateful for the excuse to sit down.

The Heart‑Chamber and its subsequent theatrics left their marks. The burns along my hands and chest have mostly healed, courtesy of Onas and a cocktail of unpleasant salves, but there’s a deep ache in my bones that insists on reminding me I was recently used as divine kindling.

Cairn notices the wince when I roll my shoulders.

“You could’ve stayed home,” he grumbles, polishing a glass with a rag that might actually make it dirtier.

“And miss your charming bedside manner?” I say.

“Never.”

He grunts.

“You’re not special tonight,” he says.

“Everyone gets a free round. City didn’t implode. Gotta acknowledge small miracles.”

I glance around.

He’s right.

Glasses gleam on every table, in every fist.

Some raised.

Some clutched like lifelines.

The Hollow King has always been neutral ground. Tonight, it feels like it’s trying on something new—half tavern, half… sanctuary.

I liked it better when it was just a good place to get drunk and make bad decisions.

I like this more.

A low, impatient whistle cuts through the murmur.

Cairn’s head jerks toward the stage.

“Right on cue,” he mutters.

He thumps his rag over his shoulder and raises his voice.

“All right, you degenerates,” he calls.

“Drink quiet for five minutes. I paid good coin for this one to drown out your whining.”

He doesn’t mean it.

Not entirely.

But the crowd reacts as if he’s just promised free rounds for life.

Cheers.

Catcalls.

A few heartfelt groans from the men at the card table who know better than to talk through a performance and risk Cairn’s wrath.

The lights over the small raised platform at the far end of the room flare.

Someone tweaks a glyph.

The bone‑lamps there shift from warm gold to a cooler, bruised‑violet hue, painting the stage in theatrical shadows.

Then he steps into them.

Seraph.

I’ve heard the name murmured in Vein alleys and whispered in Tribunal margins.

Grace‑den star.

Maestro’s favorite toy.

The kind of performer you don’t just watch; you consume.

Seeing him under Cairn’s lights is… something else.

He’s all sharp lines and soft glamor—a cascade of white‑gold feathers spilling from his shoulders, catching the purple light, glitter dusted along high cheekbones, lips painted the color of fresh bruise. His hair falls in messy, artful curls around his face, dyed the soft pink of old scar tissue.

His clothes aren’t much—tight black trousers, a gauzy shirt open too far down his chest—but they’re arranged with surgical precision to draw the eye.

There’s a collar at his throat.

Most people would miss the sigil worked in subtle, oily sheen into the metal.

I don’t.

Maestro’s mark.

Contract glyph masquerading as fashion.

My hands curl around my glass.

“Feathers,” Lysa murmurs.

Her tone is analytical, not admiring.

“Impractical in a fight.”

“You say that like he’s expected to fight,” I say.

She goes quiet.

Seraph takes the mic stand like it’s an old lover—one hand wrapped loose around it, body angled just so, eyes lowered under thick lashes as if the contact is almost… too much.

Then he looks up.

The room exhales.

He opens his mouth.

The first note is sugar.

Bright, sweet, spun so fine it almost disappears as it threads through the smoky air.

The second has glass at its center.

The kind you only notice when you swallow it and realize, too late, you’re bleeding from somewhere you can’t name.

The song is ostensibly upbeat, something with a backroom tap and a lazy swing to it that makes a few drunks at the front start nodding in time.

But beneath the tempo, the melody arches and curls around a sadness that makes my burned bones ache in sympathy.

Lysa leans forward, forearms on the bar, eyes locked on Seraph’s face.

“He’s hurting,” she says quietly.

“Of course he is,” I reply.

“Look at him. He’s beautiful.”

“That’s not what I—” she begins.

Then she catches herself.

Sighs.

“Yes,” she says.

“That too.”

He moves like he’s weightless.

Not in the way I do, bone‑light and predatory, but with a sort of desperate grace, as if every flick of wrist and shake of hip is choreographed down to the tendon. Each gesture screams I am fine, I am pretty, I am exactly the fantasy you paid for.

His eyes do not match.

They are black as wet ink under the glitter.

Dead‑tired.

Watching the crowd with a detached calculation that makes my skin crawl with unwelcome recognition.

Property eyes.

I drink.

The whiskey doesn’t help.

By the time he hits the last note, holding it just a fraction longer than necessary, letting it crack perfectly on the edge of his range, the Hollow King is on its feet.

Applause.

Whistles.

Someone throws a handful of coin onto the stage.

He bends his head, lets the noise wash over him.

There’s no joy in it.

Only relief.

Like a man who’s finished a particularly complex piece of surgery on himself and managed not to die.

He steps off the stage into the shadows at the side.

The music from the corner musician swells again, filling the space between sets.

I set my glass down.

Harder than intended.

“Cyran,” Lysa says.

Warning.

Question.

We’ve had this conversation before, in different rooms, about different people.

I stare at the space where Seraph disappeared.

“He has a collar,” I say.

“So did you,” she says.

“Past tense,” I reply.

She nods.

Slow.

“Can you save everyone?” she asks.

Quiet.

Precise.

Cutting through instinct.

“No,” I admit.

I don’t try to make it a joke.

“But I can tell him there’s a lock.”

“And that it opens?” she says.

Her tone is skeptical, but her eyes are soft.

I hold her gaze.

“Yes,” I say.

“Because I’m sitting in your bar, darling, drinking terrible whiskey and making you suffer my presence without anyone shining a sigil at my throat.”

She snorts.

“Don’t remind me,” she mutters.

But her hand finds my knee under the bar.

Squeezes.

“Go,” she says.

“Before you start brooding enough to curdle Cairn’s entire inventory.”

“How will you cope without my charm to defend you from predatory bartenders?” I ask.

Cairn snorts.

“If anyone here needs defending,” he says, “it’s him.”

He jerks his chin at me.

“From himself.”

“Noted,” I say.

I slide off the stool.

My body protests.

I ignore it.

Old instincts have me cataloging the room as I move—who’s watching, who’s drunk enough not to remember this conversation, who’d sell Maestro information for a few coins.

I pick a path that’s obvious enough not to read as sneaking.

Seraph reappears near the end of the bar, stage‑makeup slightly smudged, a thin sheen of sweat on his throat catching the light. Up close, he’s smaller than he looks under the glow, bones finer, wrists too thin where feathers end and skin begins.

He walks like his knees have learned the exact measure of the stage and resent any floor that doesn’t match it.

As he passes, the crowd parts for him automatically.

Deference.

Hunger.

Resentment.

He wears all of it like a second costume.

When he draws level with me, his gaze flicks up.

He does a double take.

Well.

That’s flattering.

Up close, I suppose I do cut a figure—burns faded to interesting scars, shirt open at the throat just enough to suggest, casually, that I own this skin now.

“Handsome,” he purrs.

The word is drawn out, dipped in honey and smoke.

“Testing the merchandise? I do specials for war heroes.”

He leans in as he says it, hand lifting toward my chest, fingers poised to trace old sigils, to see which ones light.

Once, I would have let him.

Once, I might have encouraged it.

Take the pretty thing that’s offered, make a joke of it, drown in the familiar currency of being wanted for my body and nothing else.

I catch his wrist.

Gently.

Not hard enough to restrain.

Just enough to redirect.

His pulse leaps under my fingers.

He goes still.

The smile on his painted mouth doesn’t change.

His eyes do.

Wariness trickles in.

“Careful,” he says, lighter than the situation warrants.

“Maestro doesn’t like damaged goods.”

“I’m retired,” I say.

I let the word sit between us.

Watch the way his pupils dilate around it.

“Lucky you,” he says.

The bitterness in it scrapes my bones like old chains.

“Not luck,” I say.

“Work.”

His eyes flick to the faint outline of Tribunal stamps half‑erased on my hands.

To the scar just visible at my collar where the Vigil mark used to flare at any command.

“A different kind of cage,” he says.

“Congratulations. You changed owners.”

“No,” I say.

Firmer.

I loosen my hold on his wrist.

Leave my hand hovering, an offer, not a grip.

“Read the fine print,” I add.

“The Temple’s gone from my ledger. The Vigil’s marks are fading. The Prime Contract’s node is dust. There’s ink in my favor now. Not theirs.”

He stares.

Skeptical.

“Good for you,” he says.

“For some of us, the walls aren’t optional decor.”

His fingers flick the Maestro sigil where it lies snug around his throat.

The glyph there shivers at the contact, like something waking.

My stomach turns.

“I know,” I say.

I keep my voice low, so it doesn’t carry over the bar noise.

“I’m not here to buy you. Or pity you. Or offer you some noble, doomed rescue that ends with my pretty head on a Maestro’s wall.”

He studies me.

Slow.

The noise of the Hollow King fades to a low, indistinct thrum around us.

“Then what are you selling?” he asks.

When in doubt, always ask that.

He’s good.

“Nothing,” I say.

“For once.”

I let myself smile.

Not the full, bladed thing.

A smaller, tired curve.

“There’s a way out,” I tell him.

“Not easy. Not quick. Not clean. But real.”

“For you, maybe,” he says.

His eyes slide toward the stage, where the next act is setting up—two bone‑drummers arguing quietly over tempo.

“For the pretty ones the city suddenly decides are worth keeping.”

“Seraph,” I say.

He flinches at the sound of his own name in my mouth.

Interesting.

“I never lie about cages,” I continue.

“Because I spent too long convincing myself mine was made of something nicer than bone.”

He swallows.

The movement makes the sigil at his throat pulse, a faint, ugly oil‑sheen reacting to his blood.

I lower my voice further.

“Your Maestro,” I say.

“Does he sign his own contracts, or does he hide them in other people’s skin?”

His eyes sharpen.

“Careful,” he says again.

There’s real warning in it this time.

“Men who ask questions like that don’t have long, happy lives.”

“I was never promised long,” I say.

“Happy is… under review. Surprisingly promising.” I tilt my head toward the bar, where Lysa sits, watching us, calm and clinical and quietly furious on behalf of every person she’s ever seen used like this.

Cairn is behind her, polishing another glass into oblivion, wings half‑unfurled, gaze bouncing between us and the rest of the room.

“You see them?” I ask.

“Woman with the ink, man with the wings?”

Seraph’s gaze flicks over my shoulder.

Returns.

Slow.

Suspicious.

“Yes,” he says.

“Tribunal mouse and grumpy gargoyle.”

I huff.

Accurate.

“They helped me break my chains,” I say simply.

“And they’re very bored when they don’t have something impossible to fix.”

“I am not a charity case,” he snaps.

There.

Heat.

Good.

“I didn’t say you were,” I say.

“I said you’re a performer in a gilded cage. And I happen to know a few people who are getting very good at picking locks. Legal ones. Magical ones.”

He stares.

Long enough that the noise of the bar creeps back in at the edges—a clink of glass, a laugh, the low thunk of Cairn’s tail against chair legs as he pretends not to eavesdrop.

“Don’t lie to me,” Seraph says finally.

Soft.

Raw.

“I have enough men paying for that privilege already.”

The words land like bruises.

“I lie about a great many things,” I say.

“Names. Fees. Whether that color suits someone. But not this. Not anymore.”

I release his wrist entirely.

Let my hand fall to my side.

“If you want out,” I say, “find the Hollow King on a quiet night. Ask for Cairn. Tell him you’re ready to talk terms that don’t end with you on an altar.”

He swallows again.

His tongue flicks out—habitual, nervous—catching a smear of lipstick at the corner of his mouth.

“You going to be there?” he asks.

There’s something in it that isn’t flirtation.

Hope, maybe.

Hate, maybe.

They feel similar at first.

“Sometimes,” I say.

“But even if I’m not, they will be. And they’re much scarier than me.”

Lysa would be insulted by that.

Cairn would be proud.

He studies my face like it’s a contract.

Looking for loopholes.

Looking for the catch.

“In my experience,” he says slowly, “ways out always cost more than the cage.”

“Yes,” I say.

“They do. You pay in scars and time and ugly truths. You pay in sleepless nights and blood and the slow, horrible realization that you were not, in fact, born to be someone’s toy. It’s expensive.”

He smirks, a brittle, self‑aware twist.

“Sold,” he says.

Then the smirk cracks, and for half a second I see the boy under all that glitter, staring out from behind a Maestro’s sigil and wondering if he dares move when the door’s left ajar.

“Think about it,” I say.

“No rush. Your captor isn’t going anywhere. Men like that never believe the house can burn down around them.”

His eyes spark.

“Sounds like you’re speaking from experience,” he says.

“Oh, sweetheart,” I say.

“You have no idea.”

Something in him shifts.

Not enough to be a revolution.

But enough.

A breath drawn less shallow.

A line in his shoulders easing.

“All right,” he says.

“I’ll… think about it.”

He glances toward the stage, where Cairn’s tapping his claw against the floor pointedly, gesture that means break’s over, back to work.

“Duty calls,” Seraph says.

He takes a step away.

Stops.

Looks back at me.

“You really retired?” he asks.

“From the entertaining, I mean. Seems a waste.”

The old Cyran would have had a dozen answers, each more lewd than the last.

I let myself grin.

But I shake my head.

“I’m taken,” I say.

Simple.

No performance.

He blinks.

Once.

Twice.

His gaze flicks to the bar.

To Lysa, who, to her credit, doesn’t look away when his eyes hit her. She just raises her glass slightly in an almost‑salute, as if to say: I see you. I see him. I see the shape of this story.

“Lucky you,” Seraph murmurs.

It doesn’t sound bitter this time.

“Not luck,” I say again.

“Work.”

He inhales.

Nods.

There is, impossibly, the faintest hint of a spring in his step as he climbs back onto the stage.

The lights flare violet.

He takes the mic.

Looks at me.

Sings.

The next song is sharper.

Still sad.

But under the sugar and glass, there’s a thin thread of steel that wasn’t there before.

A promise.

Or maybe I’m just romantic enough now to hear it.

When I make my way back to the bar, Lysa is waiting with that look.

The one that says she already knows what I did and is compiling objections and commendations in equal measure.

“Well?” she asks.

“Well,” I echo.

“He’s tragic. He’s glam. He’s in a very stupid amount of danger. I like him.”

“Of course you do,” she says.

“You have a type.”

“Pretty and doomed?” I ask.

She arches a brow.

“Pretty and caged,” she corrects.

“Ex‑caged,” I say.

“I prefer my companions at least halfway out of their shackles.”

She brushes her thumb over the back of my hand where it rests on the bar.

“No one’s ever entirely out,” she says.

“Not while the system still exists. But we’re… making progress.”

“Hmm,” I say.

“You’re a bad influence, Auditor.”

“Am I?” she asks.

Her smile is small.

Fierce.

“Yes,” I say.

“You make me want to save people I don’t even know yet.”

“Good,” she says.

“We’ll need that.”

On stage, Seraph hits a note that would shatter glass if Cairn hadn’t reinforced half the barware with subtle bone‑runes. The room roars. Coins clatter onto the stage again.

Cairn slides another drink toward me.

“On the house,” he repeats.

“This one’s for sticking your nose into Maestro’s business. If he kills you, I want it on record that I fueled at least one of your last good nights.”

“You have no faith in my survival skills,” I say.

He snorts.

“I’ve seen your survival skills,” he replies.

“And yet, here we are.”

“Here we are,” I agree.

I pick up the glass.

Turn to Lysa.

“Last toast?” I ask.

“For this book, at least.”

She frowns slightly.

“Book?”

“Section,” I amend.

“Chapter. Miserable, bloody, occasionally sexy era of our lives.”

Her lips twitch.

“Fine,” she says.

“To…?”

I think about Bone Walls and broken contracts.

About god‑hearts and burnt stew and the way her hand feels in mine when there’s no altar waiting for us.

To the singer on stage whose collar still gleams with someone else’s ownership.

To the people we haven’t met yet, under different ribs, living different flavors of the same cage.

“To bad influences,” I say.

She snorts.

Raises her glass.

“To stubborn survivors,” she counters.

Cairn grunts.

Lifts his mug.

“To idiots who think they can fix the world,” he adds.

We clink.

Drink.

For tonight, I let myself be… just this.

A man on a bar stool in the Vein, shoulder to shoulder with the woman who voided his property stamp, trading insults with a chimera who pretends not to care, listening to a caged bird sing about breaking.

Tomorrow, there will be laws to write.

Systems to dismantle.

Locks to pick.

Contracts to burn.

There will be Maestro and his kind, and Seraph and theirs.

There will be work.

But tonight?

Tonight there’s cheap whiskey that burns like heaven.

There’s Lysa’s thigh pressed warm against mine under the bar.

There’s a song in the air that sounds, for the first time in centuries, like possibility.

And for once, I’m not the one bleeding for it.

I’m just… here.

Alive.

Listening.

Planning the next impossible thing with a smile on my face and ash finally shaken from my shoulders.

The Damned stick together.

We always have.

We just finally remember what for.
