# Chapter 19 — *The Maestro's Debt*
### Cyran | POV

I’d rather bargain with a demon. Demons have clearer contracts.

The alley outside the Hollow Archive still smells like marrow-fog and panic when I say it.

We’re half-sprawled in the muck, backs to damp bone, lungs burning from the sprint and the climb. My coat is full of Archive dust, my hands are full of tiny cuts, and my skull is full of six hateful words. 

Obedience is the only Virtue.

They sit behind my eyes like someone carved them there with a dull knife. Every time I blink, I see the red ink, the neat little halo of sigils, the way the letters bubbled when Lysa poured acid on them and the book screamed. 

Subject 9: SUCCESS.

Me.

Of course I’m a success. Nothing like being a perfected recipe to make a man feel special. 

Lysa pushes herself upright beside me, wincing as her spine meets the alley wall.

“We need to move,” she says. “Before someone glances up, sees the broken vent, and decides to investigate why the Archive is sulking.” 

“Let them,” I mutter. “I hope the entire Order gets a splitting headache from that little tantrum.”

She gives me a look.

Not unamused. Just… focused.

She’s pale under the grime, jaw tight, eyes doing that sharp, assessing flick they do when she’s mentally sorting information into piles: urgent, later, evidence. There is a new pile now. Inconvenient blasphemies. 

“The Key exists,” she says quietly. “Thessen has a copy. We know that much.” 

“And we know the phrase,” I answer.

I don’t repeat it.

Saying it aloud in here, in the city’s ribs, feels like inviting it to try its luck.

Her hand twitches as if she wants to cover my mouth for good measure.

She doesn’t.

“We destroyed the original,” she reminds me.

“Yes,” I say. “I’m sure the Archive will compose a mournful hymn about its loss.”

“Cyran.”

There it is again—my name like a line drawn.

“We hit one of his foundations,” she says. “That matters. But he still has a copy. That means the question isn’t if he uses it. It’s when.”

The word hangs between us.

When.

Once upon a time, I didn’t plan beyond the next performance, the next ritual, the next set of orders. Then I started counting in Redemption Marks and rotation schedules. Now I’m apparently on a clock I can’t see, waiting for a rich man with a stolen word to decide when my spine stops belonging to me.

“I always did hate surprises,” I say lightly.

“Then let’s take this one away from him,” she says. “We need his timeline. His next move. Before he makes it.”

“And how, darling Scribe,” I ask, “do you propose we divine that? Cast bones? Ask the god’s corpse nicely?”

“I’d prefer something more reliable than prayer,” she says dryly. “You said once there are people who traffic in information the Tribunal doesn’t see.”

I blink.

“Oh, no,” I say. “Absolutely not.”

She arches a brow.

“You’d rather go into tomorrow blind?”

“I’d rather be flayed again,” I say. “At least that’s an honest pain.”

Her expression doesn’t change.

She just waits.

I hate that she’s learned that trick from me.

“There is… someone,” I admit.

The word tastes like old wine left too long in a barrel. Sour and expensive.

“An information broker,” she says.

“That’s a generous term.”

“What would you call him?”

“A bored god with a conductor’s baton and far too much time on his hands,” I say. “He doesn’t worship power, he arranges it. For a price.”

Her eyes narrow, calculating.

“What’s his name?” she asks.

“The Maestro,” I say.

Lysa goes very still.

“I’ve heard that name,” she says slowly. “In a Vigil report. And I saw him at a distance once. In the Spine. A private club with bone chandeliers and too much velvet.”

“Ah,” I say. “Yes. That would be him, showing off.”

I push myself to my feet.

My knees complain; my ribs add their own chorus. The sigil-burn on my sleeve from the construct’s near miss throbs in time with my heart. Everything hurts. 

I’m alive.

I’m still myself.

Thessen doesn’t get to know when I stop being either before I do.

I hold out a hand to Lysa.

She takes it.

Her palm is rough with ink-calluses and fresh Archive cuts. Her grip is steady.

“The Maestro keeps a music room behind his club,” I say. “He’ll know what Thessen’s planning. He knows everything.”

“And his price?” she asks.

“Always the same,” I say. “Debt. Nothing so mundane as coin. He prefers promises.”

“That’s a problem,” she says immediately.

“Welcome,” I reply, “to my old life.”

She gives me a look that says, quite clearly, I am too tired for this brand of fatalism.

“We go,” she says. “We listen. We negotiate. We do not sign away anything you can’t afford to lose.”

“Darling,” I say, as we slip out of the alley and back into the sleeping veins of the city, “at this point my future is one long list of things I can’t afford to lose. Let’s see how much credit he’s still willing to extend.”

***

The Maestro’s club is in the Spine, of course.

Where else would you put a place like that but in the god’s vertebrae, balanced between skull and heart like a knife edge? The district doesn’t sleep, but it pretends to—alleys shuttered, lanterns turned down, courtesans and cardsharps on quiet shift, gauging whether the late-night trade will outweigh the risk of being caught in whatever trouble rolls downhill from the rich bones above. 

From the street, the club is just another layered façade: bone columns carved into fluted spirals, velvet drapes spilling from upper balconies, soft light leaking out between ribs, too carefully curated to be anything but a stage. You go in through the front door if you want to be seen. 

We are not here for the floor show.

We circle around the back, to the service alley that smells of spilled wine, sweat, and the sour tang of rosin—the powder musicians use to make their bows bite. 

A single door sits recessed into the spine-bone, unmarked.

I rap three times.

A little slot nowhere near eye level slides open.

“Closed,” a bored voice says.

“It’s me,” I answer.

There’s a pause.

“State your scale,” the voice intones, ritual words wrapped in boredom.

“Minor,” I say. “Always.”

Another pause.

“Off-key, more like,” the voice mutters, and the bolts slide back.

The door opens onto a narrow corridor lined in faded velvet.

The sound hits first.

No tavern din. No laughter, no clink of glasses, no off-tempo clapping.

Music.

Strings and brass and something like a pipe-organ dragged through a war, all layered on top of each other in a dissonant, controlled chaos. It stops and starts in jagged phrases, as if an invisible hand is cutting it apart with a blade. 

“Stay close,” I tell Lysa.

“I’m not wandering off to hum along,” she says.

The corridor spills us into the Maestro’s music room.

I always forget how big it is.

The ceiling disappears into shadow, high enough that the light from the hovering lamps can’t quite reach it. The walls are lined in shelves—of course they are—holding instruments instead of books: violins, violas, cellos, flutes and drums, things with too many strings, things that look like someone took a harp and stretched it inside-out. 

None of them are being played.

They’re all playing themselves.

Bows move on their own, drawing shrieks and sighs from violin strings. Drumheads thrum as if struck by invisible hands. A bone flute spins lazily in midair, exhaling a thin, reedy line of melody that never quite resolves. The air smells of varnish, rosin, and the cold dust of disused seats in old concert halls. 

In the center of it all, with his back to us, stands the Maestro.

He wears black.

Not Vigil black—theirs is practical, built for blood and shadows. His is theatrical, tailored within an inch of its life, long coat flaring just so when he moves. A white shirt cuffs at his wrists like a concession to civility, hands pale in the dim light. 

His head is bare.

Instead of hair, he wears a mask.

It’s porcelain, or bone made to look like porcelain—smooth, featureless save for the suggestion of cheekbones, a hint of nose, and a mouth drawn in a permanent, faintly amused line. Where eyes should be, there are just two narrow, dark slits.

He conducts.

No baton.

Just his hands, carving through the air in precise, economical arcs. When he sweeps up, the strings surge. When he cuts sideways, the drums choke off mid-beat. When he closes his fist, two flutes fall silent as if throttled.

He does not turn when we enter.

“You’re off-key, Cyran,” he says, conversationally.

His voice is smooth and pitched like a well-tuned instrument—no accent, no roughness. If voices could wear velvet, his would.

“I’ve had a long night,” I reply. “Forgive me for not arriving perfectly in tune.”

“Tsk,” he says.

His fingers flick.

The whole cacophony grinds to a halt at once.

Silence slams down.

Sudden, absolute.

My ears ring with the absence.

“I prefer my surprises harmonized,” he continues, still not looking back. “But I suppose one must take what the gods’ corpse sends.”

Beside me, Lysa has gone very still.

I can feel her dislike gathering like static in the air.

“Maestro,” I say, making a little half-bow he can feel even if he can’t see. “Forgive the intrusion. I’m—”

“In a hurry,” he says, finally turning.

The mask gives him an inhuman calm.

My brain insists on trying to animate it, to find eyes, a twitch of lip, some sign of expression. It finds nothing. The only clues are in his posture, in the angle of his head, in the way his fingers never quite stop moving, as if they’re counting a beat only he can hear. 

He looks straight at me.

Or at least, the dark slits in the mask angle in my direction.

“Thessen, then,” he says. “He’s been so very loud lately. Buying things he shouldn’t. Parading assets.” The mask tilts, infinitesimally. “Ruining the acoustics with his ego.”

“He’s planning a spectacle,” I say. “We’d like to know when.”

“We?” the Maestro echoes.

Only now does he acknowledge Lysa, as if she appeared between his last breath and this one.

His head turns.

“Ah,” he says, drawing the syllable out like a note. “The Scribe.”

Lysa’s shoulders square.

I don’t think she likes that any more than I like being called ‘asset,’ but she doesn’t give him the satisfaction of flinching.

“You heard of me,” she says, voice clipped.

“Everyone heard of you,” he croons. “Standing between our dear Relic and a priest at the gala, quill at the ready, spine so straight you put the god’s own to shame. Very brave. Very foolish.” 

“Occupational hazard,” she replies.

He laughs softly.

“A Tribunal investigator who doesn’t like the taste of leashes,” he muses. “How novel.”

“Maestro,” I cut in. “I’m not here for your reviews.”

“No,” he agrees. “You’re here because you don’t know when Thessen plans to pull your string, and that frightens you more than the fact that he has it.” 

The words land like fingertips on my throat.

I make myself not touch the spot.

Lysa’s hand twitches.

She manages not to touch me, but I feel the aborted motion like a ghost.

“You know why we’re here,” she says. “Which means you know what we need.”

“Oh, certainly,” he says. “I know many things. Why, just tonight, I learned that the Hollow Archive can still scream.” 

He looks delighted.

It makes my skin crawl.

“Very rude, by the way,” he continues. “Do you have any idea how much work went into curating those blasphemies? And you simply… tore a page out.”

“Consider it an edit,” Lysa says.

He inclines his head a fraction.

“I do admire an editor with convictions,” he says.

“The time, Maestro,” I say.

He sighs theatrically.

“You rush art, Cyran,” he chides. “It wilts. But very well. Thessen plans to debut his little toy at the Great Pulse tomorrow night. Temple of the Heart. Midnight.” 

Great Pulse.

Of course.

Once a year, the dead god’s heart still remembers how to beat properly, every vein and artery in the city humming in sync for one perfect hour. The faithful call it a miracle. The cynical call it a relic of an extinct physiology. 

Thessen, apparently, calls it free staging.

“He needs a spectacle,” the Maestro goes on. “Something grand to bind his investors. What better way to demonstrate control than to pull the string of his favourite marionette in front of half the city’s elite?”

My stomach drops.

The image is easy to conjure: the Heart Temple thrumming, the Great Pulse reverberating through bone, and me, in the center, turned into an obedient weapon with one red phrase.

“Why?” Lysa asks.

The Maestro tilts his head at her, as if amused that she dared.

“He wants leverage,” she says. “He already has power. What does he gain?”

“Ah,” the Maestro breathes. “A mind that asks useful questions. How refreshing.”

He lifts one hand.

Around us, a violin twitches in midair, plucks a single, eerie note.

“Thessen is ambitious,” he says. “But he is not… creative. He is, in fact, employed. The Prime Contract needs proof of concept. A demonstration that their investments yield obedient gods on a leash. Thessen wants to be the one who presents the first fully‑functional model.” 

“There are more like me?” I hear myself ask.

“Potentially,” he says. “Or there will be. You’re the prototype. Subject 9. Success.” 

The number makes me want to break things.

I keep my hands at my sides.

“And you,” Lysa says slowly, “are telling us this out of the goodness of your heart.”

The Maestro laughs.

It’s a graceful sound, but there’s nothing warm in it.

“Oh, my dear,” he says. “I haven’t had a heart in years. It kept getting in the way.” He turns his masked face toward me again. “I am telling you this because watching two systems devour each other is infinitely more entertaining than watching one run smoothly. And because I enjoy your voice, Cyran.”

There it is.

The price, humming just beneath the surface.

“The time and place,” I say. “That’s all we need. We’ll take care of the rest.”

“Mmm,” he says. “Will you?”

His fingers dance, a lazy little flick of wrist, and one of the cellos glides forward, bow scratching out a few low, ominous notes.

“The price,” I say, because you might as well lance the wound yourself.

“Always so eager to bleed for others,” he muses. “It’s endearing.”

“The price,” I repeat.

He waves a hand.

“I have enough money,” he says. “Enough favours of the mundane kind. Petty debts bore me. I want… art.”

Lysa steps in front of me before I can open my mouth.

“Cash,” she says. “Or information of equal value. I can give you Tribunal schedules, donor lists, preliminary drafts of proposed contract reforms. No open-ended favours.” 

He regards her for a long, silent beat.

“You are delightful,” he says at last. “Like a blade pretending to be a quill. But no. I am not interested in your paperwork.” He looks back at me. “I want a song.”

I blink.

“Pardon?” I say.

“A song,” he repeats. “From your pretty throat, one day. When I call it in. No appeals. No substitutions. No humming a few bars and calling it done.” 

My mouth goes dry.

Of all the things he could have asked for—blood, body, another murder—this feels… worse.

Singing was one of the first cages.

Ritual chants on cold marble. Liturgies half in a language I didn’t understand, half in moans and sighs someone else composed for my mouth. Songs to invoke, to seduce, to smooth over the ugliness of what was being done. 

I don’t sing anymore.

I laugh. I flirt. I speak. I scream.

But I do not sing.

“No,” Lysa says, sharp as a snapped string.

The Maestro tuts.

“You bring such a charming moral compass with you these days, Cyran,” he says. “Does she know how often that needle used to point to self‑destruct?”

“More than you might think,” she says, not taking her eyes off him. “He’s not a jukebox you can kick when you’re bored.”

“On the contrary,” the Maestro says, amused. “He is precisely that. Or he was. I’m giving him a chance to make his own music for once.”

“That’s not what you’re doing,” she says quietly. “You’re putting a hook in him for later.”

He inclines his head.

“Of course I am,” he says. “I am an information broker, not a philanthropist.”

He looks back at me.

“Well?” he asks. “Do we have a bargain?”

Lysa’s hand clamps around my forearm.

“Cyran,” she says under her breath. “This is exactly the kind of open‑ended debt that got you chained to the Vigil in the first place.”

“I know,” I say.

“Then say no,” she presses. “We’ll find another way. There’s always another witness, another record, another—”

“We have less than twenty‑four hours,” I say.

The words come out less flippant than I intend.

“I can fight Thessen,” I continue. “I can out‑perform a greedy little priest with delusions of grandeur. I cannot fight a god‑corp with a Command Key and a schedule I don’t know.” I look at her. “We need the time.” 

“We already have it,” she says. “Midnight. Tomorrow. Great Pulse. Temple of the Heart. He gave us that before naming his price.” 

“Details,” the Maestro singsongs. “Details, details. You know roughly when the ax will fall, yes. But what of the rest? The route his investors take. The names on the guest list. The redundancies. The backup triggers. The escape hatches. These are not things you can guess, little Scribe. These are things you buy.” 

He’s right.

I hate that.

He’s always right about the economy of terror. He understands how people move when they think they’re safe, how they panic when they realize they’re not. He can orchestrate it all like a symphony.

If we go in blind, we might still pull it off.

We might also die.

Or worse, I might live with a different set of strings.

Lysa must see the calculation in my eyes, because her grip tightens.

“I won’t let you trade yourself away again,” she says, low.

The thing in my chest that used to be all sharp corners and hollow places does something fragile and painful at that.

“I’m not trading myself,” I say.

“Your voice is part of you,” she snaps. “Your consent. Your… choice.”

Her cheeks flush.

As if she’s said something indecent in a confessional.

The Maestro watches, rapt.

“Fascinating,” he murmurs. “Do go on.”

I ignore him.

“This is my choice,” I tell her. “That’s the point. The temple took my songs. The Vigil rented them out. This… I give. On my terms. Later. Not now.” I swallow. “If the price of keeping Thessen from puppeteering me in front of half the city is one performance for an insufferable conductor, I’ll pay it.”

“It won’t be just one performance,” she says.

“I know,” I say again. “And I will still be me when I take that breath, not whatever Thessen thinks he’s bought.”

She searches my face.

If there is pity there, I might break.

There isn’t.

Just fury.

And fear.

And something like… belief.

“Your choice,” she says finally, voice carved thin and precise. “But if he tries to turn that choice into a leash, I will cut his hands off.”

“Oh,” the Maestro says, delighted. “I do like her.”

I turn back to him.

“Done,” I say.

The word feels like stepping off a balcony.

His fingers still.

The room goes even quieter, somehow, though the instruments haven’t resumed.

“Inadequate,” he says mildly. “We need something a touch more formal, my dear.”

He extends a hand.

I stare at it.

Long fingers, pale, nails trimmed and clean. No visible marks of worship, no ink, no bone jewelry.

Just a hand.

No way that’s safe.

I take it anyway.

His grip is cool and dry.

“Say it,” he purrs. “So the walls can hear. So the bones remember.”

I clench my jaw.

“I, Cyran—” I begin.

He tuts.

“So serious,” he chides. “Try again without sounding as if someone is carving it into your spine.”

“That’s how it feels,” I say.

“Yes,” he says. “That’s how you know it’s real.”

I close my eyes.

“I owe you a song,” I say. “To be sung when you call for it. One day. No appeal. No substitution.”

“And in return,” he says, “I will give you Thessen’s full itinerary for tomorrow night, his investor list, and the name of the one holding his chain.” 

I open my eyes.

“Deal,” I say.

The room seems to exhale.

Above us, the instruments vibrate, one by one, as if some unseen bow has dragged itself across their strings.

“Wonderful,” the Maestro says, releasing my hand.

There is no mark.

No burn.

No flash of sigils.

Just the echo of his grip in my bones.

He moves to a low table at the side of the room and plucks up a sheaf of parchment. Of course he had it ready. He knew we’d come as soon as the Archive screamed. 

“Here,” he says, handing it to Lysa, not me.

She takes it with the wary care of someone accepting a venomous snake.

Names. Times. Routes. Security rotations. Investors’ symbols. All the terrible, useful things she craves.

“The Creditor,” I say. “You said someone holds Thessen’s chain. Who?”

The Maestro’s mask tips.

“You’ll meet them soon enough,” he says. “But since I enjoy a good prelude… The Creditor is watching, Cyran. Has been, for quite some time. They’re very interested in whether you’ll carve your own score or keep playing everyone else’s.”

A chill walks down my spine.

“Is that supposed to be encouraging?” I ask.

“It’s supposed to be entertaining,” he says. “For me.”

“You said Thessen is employed,” Lysa cuts in. “If we cut his leash, does the Creditor simply hire someone else?”

“Oh, undoubtedly,” the Maestro says, almost bored. “That’s the beauty of systems. Remove one note, the melody rearranges. But sometimes—” his head tilts “—a wrong note can change the whole piece.”

“I like being wrong,” I say. “Comes naturally.”

He laughs.

“Go, then,” he says. “Rehearse. Bleed. Fall in love. Try very hard not to die in a way that would bore me.”

He raises his hands.

The instruments surge back to life, a chaotic, eerie swell that makes my teeth ache.

We leave to a soundtrack of discord.

***

Outside, the Spine’s night air feels almost clean compared to the room we’ve left behind.

I lean against the alley wall, let my head thump back, close my eyes.

The world hums faintly with the Maestro’s music even out here.

I wonder if he ever really stops.

“You shouldn’t have promised him,” Lysa says.

There’s no heat in it now.

Just tired certainty.

“I know,” I say.

“Then why did you?”

I crack an eye open.

She’s standing a few feet away, parchment clutched in one hand, the other fisted at her side. Her jaw is set in that particular way that means she’s furious on my behalf and hasn’t found a productive outlet yet. 

“I would promise him my kidneys if it kept you safe,” I say lightly.

She does not laugh.

“Don’t joke about carving yourself up for me,” she says. “Other people have done enough of that.”

The words hit like a slap.

Deserved.

I straighten, let some of the theatrical slack fall away.

“Then let me try again,” I say.

She folds her arms.

“I don’t want you in debt to him,” she says. “Or to Thessen. Or to anyone who looks at you and sees leverage instead of a person.”

“That list is shrinking,” I point out.

“That’s not the point.”

“It is, a little,” I say. “Once, it was everyone. Now it’s… fewer.”

Her mouth does a strange, betrayed little twist that might be on its way to a smile.

I step closer.

Careful, as if approaching a skittish animal.

She doesn’t move back.

“I don’t want to belong to him,” I say. “Or Thessen. Or the Creditor. Or the Vigil. Or the temple. I am very tired of being someone’s favourite instrument. But I am willing to play one song on command if it means living long enough to decide what else I want to be.”

Her gaze flicks to my throat.

To my mouth.

Back to my eyes.

“And what do you want to be?” she asks quietly.

“A man,” I say.

The word feels ridiculous.

Small.

Enormous.

“At your side,” I add, because I might as well bleed thoroughly while I’m at it. “Not a god, not a weapon, not a spectacle. Just… me. Sharp and broken and learning. Choosing.”

The theatrical edge is gone from my voice.

It costs me.

I let her see that.

Lysa exhales slowly.

“You don’t have to trade your future for that,” she says. “You know that, right? You don’t owe me a song. Or a life.”

“No,” I say. “I don’t. I choose.”

She studies me for a long moment.

Then she reaches out and takes my hand.

Not my wrist.

Not my throat.

My hand.

“Fine,” she says. “We’ll fight a god‑corp, a greedy priest, and a bored conductor on less than twenty‑four hours of planning and one decent nap between us.”

Her fingers tighten.

“But next time you offer someone a piece of yourself as currency,” she adds, “you clear it with me first.”

“Yes, Investigator,” I say.

This time, I let the fondness show.

She rolls her eyes.

“Come on,” she says. “We have a temple to crash tomorrow. And I need to annotate this.” She waves the Maestro’s parchment. “Viciously.”

We walk back into the bones of the city, side by side.

Somewhere deep under our feet, the dead god’s heart gives a little pre‑Pulse twitch, as if reminding us that midnight is coming. 

For the first time, I find myself thinking: good.

Let it beat.

Let it see me.

I don’t intend to bow this time.
