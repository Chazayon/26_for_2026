# Chapter 16 — *The Map on the Floor*
### Lysa | POV

I have spent my entire adult life learning the law so I would know, on the day it finally mattered, exactly where to slip the knife.

Today is the day.

The Sanctum feels colder than usual. Or maybe that’s just me.

Bone-light pours down from the high sigil-crusted dome in a flat, pitiless glare, turning the polished table into a dissecting slab and my hands into pale, shaking things on its surface. I flatten my palms anyway, spreading my fingers until the tendons go taut. It stops the trembling from showing.

Across from me, High Inquisitor Voss shuffles parchment with the air of a man tidying an altar before a sacrifice.

To my right, half-swallowed by the Sanctum’s shadows, Contract Lord Thessen lounges in his chair, ankle across his knee, one hand cradling a cut-glass tumbler of something amber and unnecessary. He’s not supposed to drink in this room. Thankfully, no one has ever cared less about the rules they write than the men who write them. 

The document lies between us, neat and white and obscene.

FINAL AUTHORISATION – DONOR TRIAL: BLOOD RELIC (C-17)

My name is not on it yet.

My mark—the little sigil that means I have weighed the Tribunal’s appetites against its laws and found them acceptably aligned—sits in faint, waiting grey at the bottom. All it needs is ink.

“Sign it, Auditor,” Voss says, tapping the page with an inkless finger. “Your seventy-two hours have lapsed. No valid ethical objections were recorded. The donors are growing impatient.” 

My title is Investigator, but he always likes to remind me I started here as a scribe.

I keep my gaze on the parchment. On the tiny loop of space where my mark would live.

In the chapel last night, my hand on Cyran’s chest, his fingers locking over mine like a drowning man grabbing a rope. You were a person. They made you a thing. That’s not redemption. That’s theft. The words are still in my mouth, ghost-echo on my tongue. They taste like oath.

If I sign this, I am their thief. As surely as Varos ever was. 

I look up.

“I am submitting a formal refusal,” I say.

My voice comes out very calm. It surprises me.

Voss stills. “On what grounds?” he asks.

“Founder Clause Eleven-A.” I don’t have to check my notes. I could recite this in my sleep. “Coercion of a bonded asset. The Donor Trial requires the informed consent of the subject. The Relic is a bonded asset; the historical record shows a pattern of coercion in his acquisition and deployment. My signature would be a violation of the Founders’ Charter.” 

Thessen stops pretending to be uninterested.

He leans forward, glass dangling between two fingers, the way a predator dangles meat over a pit just to watch the things inside jump.

“You are overstepping, Scribe,” he says, that smooth courtroom voice curling around the word like silk around a blade. “Clause Eleven-A has not been invoked in decades. It was meant for the old wars. Child conscripts. Press-ganged mages. Not… Redemption assets.” 

Redemption assets. I do not look at him. If I do, I will imagine my ink-stained hands around his throat and we will all have a different problem.

“My mandate is clear,” I say, eyes on Voss. “I am required to certify that this proceeding complies with both Tribunal law and Founder Charter. It does not. I am therefore refusing to sign.” 

Voss’s jaw clenches. A small pulse ticks in his temple.

“You’re basing this on temple-era records,” he says. “Highly redacted ones at that. Whatever happened to the Relic before the Tribunal assumed oversight is… regrettable, but not relevant. We have given him purpose. A chance at redemption.” 

You were a person. They made you a thing.

My nails bite into the polished bone. I don’t let it show on my face.

“His purpose,” I say, “is irrelevant to the Charter’s definition of coercion. You know that. Bonded assets cannot meaningfully consent to expanded deployment when refusal carries threat of decommission, imprisonment, or increased suffering. Your Donor Trial is an expansion. You are attempting to profit further off a body already stolen. The Founders wrote a word for that scenario, Inquisitor.” 

Voss’s eyes narrow. “Careful, Lysa,” he says softly.

“Slavery,” I say.

Silence drops into the Sanctum like a stone into deep water.

Thessen’s smile does not move. His eyes do.

“Careers end for less,” he murmurs.

There it is.

The threat he’s been circling since the moment I walked into this case.

I glance at him just long enough to acknowledge that I’ve heard it, that I fully understand the slice he’s aiming for, and then dismiss him as if he’s one more line item on a ledger.

“Be that as it may,” I say, turning back to Voss, “the law stands. My signature is required for audit closure. You do not have it. Convening the Donor Trial without it would be illegal.” 

My heart is pounding so hard I can feel it under my tongue.

I am not supposed to say things like this in this room.

I am supposed to be parchment and ink and quiet efficiency, the little machine that takes their atrocities and renders them legible.

Voss inhales slowly through his nose, then exhales in a long, controlled stream.

“You are making a grave mistake,” he says.

“Possibly,” I agree.

We look at each other over the blank space where my name should be.

He is a man who has outlived better auditors than me by cultivating the illusion that he is merely the weather, not the storm. I can see the calculation moving behind his eyes now, the way he’s weighing outcomes; Thessen’s displeasure versus the Founders’ Charter versus the visibility of this case.

He can’t drag me from this room and have me quietly erased. Not yet. Too many ledgers with my name on them. Too many eyes on Cyran’s file.

Thessen shifts.

“Very well,” he says finally, voice gone cool and sharp. “You refuse to sign. Your objections are noted. The Tribunal will… reconsider its path forward.” 

Reconsider.

Such a lovely word for sharpen the knife.

Voss makes a small notation at the bottom of the page. Not my name. Something else. A sigil that means contested. Flag for later.

“I expect your full written rationale on my desk by dawn,” he says. “Citing all relevant precedents.”

“Of course,” I say.

I turn.

I do not bow. I do not look back.

The door to the Sanctum is heavier on the way out. My shoulder bumps it open. The corridor beyond greets me like an old accomplice—narrow, humming with pneumatic tubes and the low drone of dictated opinions.

My knees want to buckle.

They don’t.

The marrow-mist hits the moment I step out of the Spine proper. It falls from the dead god’s ribs in a fine, stinging drizzle, carrying bone dust and the sharp tang of ozone. The lamps along the walkway smear into fuzzy halos.

My formal robes are not made for this.

The heavy indigo wool soaks through in minutes, dragging at my shoulders, wicking cold along my spine. My hair, which started the day in a respectable knot, has surrendered; stray damp strands cling to my neck.

I walk fast anyway, Tribunal mark burning under the wet sleeve of my left wrist like a brand.

Adrenaline is doing strange things to time. The world feels both louder and too muffled. Every footstep on the bone plating of the street echoes. Every hiss from the steam vents sounds like laughter.

I have just put a target on my own back the exact size and shape of Contract Lord Thessen’s ledger.

Good.

The lifts down from the Sanctum are choked tonight; clerks and junior advocates cluster under awnings, coats pulled tight, cursing the marrow weather. I take the spine-steps instead, boots ringing on stone as I spiral down toward the mid-levels, breath puffing white in the damp chill.

Halfway to my own district, I cut left, into a narrower artery of the Spine.

Old Valla’s stall crouches against the wall like a stubborn fungus, wedged between two ventilation shafts. Canvas flaps snap and shudder in the wind, stained with years of marrow and smoke. The air here smells of boiling water, dried herbs, and the particular sour-sweet of overripe bone-fruit.

Valla herself sits on a three-legged stool behind the battered counter, hands busy stripping leaves from a branch of something pale and brittle. Her cataract-clouded eyes stare somewhere over my shoulder, unfocused.

“Heavy steps tonight, little scribe,” she rasps, before I can announce myself. “You’re carrying more than ledgers.” 

I huff out a breath that might be a laugh, might be a sob.

“I should stop being surprised you can hear that,” I say, ducking under the awning. The canvas coughs marrow-water down my back; I suppress a shudder.

She smiles, showing a row of surprisingly even teeth.

“Sound hits the bones different when you’re tired,” she says. “Makes them ring. What’ll it be? Something for sleep? For courage? For not getting your fool self killed in the Spine?”

“Tea,” I say. “The good sage, if you have it. And a loaf if Old Beren stopped by.” 

She reaches unerringly for a bundle of dried leaves hanging from the stall’s frame, fingers nimble despite the stiffness in her knuckles. “Always have sage for you,” she says. “The bread’s hard as tribunal hearts tonight, but it’ll soften in steam.”

“Perfect,” I say. “Soak anything long enough, it yields.”

She snorts.

“Careful, girl. Talk like that, people will start thinking you’re dangerous.” She wraps the sage in a scrap of waxed paper, tucks the loaf into a cloth, and extends both toward me, hands steady despite the tremor in her shoulders. “Coin.”

I overpay without thinking. A few extra coppers, slipped into her palm with the same quiet guilt I’ve always felt when I climb back up to warm offices and she stays here in the marrow-damp, selling comfort to people who will never be on Voss’s ledgers except as collateral damage.

She feels the coins, frowns. “This is too much.”

“It’s raining,” I say. “Your joints will ache tomorrow.”

She clicks her tongue. “Cheap penance,” she mutters. “Go home, little scribe. You sound like thunder.”

Thunder.

Or something about to fall apart.

I clutch the parcel to my chest and keep walking.

My quarters are a narrow slit of space carved into the mid-Spine’s inner wall, halfway between the Tribunal’s respectable halls and the Vein Markets’ chaos. Close enough to the Sanctum that I can be summoned at dawn, far enough that the worst of the marrow-mist turns to simple damp by the time it seeps through my bone-glass window.

The stair up is treacherous when wet. I take it two steps at a time, one hand on the rail, heart hammering harder the closer I get.

There is a shadow curled beside my door.

For half a second, my adrenaline-flooded brain conjures Thessen’s hounds—contract enforcers in sleek bone masks, ready to drag me back up by the mark on my wrist.

Then the shadow uncoils.

Cyran pushes off the wall.

He’s stripped down tonight. No velvet, no silk, no ridiculous cravat. Just dark Vigil leather, damp with mist, and a plain black shirt under it. His hair is tied back in a rough knot that’s already losing the fight against the weather. The effect should be less devastating. It’s not.

He looks… uncertain.

Like a man who has rehearsed half a dozen openings and thrown them all away at the last minute.

“I was beginning to think you’d decided Sanctum cells were more appealing than your mattress,” he says. The attempt at lightness is there, but his voice is too tight around the edges.

“I’m punctual,” I say, because if I start with the truth—Voss, Thessen, the unsigned document—I might crumple in the doorway.

I fish the heavy iron key from my pocket. It sticks in the lock for a moment; moisture has swollen the wood around it. I jiggle, brace my shoulder, and finally the mechanism gives with a protesting clack.

The door swings inward on a room that has never felt smaller.

It is not much: one narrow bed shoved under the window, thin mattress neatly made; a scarred bone-wood table crowded with ledgers and loose sheets; two mismatched chairs; a single shelf sagging under the weight of books. A tiny hearth sulks in the far corner, cold ashes waiting for a match.

It smells like ink, paper, and the faint ghost of past sage.

For the first time, I see it as someone else might: cramped, cluttered, aggressively functional. No velvet, no gold. No space for anyone but me.

“Come in,” I say, stepping aside.

He hesitates on the threshold.

If this were three days ago, he’d have turned that pause into a flourish. A joke about crossing into a lady’s boudoir, a bow that drew my eye to the line of his throat.

Tonight he just looks at me.

“Are you sure?” he asks.

It’s a simple question. It lands like a test.

“Yes,” I say, and mean it. “I invited you.”

His shoulders loosen a fraction.

He steps over the threshold.

The room shrinks another inch to accommodate him. He seems too large for it, all that night-black leather and pale skin and history shoving itself into a space that has only ever had to hold my ink-stained solitude.

I shut the door behind us. The last of the marrow-mist cuts off. The quiet is almost startling.

I shrug out of my soaked outer robe. The wool peels away from my shirt with a wet, reluctant sound. Underneath, my white linen undertunic clings to my arms, thinner and blessedly lighter. I hang the robe on the peg near the hearth, where it will drip onto the stone instead of my books.

His eyes track the movement.

I pretend not to notice.

“It pushed through,” I say, because if I don’t fill the silence with something, the sound of my own heartbeat will drive me mad. “The Gala. They tried to move for trial tonight.” 

He goes very still.

“You’re joking,” he says. Too fast. Too sharp.

“I refused to sign,” I continue. “Invoiced Founder Clause Eleven-A on the record. Voss is furious. Thessen is… amused.” 

His jaw works.

“You defied a Contract Lord and the High Inquisitor in the same breath,” he says slowly. “On paper.” There is a strange mixture of horror and awe in his voice. “Lysa, they’ll destroy you.”

“Let them try,” I say.

It comes out before I can temper it. The words are hot, sharp, too honest.

He inhales, a sound like someone had just unlocked a new drawer in his chest.

I move.

Action is easier than the way he’s looking at me.

I kneel by the hearth, fingers numb and clumsy as I stack old marrow-logs in a criss-cross and strike flint to tinder. Sparks leap. Smoke curls, thin and bitter, until the wood catches and the first lick of flame flares up, painting the room in soft orange.

Warmth begins to creep out, slow but certain.

I set the kettle on the iron hook over the nascent fire, pour water from the jug, drop the bundle of sage into a clay pot. My motions are automatic, the choreography of a life lived between paperwork and exhaustion.

Cyran watches from the center of the room, utterly still.

It’s unnerving, the way he does that—like a predator on a leash, motionless until he isn’t. Tonight, though, there is no coiled readiness for violence in it. Just… attention. As if lighting a kettle might reveal a secret he has been hunting for centuries.

“You do this every night?” he asks.

“Tea?” I shrug. “Most. The fire, when there’s fuel. The Tribunal doesn’t like to waste heat on scribes once the lamps are out.”

He huffs something approximating a laugh.

“Monsters,” he says.

“You have no idea,” I say dryly.

His gaze flickers to the table, to the tottering pile of ledgers and loose parchment.

“Are those… for me?” he asks.

“In a manner of speaking.” I wipe my damp hands on my undertunic and cross to the table, nudging a stack aside to make room for cups. “Prime Contract. Founder Charter. Precedents on bonded asset coercion. Everything they’ve ever written that I can turn into a knife and hand you.” 

He steps closer, slowly, like a man approaching an altar he does not trust.

I pull out one of the chairs. The leg scrapes against the floor. The sound is homely and, strangely, thrilling.

“Sit,” I say.

He arches an eyebrow.

“Bossy,” he murmurs, but lowers himself into the chair.

I take the other.

The table is small. Smaller than I remember. Our knees bump under it as I reach for the nearest ledger. The brush of contact sparks up my leg like static. I do not flinch.

“We have days,” I say. “Maybe. If Thessen moves fast, less. The Vigil can’t legally redeploy you without trial, but legality is… flexible when it wants to be. So we need to build something airtight before they decide to improvise.” 

He stares at me for a heartbeat.

Then he reaches for a book.

Not with his usual theatrical flourish—not the way he handles a wineglass or a donor’s wrist. He takes the ledger the way I do: with both hands, careful not to crack the spine, reverent almost.

“Where do we start?” he asks.

Something in my chest lurches.

“We start,” I say, “by reading the Prime Contract as if it isn’t scripture.”

I flip it open between us. The thin bone pages whisper. My ink has already marked a forest of marginalia along the edges—notes, questions, curses so tiny they barely show.

He leans in.

The room tilts again, just slightly, as his shoulder brushes mine. A few stray droplets of marrow-mist cling to the collar of his shirt, catching the firelight. Up close, I can see a faint smear of something darker along his cheekbone—ink or soot or an old bruise poorly scrubbed.

My fingers twitch with the urge to wipe it away.

I don’t.

“Clause Seven,” I say instead, tapping the column. “Bonded assets may not be deployed in contexts that materially increase their risk of harm without explicit, informed consent, documented and witnessed.” 

He snorts.

“And you think anyone in that Sanctum cares what Clause Seven says,” he says.

“I think,” I reply, “that men like Voss care very deeply about appearing to care. Thessen can break legs in alleys. The Tribunal has to pretend it’s a court.” 

He glances at me, lips quirking.

“Clever girl,” he says softly.

I roll my eyes, but the words settle in my chest like a small, stubborn coal of warmth.

The kettle begins to murmur, steam fingering its way from the spout. I stand, pour the water over the sage leaves, watch them curl and darken, releasing their bitter, grounding scent.

Cyran’s eyes follow the movement.

“This is very…” he gestures vaguely, searching for the word.

“Domestic?” I supply.

“Horrifying,” he agrees. “You’ll have me knitting socks next.”

“You’d stab someone with the needles,” I say, setting a cup in front of him. The porcelain is warm against my chilled fingers. “Drink. It’ll take the marrow-cold out of your bones.”

He lifts the cup, sniffing cautiously.

“It smells like Balla’s back room,” he says, nose wrinkling. “I once watched a man get his fortune told in sage smoke and he vomited on the rug.”

“High praise,” I say. “Sip. It’s not meant to be pleasant. It’s meant to keep you upright.”

He sips.

His face contorts in an expression of such pure, unvarnished disgust that I can’t help it—I laugh.

It bursts out of me, sharp and bright, cutting through the tension like a chisel through old bone.

He stares at me, affronted, then, slowly, his mouth curves.

“There she is,” he murmurs. “I wondered if I’d chased her off in the chapel.”

“You’d have to try harder,” I say.

The bread is as hard as Valla promised. We tear off pieces, dip them in the hot tea to soften them, chew between statutory citations. It is, by any rational measure, a miserable meal. My stomach is too tight to care. The ritual of it—tear, dip, chew—keeps my hands from shaking.

We work.

Hours slide off the candle in slow layers of wax.

Cyran reads faster than I expected. His fingertip tracks the lines on the page, lips moving sometimes as he mouths the old formal phrases. Occasionally he stops, frowns, looks up.

“Here,” he says once, tapping a line I’d skimmed. “They define ‘donor engagement’ as non-essential deployment. Doesn’t that trigger your precious Clause Forty-Four?” 

“It does,” I say, leaning in. Our heads nearly knock together. “How did I miss that?”

“Too close,” he says. “You’ve been staring at this so long the ink has probably imprinted on your retinas.”

“Flattering,” I mutter, making a note.

“Truthful,” he counters.

Another time, he traces a marginalia question with his thumb. “You wrote this,” he says. “Months ago.”

“A suspicion,” I say. “About the Prime Contract’s wording on property versus personhood. I didn’t have a case to test it on then.”

“And now you do,” he says.

“And now I do,” I echo.

Us.

It settles between us without being spoken.

The rain outside intensifies, a steady hiss against the bone-glass of the window. The fire snaps softly, throwing shadows that dance along his cheekbones, along the lines of his throat where his collar gapes just enough to show pale skin and the hint of old scars.

At some point, I realise my hair has come fully down. Pins lie scattered near an empty inkwell. Strands fall into my eyes; I push them back impatiently. His hand lifts, halfway, as if to help, then drops.

The air between us grows thicker.

Not unpleasant. Just… dense. Charged.

We sit closer than we did an hour ago. Our shoulders are solidly pressed now, thighs aligned under the table. Every time I reach for the inkwell, my wrist brushes his. Every time he turns a page, his knuckles graze my forearm.

It should be distracting.

It is.

And yet the work does not suffer. If anything, it sharpens. Our thoughts move in tandem, snapping off each other in quick, bright sparks.

“What if they argue the Relic enjoys it?” he says at one point, voice gone brittle.

“They will,” I say. “We’ll counter with the definition of enjoyment under coercion.” 

“Define enjoyment, Investigator,” he says, a razor-thin smile twisting his mouth.

“Free of threat,” I say. “Free of debt. Free of obligation. Something you can walk away from.”

His gaze drops to my mouth as I speak, then darts back up, guilty and hungry all at once.

Midnight tolls from a distant bone-tower, the low chime rolling through the walls like the heartbeat of the dead god we live in.

The last ledger thuds closed under my palm.

Ink pools around my fingers where the quill slipped, smudging the final notation. My eyes burn. My neck aches. My body is one long, humming wire of exhaustion and adrenaline.

“We have a foundation,” I say softly. “It’s not perfect. But it’s… something. Enough to make them bleed for this.” 

Cyran leans back in his chair, stretching with a low groan that does unfortunate things to the way his shirt pulls over his shoulders.

He scrubs a hand over his face, leaving a streak of ink on his cheekbone darker than the one that was already there. For a moment, he looks utterly human. Tired. Rumpled. Gorgeous.

“Who knew,” he says, “that my salvation would involve this much fine print.”

“Welcome to my world,” I say.

The fire has burned down to embers, a low red glow in the hearth. The kettle has gone quiet. The room is warm now, bordering on stuffy. My linen sticks to the small of my back.

I become acutely aware, in the sudden quiet, of how close he is.

Of the fact that there is nothing between us now—no Tribunal desk, no Vigil armour, no altar, no couch staged for seduction. Just a rickety table, a stack of books, and the inch of air between my hand and his on the scarred wood.

He notices it too.

I can tell by the way his breath hitches, almost imperceptibly. By the way his eyes flick down, up, then away, like he’s checking exits on a burning building.

“Lysa,” he says.

My name in his mouth is a low, careful thing. No performative purr. No smirk tucked into the syllables.

“Yes,” I answer.

He stands up slowly.

For a heartbeat, we are the only two vertical objects in the room, facing each other across the small table like opposing counsels.

He reaches for his coat draped over the back of his chair, fingers finding the worn leather, then stopping there, gripping it too tightly.

“I should…” he begins, then trails off.

Go, I finish for him in my head.

That’s the script. The gentlemanly retreat. The monster slipping back into the night so the nice Tribunal scribe can pretend the world is less complicated than it is.

He doesn’t move toward the door.

He stands there, coat half-grasped, staring at the wood as if it holds an answer he’s not sure he wants.

The air thickens further. The room narrows down to the line of his shoulders, the bow of his head, the white of his knuckles on worn black leather.

When he speaks again, his voice is stripped of everything I have ever seen him use as armour. No flirt. No theatre. Just a man with his heart in his throat.

“Lysa,” he says again.

“Yes,” I repeat, quieter now.

He does not look at me.

His hand loosens on the coat.

“Can I stay?” he asks.

The words are simple.

They are not. Not from him.

He is not asking to avoid the marrow-mist, or to save himself the walk back to the Vein. He is not asking for my body in exchange for my help, or offering his as currency.

He is asking: am I allowed to be here? In this cramped, ink-stained, utterly unremarkable little room that holds the whole of my real life.

Am I allowed into that?

My heart does something painful and beautiful in my chest.

There is no hesitation.

“Yes,” I say.

His head snaps up.

He searches my face like he expects to find a catch written there in invisible ink.

There isn’t one.

“I want you here,” I add, because if we’re burning our careers to the ground together, we might as well be explicit.

Something in his expression breaks.

Not in the way it does under command phrases, brittle shatter of a man forced to his knees. This is… softer. A careful cracking. The first fissures in the shell he has spent two centuries lacquer-painting onto himself.

“Then,” he says, voice hoarse, “I’ll stay.”

He lets go of the coat.

The room, impossibly, feels larger for it.

Outside, the Tribunal sharpens its knives and drafts its summonses.

Inside, we stand on opposite sides of a small table, hands ink-stained, eyes tired, hearts beating too fast, and take one long, terrifying step over a threshold that has nothing to do with doors.

Tomorrow, we fight the system.

Tonight, he stays.
