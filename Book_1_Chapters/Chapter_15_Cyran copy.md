# Chapter 15 — *The Paint and the Scar*
### Cyran | POV

There is no elegant way to say that you were a slave. The words always taste like ash.

The chapel smells the same as it did when I first bled on this altar—old incense, damp stone, marrow-wax gone sour with age. The air is colder down here than it has any right to be; it creeps up through my boots, curls around my ankles, climbs the bones of my legs like a slow, insistent hand.

I pace anyway.

The lower Ribcage chapel is a forgotten thing, wedged between cracked vertebrae where the city’s architects stopped pretending they cared about symmetry. The ceiling sags with old water damage, bone darkened and stained, ribs of the god’s cage thrusting through like bad teeth. The altar is a block of rough-cut vertebra, pitted and worn smooth by centuries of offerings and desperate hands.

Fractured bone-pews tilt at slight angles, as if they’re tired of holding anyone up.

An elderly acolyte shuffles down the side aisle, taper in hand, lighting marrow-candles one by one. Each wick catches with a damp little pop, filling the room with a thin, greasy glow. His robe is patched at the elbows; his breath whistles faintly when he leans.

He isn’t temple. He’s the Tribunal’s idea of faith—something you delegate to the underpaid and aging, a box to tick on a ledger.

The door at the back groans open on a draft of evening mist, cool air whispering along the stone. I look up, already knowing.

Lysa steps in, drawing the door shut behind her with a grunt like it weighs more than she does. The last smear of sunset clings to her shoulders—dull, dying light on ink-stained indigo. Her cloak is damp at the hem; she shakes a few stubborn droplets from it before pulling the hood back, smoothing her hair with a quick, unconscious gesture that makes my stomach twist.

She looks like she hasn’t slept. There are faint bruises under her eyes, smudges of exhaustion that no amount of Tribunal starch can iron out. Her cuffs are marked dark where ink bled into the fabric.

She is the most dangerous thing in this building.

“Relic,” the old acolyte murmurs, dipping in a half-bow he probably gave better men, better gods. His eyes flick from me to Lysa and back, mouth tugging down at the corners with the slow, practiced anxiety of someone used to being near trouble.

“Leave us,” I say.

My voice lands flatter than I intend. No velvet, no teeth. Just a command, worn smooth by centuries of repetition.

He falters, glancing toward the altar as if waiting for a higher authority.

I reach into my coat, pull out a silver Tribunal mark, and flick it down the aisle. It arcs through the candlelight and lands in his open palm with a soft clink.

“Buy yourself a meal,” I add.

He stares at the coin for a heartbeat like it might bite him, then tucks it away into his robe, eyes carefully averted.

“As you wish, mlord Relic,” he says, old habit tripping over a dead title. He snuffs the last taper, shuffles toward the door, and slips out. The heavy wood swings shut with a hollow thud that reverberates along my spine.

Silence swells in the wake of his departure, thick as smoke.

I turn back to the altar.

It’s easier to face old bone than the woman who just read my life as a series of bullet points and didn’t run.

My fingers find the worn grooves along the edge of the stone—channels carved by centuries of hands trailing in prayer or desperation. I trace them, following their slow curves like they might lead me somewhere that isn’t here.

Bootsteps approach, measured and unhurried.

“Cyran,” Lysa says.

Her voice is quieter than it was in the archives, but no less steady.

“Investigator.” I keep my back to her. My reflection watches me from the cracked bone relief above the altar—a pale smear, nothing like the carefully curated creature donors get. I look like what I am for once: tired. Hollow-eyed. Less ornament, more leftover.

“You came,” I add, because my mouth insists on saying something.

“You invited me,” she says. “After accusing me of voyeurism in a closed stacks vault.”

“Mm.” I let my nail scrape a little too hard along the groove. A sliver of bone dust powders under my fingertip. “I do have a flair for the dramatic.”

“You banned the notebook,” she reminds me.

I close my eyes for a second, grateful for the thread. “I did.”

“Is the ban still in effect?”

“Yes.”

Silence, then the soft rasp of leather and cloth as she moves.

“I left it in the corridor,” she says.

I exhale slowly, unclenching muscles I didn’t realize were locked.

No pen. No ledger. No witness but the bones.

I turn.

She’s in the second pew from the front, hands folded in her lap, cloak pooled around her hips. The candles paint hollows in her face, catching on the wire of her spectacles, throwing her eyes into deeper shadow. She looks smaller sitting there, but not diminished. Contained.

Waiting.

“I suppose you want an apology,” I say.

“For what?” she asks.

“Oh, I don’t know.” I wave a hand, brittle. “Storming into your nice quiet archive and slamming your book closed. Accusing you of getting off on my trauma. Standard tantrum behaviour.”

“Do you feel sorry?” she asks, infuriatingly direct.

I huff out something that might be a laugh.

“Not particularly,” I admit. “I feel… exposed. Cornered. Furious. None of that translates neatly into contrition.”

“Then no,” she says. “I don’t want an apology. I want the truth. You said you’d give it here.”

Of course she did.

I push myself back against the altar, leaning with my palms flat on the cold stone. The chill bites through the leather of my gloves, grounding and unpleasant, exactly what I need.

“Do you have any idea,” I ask, “how grotesquely unromantic my Turning was?”

Her mouth tugs, not into a smile—into something like pain.

“I have guesses,” she says. “After yesterday.”

“Let me ruin it properly,” I say.

Any other time, I’d lace it with a leer. Make a show of leaning closer, dropping my voice, inviting her into the story like a performance.

Tonight, my voice stays flat.

“No moonlit seduction,” I begin. “No mysterious stranger in an alley offering immortality in exchange for a kiss. I was a soldier with too much blood on my hands and not enough sense to leave the front when they told me to. Varos bought my contract off a commander with a gambling problem.”

The words taste like old iron.

“I woke up on a stone slab,” I say. “Naked. Strapped down. Runic grid carved into the table so my blood would flow in very pretty lines when he opened me. He didn’t ask if I wanted to be a Relic. He asked if I wanted to be useful.”

My fingers curl against the altar.

“I said yes,” I add. “Because I was young and stupid and so very tired. I thought anything was better than another tour in the trenches. Turns out there are worse things than dying for your city.”

Lysa doesn’t move.

Her knuckles are white where she grips her own hands. Her face is carved still.

She doesn’t say I’m sorry.

Good.

“The Turning was… methodical,” I continue. “He wanted a specific result. So he bled me to the edge of death, then fed me temple wine mixed with the blood of things that shouldn’t have bled at all. Did it again. And again. Until my heart forgot which side of the ledger it was supposed to beat on.”

Memory roils under my skin: the taste of that wine, thick and bitter; the smell of burned sigils and hot marrow; the sound of my own scream echoing back off stone.

I keep my voice even.

“He carved the sigils into me,” I say. “Every line of temple script you saw in that ledger? He put it in my skin first. Each one a hook. You say the right phrase in High-Divine, it tugs. Makes you kneel, makes you smile, makes you bare your throat or your fangs whether you want to or not.”

My thumb rubs unconsciously at the inside of my wrist, where one of the more stubborn sigils lives. It hums faintly, as if the memory alone is enough to wake it.

“They called it consecration of the vessel in the file,” she says quietly.

“Of course they did.” My lip curls. “Sounds nicer than ‘we turned a man into a marionette and congratulated ourselves on the craftsmanship.’”

I push off the altar, letting the momentum carry me a few steps toward the aisle. The candles flicker as I pass, shadows shivering along the walls.

“The first time they killed me, it was… educational,” I say. “For everyone.”

My heart gives a small, traitorous twitch at the phrase. Educational. Useful. Beautiful. All the temple’s favourite words.

“They drained me,” I say. “In front of a very appreciative crowd. Did it slowly, so the donors could see the light go out. The resurrection was the main event—Varos loved a miracle. It sold better.”

Lysa’s throat works. Her mouth presses flat, but she stays silent.

“Resurrection Protocols,” I quote, mocking the ledger’s tidy script. “I read the entry once, years ago, when I still believed there might be a mistake. ‘No significant degradation after third iteration.’ Do you know what it does to a mind, knowing death isn’t an exit? That it’s just another loop in the show?”

I laugh, soft and wrecked.

“Eventually you stop trying to run,” I say. “You learn your marks. You bleed where they tell you, bite where they guide your head, smile when the donor strokes your hair and tells you you’re glorious.”

The pew creaks as she shifts, just barely.

“Sex,” I say, because there’s no point dressing that part up. “You saw the euphemisms. Intimate rites. Donor appeasement. Holy union. All very poetic ways of saying they turned me into a temple whore and called it worship.”

My hand finds something on the altar without looking—a shriveled offering-fig, left by some hopeful fool with a prayer and no better place to put it. Its skin is wrinkled, split in one place where the flesh inside has hardened to sugar-stone.

I pick it up, roll it between my fingers.

“Varos liked pretty things,” I say. “He liked arranging them. Altars, donors, Relics. Me. I was a good investment. Obedient. Regenerative. Easy on the eyes. They wrote that down, too, in your nice red book. ‘His beauty is an asset; fully exploit.’”

The echo of the words in my head makes my teeth hurt.

I squeeze the fig.

It collapses with a soft, obscene little crunch, brittle flesh and seeds grinding into paste in my palm.

“Every touch was a command,” I say. “Every kiss a transaction. Do you understand? There was no part of my body that belonged to me. If I flinched, they adjusted the wine. If I hesitated, they turned the sigils up. If I screamed too loudly, they numbed me so the donors wouldn’t have to feel… untidy.”

My voice has gone rough without me noticing. It scrapes against my throat.

“I tried to die,” I add. “Properly. More than once. Bit through my own tongue, choked on the blood so I couldn’t say the responses. Jumped from the rib balconies between rites. They dragged me back every time. You don’t waste an asset that expensive.”

The smashed fig drips between my fingers, sticky and dark, spattering the stone at my feet like a parody of blood.

“I stopped trying,” I say. “Eventually. It was easier to… partition. Put it in a box. Tell myself it was holy. Necessary. Beautiful.”

My mouth twists.

“And then,” I continue, “the Tribunal won. The temple fell. Varos disappeared into whatever hole swallows men like him. The Contract Lords came in with their ledgers and their polite voices and their new words for old cages.”

The candles hiss. A drop of wax overflows, splattering onto the altar with a faint sizzle.

“Thessen found me in the rubble,” I say. “He knew a good investment when he saw one. They didn’t even have to change the paperwork much. Swap ‘High Priest’ for ‘Contract Lord,’ ‘temple rites’ for ‘Vigil commendations,’ ‘donors’ for ‘patrons.’ Redemption instead of devotion. Same altar. Different paint.”

My boots scrape as I step down from the dais, moving into the central aisle. The pews flank me like ruined teeth.

Lysa’s gaze tracks me, steady and unblinking.

“All of which,” I say, throat tight, “brings us to you.”

Her fingers knit tighter in her lap.

“The night in my quarters,” I say. “You remember it. Of course you do. I certainly do. Very dramatic. Bleeding Vigil, exhausted Tribunal scribe, shared wine, biting offer on the table.”

Her mouth twitches. “Vividly,” she says.

“Good.”

I stop a few feet from her, the candles behind me throwing my shadow long across the floor. The smashed fig sticks to my palm; I can feel the seeds cutting into my skin.

“I saw you,” I say, “and thought: here is an opportunity.”

The words hurt.

More than the rites. More than the nights I let strangers touch me because the alternative was worse. This is a different kind of flaying—self-inflicted, but no less precise.

“You were neat,” I continue. “Uncompromising. Moral in a way that didn’t make me want to vomit. Voss’s favourite, which meant you had power. Limited, but real. I thought if I could get you on my side, I might buy myself a kinder leash.”

I force myself to meet her eyes.

“I courted you,” I say, every syllable a stone. “Because I thought it would keep me safe. Because seduction is the only way I have ever known to matter. To be useful. To be more than a weapon on a shelf.”

Her breath catches—so quiet I almost miss it.

“I flirted,” I say. “I made you laugh. I offered you my body like a bribe and my blood like an apology. Not because I thought you were easy—gods, that would have been simpler—but because the part of me that still believes I am nothing but an asset said this is how we survive.”

The fig pulp is grinding into my skin now, tacky and abrasive.

“I told myself,” I say, voice dropping, “that if you wanted me, you’d have to fight for me. That maybe, if I made you complicit, you’d be less likely to sign the paper that sells me off to Thessen’s party.”

Silence crashes down after that like a closing tomb.

There it is.

The ugliest part.

The thing I have not admitted to myself, let alone to her.

I drop my gaze to the floor between my boots.

“I am sorry,” I say, finally. “For that.”

She doesn’t move.

The candles pop and hiss. A draft squeezes under the door, teasing the edge of her cloak. Somewhere in the walls, water drips, slow and patient.

I close my eyes.

Wait.

For the slap. For the sharp scrape of the pew as she shoves back and storms out. For the quiet, measured condemnation in her voice as she says I expected better.

For anything other than this soft, unbearable silence.

Fabric rustles.

The pew creaks.

My shoulders flinch before I can stop them.

“Look at me,” she says.

I do.

She’s standing.

Closer than she was, but not in my space yet. She takes one step into the aisle. Then another. Her robes whisper against the bone floor. Her face is pale, mouth set, eyes burning with something so bright it’s almost painful to look at.

She stops an arm’s length away.

“I am not going to tell you it’s all right,” she says. “Because it’s not.”

The words land like blows. I flinch again.

“But I am going to tell you this,” she continues, and her voice does something new—something sharp and shaking and furious that has nothing to do with me and everything to do with the bones of this place. “None of what you just confessed makes you a villain in this story.”

I bark a laugh, brittle.

“Doesn’t it?” I ask. “Manipulating the nice scribe to stay alive? Using the only currency I have left? It’s a classic move, love. Textbook Relic.”

Her eyes flash.

“They wrote it down as holy duty,” she says.

The phrase cuts straight through me.

“You know that, now,” she goes on, stepping into my space before I can retreat. “You saw the words. Consecration. Compliance. Appeasement. As if carving obedience into your skin was something to be proud of. As if selling your body and your pain and your death was a sacred act.”

She is close enough now that I can see the fine cracks in her ink-dried skin, the faint tremor in her jaw. The smell of her—ink, linen, a hint of cheap soap—cuts through the chapel’s old incense like fresh air.

“You were a person,” she says, each word a strike of a gavel. “They made you a thing. That’s not redemption, Cyran. That’s theft.”

The world tilts.

It shouldn’t do that over one sentence.

I have had knives in my chest that hurt less than this—less cleanly, anyway.

I sway.

My knees very nearly give.

Some idiot part of my brain, trained on temple spectacle, thinks: ah, dramatic timing.

The rest of me is too busy trying to breathe around the fist that has just closed around my heart.

“You—” I start, but it comes out as a ragged exhale.

She doesn’t let me look away.

“Everything you just told me,” she says, low and fierce, “is proof of what they stole. Your body. Your choices. Your ability to believe anyone could touch you without demanding payment. You survived by turning yourself into what they wanted. That doesn’t make you complicit. It makes you resourceful. It makes you alive.”

“No,” I whisper. “It makes me—”

“Stop,” she snaps, sharp enough to crack the word in my mouth.

Her hand lifts.

She pauses.

“May I?” she asks.

The question hits me in the same raw place the theft line did.

“What—” My voice breaks. “What are you—”

“Your chest,” she says. Her eyes flick down, then back up. “Just here.” She taps the air over my sternum. “No further.”

My heart is trying to punch its way through my ribs.

“Yes,” I hear myself say.

Her palm settles, warm and firm, in the center of my chest.

The contact is so startling my breath stops.

No command. No ritual phrase. No hand on a chain.

Just heat. Pressure. A clear, present-point marker in a body that has not felt like mine for a very long time.

“You feel that?” she asks.

It’s a ridiculous question.

Of course I do.

Her hand is a brand over skin that has only known ownership as pain.

But the way she says it, quiet and intent, makes me drag my awareness there, to the beat under bone and muscle and old scar tissue.

My heart.

Still mine. Somehow.

“Yes,” I whisper.

Her fingers curl slightly, as if she could get an even better grip on that traitorous organ.

“This,” she says, voice softening but not losing its edge, “is not in their ledger. They can catalogue your rites and your deaths and your smiles. They can quantify your beauty and your blood. They do not own this.”

She presses, just enough that I’m suddenly, acutely aware of the shape of her hand, of the thin barrier of leather and cloth between us, of the fact that she is closer to me than any donor ever was and there is no script guiding either of us.

A sound tears out of me. I have no name for it.

Without thinking, I cover her hand with mine.

It’s an instinct as old as my first chain—lock it down, make sure it doesn’t move, control the variable.

But there’s no panic in the motion. No desperation.

Just… clutching.

Like I’m afraid she might vanish if I don’t anchor her there.

Her pulse thuds against my palm. Faster than mine. Mortal. Gloriously, defiantly mortal.

“You were a person,” she repeats, softer now. “You are a person. What they did to you is on them, not on you. And what you did to survive?” Her eyes burn into mine. “I forgive it. All of it. Full stop.”

“Just like that,” I croak.

“Yes.” Her chin lifts. “Just like that.”

“What if I don’t deserve it?”

“Then you’ll have to live with being wrong,” she says. “You’re very practiced at that.”

A broken laugh shoves its way past the lump in my throat.

“You’re terrible,” I say.

“Good,” she says. “Terrible women get things done.”

The silence that follows is different than the ones before.

Not empty. Full.

Of my rattling breath. Of her steady one. Of the distant drip in the walls. Of the faint hiss of marrow-candles. Of a heart trying to remember how to beat for itself, not for a crowd.

“Lysa,” I say.

“Yes.”

“I am still furious you read that ledger.”

“I know.”

“I am… ashamed,” I confess, the word tasting like something sour. “That you had to see it at all. That you had to hear this. That there is nothing elegant or powerful about what I was. Just… meat on an altar.”

Her hand under mine tightens.

“I am honoured you told me,” she says.

I blink.

“Honoured,” I repeat, disbelieving.

“Yes.” She nods once. “You said yourself—you stopped trying to die. You learned to partition. To perform. You have spent centuries making yourself beautiful so no one would notice you were hollow. Today, you chose to show me what’s underneath. That is not a small thing, Cyran. That is not a thing I take lightly.”

I shut my eyes.

The chapel tilts again, but differently now—like a ship slowly righting after a storm.

“I don’t know how to do this,” I admit. “This… being seen. Without the teeth. Without the performance.”

“We’ll learn,” she says.

“We?” I echo.

Her thumb moves once, a small stroke against my chest, almost lost under my hand.

“We,” she confirms. “I am not walking away. Not because of this. Especially not because of this.”

The part of me that has been waiting—patiently, smugly—for the moment she finally sees the monster and flees… fumbles.

He’s been my closest companion for a very long time, that little inner executioner. He doesn’t know what to do with unconditional terms.

“You realise,” I say slowly, “that by staying, you are making Thessen’s life very complicated.”

Her mouth curves, faint and dangerous.

“Good,” she says again. “He deserves it.”

The marrow-candles sputter as a draft curls through the chapel. Somewhere outside, a bone-bell tolls the hour.

Our clock ticks on.

Thessen’s gala still looms. Voss still sharpens his quills. Varos is still a shadow in the corners of my mind. None of that has changed.

But the ledger has a new line now.

Not in red leather. Not in temple script. In me.

I told someone the truth.

And she stayed.

I swallow hard.

“Then,” I say, forcing myself to loosen the death-grip on her hand just enough that my fingers can lace with hers instead, “let’s make sure they never get to write any more chapters, shall we?”

Her fingers twine with mine without hesitation.

“Now you’re thinking like an investigator,” she says.

“Gods forbid,” I murmur.

She huffs out a breath that might be a laugh, might be a sob.

For once, I don’t need to know which.

I just stand there, in the cold little chapel between the ribs of a dead god, her hand over my heart, mine over hers, and let the theft of my life be, for one fragile, impossible moment, something we stole back.
