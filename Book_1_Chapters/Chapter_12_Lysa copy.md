# Chapter 12 — *The Gala of Teeth*
### Lysa | POV

I used to believe the Tribunal’s cruelty was accidental, a byproduct of paper and bone and too many people hiding behind procedure.

Sitting here, in the Sanctum, I can finally see the teeth.

The chamber is round, carved into the thickest part of the Spine, and everything about it is designed to make you feel small. Pale vertebra-stone curves overhead in an imitation of a dome, etched with legal sigils so dense they read more like a threat than a prayer. Light filters down through slits of bone-glass, flat and cold, turning the long polished table into a slab ready for carving.

I sit at that table, hands folded neatly atop a sheet of clean parchment, spine straight in my formal indigo robes. My collar scratches. My head aches—half from the residual sunlight of the Hanging Gardens, half from the knowledge of exactly what I am about to hear.

Across from me, High Inquisitor Voss steeples his inkless fingers, expression carved into its usual mask of benevolent bureaucracy. To his right, lounging as if this is a private parlour and not the beating heart of the city’s justice, sits Contract Lord Thessen.

If you can call it justice.

Thessen smells of crushed lavender and old blood. The former clings to his skin from some expensive cologne, sweet and choking; the latter lives quieter, under his nails and in the fine lines at the corners of his mouth. He hides that part better.

Between us, resting in an offensively tidy stack, is the reason he asked that I attend this meeting in person: a sheaf of embossed parchment, crisp edges, ink still faintly glossy. Each page bears the same heading in elaborate calligraphy.

Sanctioned Donor Galas.

My stomach is very still. My hands are not; I have laced my fingers together to keep anyone from seeing the way my left thumb keeps wanting to rub at the inside of my wrist, where my Tribunal mark sits like a bad decision.

“Investigator Vaskin,” Voss says, finally, as if we are discussing weather patterns, not human bodies. “I appreciate your prompt attendance. Lord Thessen has graciously agreed to walk us through his proposal.”

Graciously. Of course.

Thessen smiles. It is a slow, feline thing, showing just enough teeth to hint. “Investigator,” he purrs. His voice is all velvet and courtroom charm, tailored to sound reasonable. “You’ve had time to review the draft?”

“Not in full.” I keep my tone neutral, professional, the way I learned to make it in my first month in this building. “I received the summary this morning.”

He plucks the top page from the stack with delicate fingers and slides it toward me. The parchment makes a soft hiss on the polished bone, the sound exactly like a blade being drawn.

“Then let’s remedy that,” he says. “We are, after all, all on the same side. Sanctuary must eat, and the Tribunal must balance its ledgers.”

I pick up the page.

The heading swallows half of it; the rest is a maze of small script and legal euphemism. I read fast.

Intimate feeding experiences. Exclusive patron access. Subsidized Relic maintenance. Tiered contribution models.

My skin goes cold. I keep my face very still.

State-sanctioned prostitution, wrapped in lace and temple language and the pretense of charity.

It is all there, in the careful, bloodless words. High-value donors will be invited to carefully controlled events where they may experience the thrill of controlled predation. Proceeds to fund Vigil operations and civic improvements. All biological resources managed under Tribunal oversight.

I do not see Cyran’s name on this page.

I don’t have to. I can hear the echo of Voss’s briefing in my skull, the way his tone slid just slightly when he said the Blood Relic case was of particular interest to our donors. The way Thessen’s seal appeared and reappeared in the margins of Cyran’s file like a stain.

My grip tightens on the quill in my right hand.

Thessen leans back in his chair, lounging in the shadow of the ribbed wall. He is eating something small and glossy from a silver dish—candied marrow-plums, ridged and lacquered in sugar. He plucks one up, holds it between forefinger and thumb.

“You must see the elegance of it,” he says. “The city gains revenue. The Relics gain controlled outlets for their… appetites. Our donors feel safer knowing the monsters are chained close, not stalking them in the streets. Everyone wins.”

Everyone except the person whose body is on the table.

Voss nods, pleased. “The Redemption Office has long sought a way to monetise the Relic program more directly. This seems… efficient.”

My quill snaps.

The sound is quiet—a sharp crack against the hum of distant pneumatic tubes and rustle of parchment—but in the Sanctum’s acoustics it might as well be lightning. Ink bleeds out across my fingers, warm and black, seeping into the grooves of my skin and the chewed edges of my cuticles before I can set the broken thing aside.

Three spots hit the proposal page, flowering dark over the word exclusive.

No one comments.

I set the ruined quill down very carefully.

“Under which statute,” I ask, “are you classifying this?”

There is the faintest tightening at the corner of Voss’s mouth. “Biological Resource Allocation,” he says. “As defined in Amendment—”

“I know the Amendment.” I don’t look up from the page. I can feel their eyes on me; I don’t give them the satisfaction of seeing mine yet. “I’m asking which specific clause you believe allows for this… arrangement.”

“Relics are bonded assets,” Thessen says smoothly, before Voss can answer. “The Tribunal authorises their use. We are merely… expanding the parameters of their service. In exchange, they are kept fed, clothed, and protected. It is far kinder than what the temple did with their… ornaments, don’t you think?”

Ink sticks under my nails. My heart is beating too fast and too hard. I can hear my own pulse in my ears, white-noise under the drone of his voice.

“You are aware,” I say, “that Statute Forty-Four prohibits endangerment of a subject through non-essential deployment.”

Thessen licks sugar from his fingertips. “Our Relic enjoys his work, Auditor. He has a documented history of… enthusiastic participation in intimate settings. We are merely formalising what is already in place. He is, you might say, uniquely suited to the role.”

There is a flash of Cyran in my mind’s eye: lounging in his quarters, glass of wine in hand, telling me he could, very easily, make my life more comfortable if I would only sign away my ethics along with his body.

The way his eyes looked when I told him no.

“You use that word,” I say. “Enjoys. Do you have documentation of his informed consent to this arrangement? Or do you simply call it enjoyment because he doesn’t fight hard enough to satisfy your conscience?”

Voss exhales through his nose, a patient sound. “Auditor—”

“Investigator.” My voice is glacial. “If you’re going to address me while we discuss selling a man, Inquisitor, I’d appreciate you use my proper title.”

The room holds its breath.

Thessen chuckles. “Fiery,” he says. “I can see why they assigned you to him. He always did like the principled ones.”

I stand very still. If I move too much, I will throw ink in his face.

“Lord Thessen’s proposal has been cleared for ethical review,” Voss says, every word smoothed flat. “You are here as a formality, Investigator. We need your audit complete and your signature in place before the first Gala. The donors are already expressing interest.”

“So am I,” I say.

Thessen tilts his head, amused. “Oh?”

“In the ethical review.” I place the page back on the stack, very precisely aligning the corners. My ink-stained hand leaves a faint smudge. Good. “As you know, any new classification of biological resource deployment requires a seventy-two hour scrutiny period under Statute Forty-Four, Section Eight.”

Voss frowns faintly. “That is intended for untested compounds, not—”

“Not what?” I ask. “Not for new ways of leasing out a thinking person’s body?” I turn my head, finally, and look Thessen in the eye. “You want to redefine an existing bonded asset’s use in a way that materially increases his risk of harm. Unless the law has changed since this morning, that triggers mandatory ethical review.”

For a moment I think Thessen will argue.

Then his mouth curves. He pops another candied plum into it, bites down with a small, obscene crack of sugar shell. “By all means,” he says. “Invoke your review, little scribe. Seventy-two hours. Use them well.”

Voss’s jaw tightens. “We will, of course, expect your findings promptly,” he says. “This program is… vital to our financial projections.”

“I’m sure it is,” I say. “I’ll file the invocation before I leave the building.”

“Excellent.” Thessen rises, smoothing a non-existent wrinkle from his dark coat. The lavender wafts stronger as he moves, sickly-sweet. “In that case, I will leave you to your… paperwork. I look forward to your conclusion.”

I doubt that very much.

He inclines his head to Voss, then to me. To him, we are functionally interchangeable—two different ends of the same ledger.

When the door shuts behind him, the temperature in the room seems to drop.

Voss sighs, more heavily than he usually allows himself in front of subordinates. “You have bought yourself time,” he says. “I would prefer we not waste it on dramatics.”

Dramatics.

My hands are still stained black. The ink has seeped into the lines of my palm, the webbing between my fingers. I could scrub for an hour and the traces would linger.

“I’ll need access to historical resource allocation precedents,” I say. “And the original creation rites documentation for the Blood Relic program.”

His eyes flicker. “Those are restricted.”

“Then unrestrict them,” I say. “Without full context, any review will be superficial.”

He studies me for a long moment, expression unreadable.

“You are on thin ice, Investigator,” he says at last. “There are many who would be eager to replace you if they perceive… bias.”

“If they wanted bias,” I say, “they wouldn’t have hired an auditor. They would have hired a notary.”

His lips twitch, almost despite himself. “You’re not here to change the law, Lysa. You’re here to apply it.”

I stand.

“Sometimes,” I say, “application requires reminding people the law exists.”

I collect my satchel, my ruined quill, what remains of my composure, and leave before he can respond.

The Sanctum doors close behind me with a heavy, organic thud. Outside, the Spine’s main corridor stretches in both directions, long and gray, full of the familiar mechanical heartbeat of my working life: the hiss and thunk of message tubes overhead, the scratch of a hundred quills in the clerks’ rooms below, the low murmur of voices arguing in legalese.

I walk fast.

Ink itches on my skin. I want to scrub, to scrape, to peel it off, but there’s no time. The marrow-light filtering through the high bone-windows is already drifting toward evening gray. Seventy-two hours. I have bought Cyran three days of not being paraded in front of men like Thessen and asked to bleed for their entertainment.

That is something. It is not nearly enough.

“Vaskin.”

The voice comes from my right, low and sharp. A hand closes lightly around my elbow, stopping me before I barrel past the side corridor.

I turn.

Scribe Meris stands half in shadow, half in the cold spill of corridor light, as if unable to fully commit to either. Her hair, gone mostly grey years ago, is coiled at the nape of her neck in a knot held by a chipped bone pin. Her spectacles hang on a heavy chain against her chest, pendulum steady as she peers up at me.

“Meris,” I say. “I’m in a hurry.”

“So I gathered.” Her gaze drops to my hands. “Ink.”

“Yes.” I flex my fingers, and the black stains flex with them. “Occupational hazard.”

She snorts softly. “Not that much. What did you do, stab Voss with your quill?”

“Tempting,” I say. “No.”

Her eyes search my face, reading it the way she reads statutes. She must see something there, some crack in the professional veneer, because her jaw tightens.

“You’re playing with fire,” she murmurs. “I saw the summons. Thessen doesn’t convene private meetings for nothing.”

“Has there ever been a time he did anything for nothing?” I ask.

“Not in my tenure.” She glances down the corridor, shoulders hitching, as if expecting Thessen himself to step out of the marrow-wall. “You push him, he pushes back. And he doesn’t use quills.”

“I know.”

“Do you?” Her voice drops. “He breaks people who mess with his ledgers, Lysa. Quietly. Neatly. Nothing left but a note in the archive that says personnel change. You still have the luxury of your name intact.”

Not for long, if I have anything to say about it.

“I can’t rubber-stamp what he wants,” I say. My voice feels like it belongs to someone else—calm, matter-of-fact, while my heart is battering against my ribs. “He’s trying to turn Cyran into a donor amenity.”

“Cyran.” She tastes the name like old news. “The Blood Relic. Of course.”

“You’ve read his file?”

“Everyone has.” She gestures vaguely in the direction of the upper stacks. “Red leather. Too many redactions. Smells like Thessen.”

“That’s… an unfortunately accurate description,” I say.

She huffs a reluctant breath that might be a laugh.

“Come here,” she says, and tugs me into the alcove at her back.

It’s small, little more than a bite taken out of the corridor where a service hatch used to be. Dust gathers in the corners. Old legal notices are nailed to the wall, their ink faded to illegibility. The hum of the building feels quieter here, as if the tubes and the clocks can’t quite reach.

Meris digs into the wide sleeve of her robe and pulls out a key.

It is old. Brass gone dull with age, teeth worn smooth in places. The head of it is shaped like a stylised jawbone, delicate etching picked out along the curve.

She presses it into my ink-smeared palm.

“The Marrow Archives,” she says. “Lower Halo Ward. Back stacks.”

I stare at the key, then at her. “That section’s restricted.”

“Yes.” Her mouth twists. “To people with that key.”

“Meris—”

“Don’t thank me.” She glances over her shoulder again, tongues of anxiety licking at the corners of her composure. “Just… find a precedent. If you’re going to bleed for this boy, you’d better have something sharper than righteous anger in your hand when they come for you.”

“I—” My throat closes for a second. “Why?”

“Because I’m too old to fight that fight anymore,” she says, with a tight little shrug. “And you’re not. Don’t make me regret it.”

The key is heavier than it looks. It bites into my stained palm.

I close my fingers around it.

“I won’t,” I say.

She nods once, jaw set, then steps past me into the corridor, spectactles swinging. By the time I step out after her, she’s already vanished into the flow of scribes.

I stand there for a heartbeat, old dust in my nose, new brass in my hand, and let the weight of what I’ve just decided settle.

Seventy-two hours.

Three days to prove that what they want to do to Cyran is illegal, immoral, and lethal.

Three days to dig through bones both literal and legal and find something sharp enough to cut a Contract Lord.

I tuck the key into my inner pocket, feel its chill against my ribs, and start walking.

The Spine’s transport capsule is a bone cylinder big enough to swallow a dozen people at a time, suspended on chains and guided by rails that vanish into the lower districts. I ride it alone, hands shoved into my sleeves, ink itching, Meris’s warning echoing alongside the memory of Cyran leaning against an alley wall, smirking through his own pain as he bowed to a terrified mother.

By the time the capsule shudders to a stop in the Vein Markets, the marrow-mist outside has thickened into something that almost passes for rain. It sticks to my hair and lashes in fine, needling droplets as I step out, soaking into the hem of my robes.

The Vein is everything the Spine is not—loud, hot, chaotic. Lanterns burn low in the encroaching gloom, casting everything in shades of red and sickly gold. The air tastes of frying oil, cheap incense, and the faint, electric tang of spilled god-blood from some unlicensed shrine further down.

Cyran’s building rises from the market like a polished bone implanted into a broken jaw. The entrance is guarded, not by men with spears, but by the absence of anyone fool enough to loiter too close. Everyone knows who lives in these apartments. Who owns them.

Who owns him.

My boots thud on the narrow stair. By the third flight, my temples are pounding in time with my pulse. By the fifth, my ink-stained hand has balled into a fist around the cloth I’m using to wipe it, as if I can erase the evidence that I was ever in that room with Thessen, listening quietly while he discussed my audit subject like a new piece of furniture.

I knock on Cyran’s door once. Sharp. No hesitation.

A beat.

Then the door opens on a wash of warmth and soft, expensive light.

He fills the frame like a stage entrance.

Gone is the Vigil armour and grim leather. Instead, he’s in a loose silk shirt, half-unbuttoned, sleeves rolled up his forearms. His feet are bare on the polished floor. His hair is unbound, a pale, ridiculous halo for a man who insists he belongs in shadows.

In one hand, he holds a glass of red wine. Of course.

His mask is firmly in place: that indolent, dangerous charm he wears for donors and commanders alike. The smile he gives me is perfectly measured—pleased, a little mocking, just enough teeth.

“Investigator,” he drawls. “To what do I owe the pleasure? Another surprise inspection? I should have tidied.”

The room behind him glows in the rich, muted way only money can buy. Velvet drapes. Low chairs. A fire banked in the hearth, more for ambience than heat. It looks nothing like the city outside his walls.

It looks like a cage someone tried to make comfortable.

“I have news,” I say.

He lifts the glass a fraction, gesturing me in. “That sounds ominous. Come in, then. We’ll dress it up with alcohol.”

“I’m not staying.” I step past him into the room without waiting for an invitation, because if I stand on his threshold with that glass and that smile, my resolve will soften around the edges.

He closes the door with a soft click.

“Now I’m wounded,” he says. “You storm in here looking like the wrath of some vengeful clerk god, and then you won’t even sit down. At least let me offer you—”

“Thessen has proposed sanctioned donor events.”

The words land between us like a dropped stone.

His smile freezes. For a heartbeat, his whole body goes still, glass suspended halfway to his lips. All the pretty lines of indolence and invitation vanish, as if someone has drawn a thumb across wet ink.

“What,” he says.

I don’t soften it. I won’t. I cross to the center of the room, plant my feet on the expensive rug, and face him squarely.

“Sanctioned donor galas,” I repeat. “Wealthy patrons paying the Tribunal for access to… intimate feeding experiences. You as the main attraction.”

The glass tilts in his hand, just enough that the wine kisses the rim, then settles.

I watch the words hit him.

He doesn’t flinch. That would be too honest. Instead, the charm returns all at once, like a door slamming.

“Ah,” he says lightly. “Of course. A gala. How festive. Will there be candlelight? Music? Perhaps a little engraved card with my market rate so no one feels cheated?”

His voice is smooth. His eyes are not.

“I invoked a seventy-two hour ethical review,” I say. “They can’t move forward until it’s complete.”

He turns his back on me.

It’s such a small motion, and it feels like someone has just shut a door in my face.

He walks to the sideboard where the decanter sits, casual as a man topping up his drink after a long day of respectable work. The silk of his shirt shifts over the long lines of his back, catching the golden firelight. His bare feet are silent on the stone.

“Of course you did,” he says. He tips the last of his glass into his mouth, then reaches for the bottle. “How… bureaucratic of you. After all, what’s the violation of a man’s body compared to the sanctity of proper forms?”

“I didn’t agree to it,” I say. “I delayed it.”

“Delayed.” The word comes out brittle. He pours more wine with a hand that only shakes a little. “Tell me, Auditor—does the review ask whether the Relic in question might prefer not to be pimped out like a particularly pretty goat? Or does it simply calculate how many donors can fit in a room before the bloodstains become untenable?”

“It asks whether the law allows it,” I say, because if I let myself answer the question he’s actually asking, we will both splinter. “And whether it constitutes undue endangerment. I can work with that.”

He laughs.

It is not a pleasant sound. It’s too sharp, too loud in the warm, echoing room.

“Undue endangerment,” he repeats. “I stand on altars while men cut me open and call it redemption, but this—this might be undue.”

“Cyran—”

“How much is my entry fee?” he asks suddenly, still facing the hearth. His shoulders are very straight. His knuckles are white on the stem of the glass. “Do they plan to auction me? Raffle tickets? Perhaps an ongoing subscription model—feed your favourite monster monthly, get a complimentary front-row seat to his eventual breakdown.”

The words are a blade. They don’t touch me; he isn’t aiming them at me.

They still cut.

I force my voice to stay level. “I am not signing it,” I say. “I am not authorising this, and I am not going to let them blindside you with it in some public ceremony. That’s why I’m here.”

He goes very still.

Slowly, as if it costs him something, he turns back to face me.

His eyes are not theatrical now. They are empty in a way that sets off every quiet alarm in my body.

“And what, precisely,” he says, “do you call this? Because from where I’m standing, it looks rather a lot like a blindside.”

“I told you the moment I walked in,” I say. “I didn’t wait for you to hear it through gossip. I didn’t—”

“You came from them,” he snaps. The volume rises for the first time, a crack in the polished surface. “You sat in a room with Thessen and Voss and listened while they discussed where best to put my mouth for maximum profit, and then you came here to tell me you filed the correct form.”

His pace picks up as he speaks, carrying him across the room in a slow, circling track that keeps me always at the center of his orbit and always at just enough distance to make closing it feel dangerous.

“You think I enjoyed that?” I demand. “You think I—”

“I think you are very good at what you do,” he bites out. “I think you know exactly which lines of the law to underline in your beautiful neat hand. I think you are part of the machine that owns me, whether you like it or not.”

“Stop.” I take a step forward. He does not back away. “You don’t get to put me on their side because it’s easier than admitting I’m trying to pull you off their ledgers.”

His mouth twists.

“Are you?” he asks, so soft it barely counts as sound. “Because from where I’m standing, you’re holding the ledger. You’re the one they sent. You’re the one who gets to decide whether I’m profitable enough to keep.”

The words land like blows, because they are not wrong. Because I have sat in rooms and written things that turned into punishment sigils and revoked Marks and people screaming under bone-gongs.

“If I wanted to be part of that,” I say quietly, “I would have signed the proposal and gone home.”

He inhales. It’s jagged.

“A delay doesn’t change the ending,” he says. “It just gives them time to pick their outfits.”

He lifts the glass in a mock-toast. Red wine sloshes against crystal.

“You’re angry,” I say.

He bares his teeth. “How astute.”

“Good,” I say. “You should be.”

That throws him.

He blinks, thrown off the script he clearly expected me to follow. “Pardon?”

“You should be furious,” I say, stepping into his circling path, forcing him to either break it or walk through me. “You should rage and throw things and call them every name you know. They are trying to put you back in the exact cage you fought your way out of.”

“That’s very stirring,” he says. “Will you write that down in triplicate for me?”

“Stop it.”

“Stop what?” he demands. “Stop seeing clearly? Stop noticing that every time you draw a line in the sand, they just move the tide in a little closer? You bought me seventy-two hours, love. In three days, they still get their gala. You still get your desk. I still get to be the main course.”

“Do you think I don’t know the cost?” I shoot back. “Do you think I’m not staking my career on this? Thessen all but told me he’ll break me if I get in the way of his profits.”

“And you stayed anyway.” There is something almost like wonder in his voice, quickly curdled into bitterness. “Tell me, Auditor. When you negotiated this valiant delay, did you also negotiate your cut? Or just mine?”

The room goes very quiet.

Heat flushes up my neck. Not shame. Fury.

The glass in his hand trembles.

“Say that again,” I say.

“Did you negotiate your cut?” he repeats, louder now, knife-sharp. “You’re risking an awful lot of ink for me. It would be terribly inefficient if you didn’t at least get a slice of the ticket sales.”

Something in my chest snaps.

“I will not,” I say, very softly, “let you rewrite me into your abusers because it’s easier than admitting someone might actually care whether you live or die.”

His jaw clenches. “Care,” he says. “That’s a generous word for someone who came here with a status update.”

“I came here with the truth,” I snap. “Because I promised you I wouldn’t be one more person who lied to you for your own good.”

He laughs again, harsh and ugly. “And I suppose I should fall to my knees in gratitude for your brutal honesty.”

“You’re allowed to be angry with me,” I say. “You’re not allowed to accuse me of selling you.”

“Everyone sells me,” he snarls. “The temple sold me to their gods. The Tribunal sells me to the city. Why should you be any different?”

“Because I said no,” I fire back. “When you offered yourself to me like a favour, I said no. When they waved this parchment under my nose, I said no. Do you understand how rare that word is in this building?”

He stares at me.

For a second, something like hurt flickers behind his eyes. Then he hurls the wine glass at the hearth.

It hits the stone with a crystalline shriek and explodes, shards and red wine arcing out in a violent halo. Wine spatters the grey stone like blood, runs in thin rivulets toward the grate. A stray shard skitters to a stop near my boot.

I do not flinch.

His chest is heaving. Fangs dimly show between his lips, bright against the flush that has risen under his pallor. His hands are empty and shaking.

“I have fought for two centuries,” he roars. “I have obeyed every order, stood on every altar, smiled for every donor. It always ends the same.”

I stand my ground.

“I am not them,” I say. My voice is quiet, but it carries. “And I am not giving up.”

“You should,” he says. “You should walk out that door and forget you ever saw my file. You could still have a nice, unremarkable career making neat notes about other people’s tragedies.”

“No.”

“You will lose,” he insists. “They will take my body, and they will take your job, and then you will have nothing to show for it but broken glass and a handful of principles.”

“Then we lose together,” I say.

The words hang there, stark and terrifying.

He blinks, thrown.

“Together,” he repeats, as if testing the shape of it on his tongue.

“Yes.” My heart is hammering so hard it hurts. I take another step closer, until there is barely a hand’s breadth between us. I have to tilt my head back to hold his gaze. “Us. Against them. Not you against me.”

His breath ghosts hot against my cheek. The room feels suddenly too small, overheated by the fire and the argument and the sheer pressure of his presence. I can smell the wine on his breath, the faint metallic tang of his own blood where he must have bitten his tongue.

“Do you have any idea,” he murmurs, “how dangerous that sounds?”

“Yes.”

“Do you have any idea,” he says, voice dropping, “how many times I’ve heard some variation of, I’m not like them, right before someone tightens the chain?”

“Yes.” I swallow. “And I am still saying it.”

We stand there, chest to chest, breath mingling, both of us vibrating with something too large and jagged to name.

He is waiting for me to leave. I can see it in the tightness around his eyes, the way his hands keep flexing, as if bracing for the feel of the door closing behind me.

I don’t move.

Slowly, very slowly, the fight starts to bleed out of his shoulders. His hands sag open at his sides. His gaze drops from my eyes to my mouth, then back up, as if embarrassed he looked.

“You should go,” he says, finally, but there is no conviction in it. “Before I say something cruel we can’t unsay.”

“You already did,” I say. “I’m still here.”

He lets out a sound that might be a laugh if it weren’t so tired.

“I don’t know what to do with that,” he admits.

“Good,” I say. “Maybe that means we’re finally off your script.”

For a moment, neither of us speaks.

Outside, somewhere in the Vein, a bell tolls the hour. In the Spine above us, Voss is likely drafting memoranda. Thessen is polishing his teeth on the thought of profit. The city keeps grinding along, bone on bone.

In here, there is nothing but wine on stone, glass on the hearth, ink on my hands, and the man who has just shown me the ugliest part of his anger and is still standing close enough to touch.

“We have seventy-two hours,” I say. “I have a key to the Marrow Archives. There might be something there we can use to stop this.”

“We,” he repeats, even softer.

“Yes,” I say again. “We.”

His eyes close briefly, as if the word physically hurts.

When he opens them, they are still dark and wounded. But there is something else there too, something fragile and impossible that looks very much like trust.

“Then,” he says, “I suppose we should make them count.”

I nod once.

The glass still glitters in the corner of my eye, red and broken. The ledger in my satchel weighs heavier than it did this morning. The ink on my hands has dried to a tight film, black in all the places the law likes to pretend it’s clean.

They are going to try to turn him back into an ornament.

They will go through me to do it.

Seventy-two hours.

I meet his gaze and feel the shape of the next choice pressing in from all sides: the ceremony in the Gods Skull tonight, the archive key against my ribs, the fact that if I keep walking this path, there is no way back to the version of myself who thought cruelty here was ever accidental.

“Tomorrow,” I say. “I’ll be in the Skull chamber. I’ll find you after.”

His jaw tightens at the word Skull, temple ghosts flickering behind his eyes.

“Brave of you,” he murmurs. “Standing between me and a priest twice in two days. Someone should tell you that’s a terrible habit.”

“I’m sure you will,” I say.

He huffs a breath. It is not quite a laugh.

Neither of us steps back.
