They don’t want an audit. They want a brochure.

The thought slides through my head as I stand at the end of the long bone table, hands folded around my ledger, the Tribunal sigil above the door staring down like an accusing eye. The room smells of ink, tallow, and lavender. The lavender is not from the candles.

Lord Thessen smells like lavender and old money, a scent designed to mask the rot underneath.

He sits at the far end of the table in a chair that is not, technically, a throne but has certainly been informed by one. Purple velvet robes spill around him like a carefully arranged bruise. Gold rings wink at his fingers, catching the weak afternoon light slanting through the tall bone-windows. In one hand, he holds a small silver dish he’s brought with him, because of course he has. In the dish, a heap of grapes the colour of old blood.

He eats one as I speak. The wet pop of skin splitting is louder than it should be in the hush.

“In summary,” I say, keeping my eyes on the neat lines of my own handwriting instead of his mouth, “preliminary field observation indicates that Subject Cyran fulfills the parameters of his Vigil assignment with efficiency and consistency. Documented complaints appear to be contextual rather than indicative of systemic dereliction.”

My tongue wants to curl around a different word than contextual. Exploitative, perhaps. Vindictive. But words like that don’t live in preliminary reports.

At least, not if you want to keep your clearance.

High Inquisitor Voss sits to Thessens right—of course he does—hands folded on the table, expression mild. The two junior scribes at his flanks are trying too hard to look invisible, quills poised above their parchment, eyes down. No one else speaks.

My voice sounds very calm in my own ears. That’s how I know I’m angry.

“Complaints from Contract Lord Thessen regarding unbecoming conduct,” I continue, “have been partially substantiated in terms of reported behaviours.” That is as far as I will go toward calling Cyran’s transactional flirtations what they are. “However, there is insufficient evidence at this stage to conclude that Subject poses an elevated risk to civilian safety beyond that inherent in his role.”

Another grape. Pop. Wet chew.

Voss nods slowly, as if I have confirmed something he decided before I walked in.

“Excellent,” he says. “Very good, Investigator. Your neutral phrasing will be… reassuring to the donor committee.”

Neutral phrasing. Reassuring. The language of invisible knives.

One of the juniors writes something in the margins of his minutes that I can’t see. I focus instead on the tiny smear of ink on the side of my own index finger, where my quill had blotted earlier. Anchor points. Small realities.

“We can proceed with the Donor Gala on schedule, then,” Voss adds, turning the page of the document in front of him as if he’s discussing catering, not a person’s future.

My pen stills. I look up.

“Gala?” I ask, before I can remind myself that questions in this room are invitations to be managed.

Thessen smiles.

It is not a pleasant expression. Too many teeth, too little humour.

“An initiative of the Redemption Committee,” he says smoothly, as though I haven’t been stamping their paperwork for the last three years. “Wealthy patrons like to see what their contributions support. It encourages continued investment in our… shared project.”

His eyes are pale as old parchment, shot through with lines of tired blue. They rest on me only briefly before drifting to the red spine of Cyran’s file where it sits open near Voss’s elbow.

“The Blood Relic,” he continues, rolling the title with practiced ease, “is a potent incentive.”

A grape squishes between his fingers as he picks it up. Juice runs down to the first knuckle before he pops it into his mouth.

For a heartbeat, I forget how to swallow.

“You’re proposing,” I say carefully, “to showcase a highly trained Vigil asset at a social function. With civilian donors present.”

“Not proposing,” he corrects, in that gentle tone rich men use when they want you to feel childish. “Confirming. The wheels are already in motion. We merely needed your—” a little wave of the grape dish “—stamp.”

My stamp. My seal. My official, neutral assurance that everything is in order.

In my mind, I see Cyran in his quarters. Silk shirt half-open. Mirrors. Wine. “I’m told I’m excellent payment.”

The line slots into place with horrifying ease.

“The file,” I say, and have to pause to sand the edges off my voice, “indicates volatility.”

“A touch of danger adds to the allure,” Thessen replies. “Our donors lead such dull lives, Investigator. Accounts to balance, contracts to sign, quotas to meet. They respond better to… visceral incentives.”

Another grape. Another wet pop.

He is talking about Cyran the way I’ve heard stock-breeders talk about prize animals. Temperament. Show potential. Market value.

I keep my face exactly where it’s supposed to be—serious, attentive, untroubled by anything as unprofessional as revulsion.

“With respect,” I say, “Subject Cyran has already fulfilled three decades of high-risk service. Subjection to further unsanctioned stressors may compromise his efficacy.”

There. That should be harmless enough. I haven’t said exploitation. I haven’t said abuse. I’ve just suggested that overworking your weapon dulls the edge.

Voss makes a small, considering noise. Thessen looks unbothered.

“His efficacy,” Thessen says, “is not diminished by being seen, Investigator. Quite the opposite. Our Relics are symbols of the city’s strength. Hiding them away in patrol routes and rat-tunnels while our benefactors sign their names in the dark does no one any favours.”

“The Redemption system is funded,” Voss adds mildly, “by those benefactors. It is in all our interests that they remain… satisfied.”

Satisfied.

The word sits on the table between us like something that should be covered with a cloth.

I tighten my grip on the ledger. My knuckles go white under the ink smudges.

“I understood the audit to be a review of his suitability for continued field deployment,” I say. “Not a marketing campaign.”

“Everything is a campaign, in the end,” Thessen replies. “Don’t look so distressed, Investigator. He was designed for this.”

Designed. As if Cyran had been sketched in the margins of some forgotten rites manual with this exact outcome in mind—body as lure, blade, spectacle.

Somewhere under my ribs, something cold curls tighter.

Voss clears his throat. “Your role,” he reminds me, in his schoolmaster’s tone, “is to provide clear, unbiased assessments. You have done so admirably. What the Redemption Committee chooses to do with that assessment is… beyond the scope of your duties.”

Beyond the scope. Convenient phrase. It means you’re still implicated, but they won’t let you see where the bodies are buried.

“Understood,” I say.

The word tastes like ink and ash.

“Very good,” Voss says, already looking past me to the juniors. “We’ll reconvene after the first donor event to incorporate any feedback into the final report. You’re dismissed, Investigator Vaskin.”

Dismissed. Not thanked. Not engaged. Simply removed from the room like a clerk whose job of ferrying numbers from one column to another is complete.

I nod once. I do not bow.

As I turn, Thessen’s voice follows me, lazy as smoke.

“Do make sure he’s properly polished before the Gala, won’t you?” he says. “The right words on a page can make even the most… temperamental asset very appealing.”

I don’t trust myself to answer.

The door closes behind me with a soft, precise click. The lavender fades, thinned by the Spine’s usual smells—dusty bone, old ink, stale tallow.

My legs carry me down the corridor on autopilot. The Tribunal architecture looms overhead: vertebra-arches, carved pillars, the occasional hairline crack spidering down a wall that maintenance reports will call minor and superficial. Office doors blur past. The hallway is full of ambient noise—quills scratching, message tubes sighing, distant footsteps—but no one seems to be going anywhere in particular. Everyone is simply… processing.

Like the building itself.

I stop at the first junction where I’m temporarily out of sight of anyone who matters and press my back to the wall, hardness of bone seeping through my robes.

My hands are shaking.

I look at them as though they belong to someone else. Ink-stained fingers, the faint red mark on my palm from where I’d gripped the bandage too hard wrapping Cyran’s arm last night. I remember the heat of his skin under my hands, the way he’d thrown himself in front of a child without thinking, the bewilderment when I’d been angrier about his risk than his success.

I remember his offhand, defensive little line: I don’t bite without invitation.

Then consider this a permanent lack of invitation.

I’d thought, in that moment, that I was drawing a clean line. Detaching desire from duty. Protecting us both.

Now I can see the line Thessen is drawing instead, thick as rope, looping back around Cyran’s throat. Temple rites, donor galas, sanctioned “incentives.”

Your body isn’t payment I’ll accept, I’d told him.

Mine isn’t the only acceptance that matters.

I push away from the wall before my legs decide to fold.

My office is down two flights and one echoing hall, past the clerks room where the pneumatic message tubes wheeze and thunk like mechanical arteries. The smell of iron-gall ink and cold tallow hits me like a familiar, suffocating blanket.

Kess looks up as I pass the threshold. She has her usual ledger open on the corner of my desk, which is her preferred method of loitering.

“Well?” she says, bright-eyed. “Is it true about the Gala? They say Lord Thessen is bringing actual sun-fruit this time, from the Green Spine. For the donors. And that the Blood Relic is going to—”

“That will be all, Kess,” I say.

Quiet. Precise. The words land with more force than if I’d raised my voice.

She blinks, taken aback. “I was only—”

“I know.” I soften it a fraction, enough that the sting is about the topic, not her. “I need the D-series donor-relation guidelines. Current edition.”

Her brows climb. “Those are restricted.”

“So is this audit,” I remind her. “Fetch them, please.”

She hesitates, then scurries off.

I lower myself into my chair. The wood creaks under me, complaining. My oil lamp is already lit—someone must have come by earlier and struck it for me—but the flame is weak, guttering in the draft. It’s always weaker on days when Voss’s office smells of bergamot and things move in directions they shouldn’t.

I should open a clean page. I should write what just happened into the record in neutral, bloodless sentences that can survive being read aloud in rooms that smell like lavender.

Instead, I stare at the red spine of Cyran’s file where it sits on the corner of my desk, corner scuffed from too much handling.

I flip it open to the section marked DISPOSITION RECOMMENDATIONS.

Capacity for independent function limited.

Recommended disposition pending threshold review.

They built the trap years ago. All we’re doing now is dropping the bait into it and calling it reform.

My left wrist itches. I rub at the Tribunal mark there through my sleeve—a little circle of ink that once meant something like purpose and now feels uncomfortably akin to ownership.

I could march back up to that briefing room. I could say the words I didn’t say: This is sanctioned abuse. This is accessory to exploitation. The temple didn’t use him, Lord Thessen. They abused him. Let’s call it what it was.

I know exactly what would happen if I did. Best case: reassignment. Worst case: my own file, redacted in all the same places, someone else standing at the end of a table writing contextual while I’m paraded at some other kind of gala.

The law is a cage. It’s also the only weapon I’ve trained my whole life to use.

I stand.

The chair scrapes back. The sound is too loud in the small office. I pull my satchel toward me, tuck my ledger inside with more care than it deserves, and blow out the lamp.

I need to get out of the Spine before I suffocate on good intentions and old lies.

The transport capsule smells faintly of mildew and stale breath.

I step in, alone. The bone door slides shut with a pneumatic hiss, sigils along the frame pulsing as the mechanism engages. The capsule lurches, then begins its descent through the gods carcass, down from the bureaucratic vertebrae toward the pulsing underbelly of the Vein Markets.

The ride is short but disorienting. Bone tunnels flicker past the narrow viewing slit, lit intermittently by embedded glow-stones. We pass the level where the Vigil barracks squat like barnacles; the level where the Redemption Halls’ punishment sigils hum a faint, punitive blue; finally, the level where light changes.

The Vein Markets don’t glow like the Spine. They burn.

By the time the capsule doors hiss open, the air is thicker, warmer, crowded with smells that have nothing to do with ink. Frying oil. Spiced marrow. Sweat. Cheap perfume. The underlying, constant tang of god-blood, less tidy down here, less controlled.

Noise hits next. Laughter. Shouting. The clatter of dice, the rise and fall of some off-key song, the deeper thrum of drums beating time for bodies pressed too close.

I step out into it and feel my shoulders drop half an inch despite myself.

The Hollow King’s Tavern is tucked into the curve of a rib not far from the main market thoroughfare, its sign a cleverly carved crown perched at a jaunty angle over a skull. Someone has painted the skull’s eye sockets with glow-pigment so they watch the street with a lurid amber stare.

Inside, the light is lower, almost intimate. Lanterns hang from the ribcage overhead, their glass smoked and streaked, turning everything a warm, dirty gold. The floor is uneven bone, patched with worn planks where the marrow gave out. Tables huddle together like conspirators. Every surface is sticky.

Perfect.

I pause just inside, letting my eyes adjust. Letting the smells recalibrate. Marrow-whiskey, spilled beer, old smoke layered over new. The press of people is immediate—dock workers, Vein merchants, off-duty wardens in half-unlaced uniforms, a smattering of Tribunal grey at the periphery trying to look like they belong.

No one looks twice at me.

That is a lie. A few people do. I feel their glances snag on the cut of my indigo robes, the glint of my spectacles, the way I stand too straight for a place like this.

But no one challenges me. Not yet.

I make for a corner table that still has four legs and as little grime as possible and claim it with the placement of my satchel. The wood sticks faintly to my palm. My robes pull at my knees when I sit.

A server appears at my elbow, a tall woman with scarred knuckles and a bored expression.

“Marrow-ale, whiskey, blood-wine?” she rattles off. “We’ve got the cheap stuff and the pretending-not-to-be-cheap stuff.”

“Whiskey,” I say. “The kind people order when they want to taste it.”

Her brows lift a fraction. “Leave the bottle?”

“Yes.”

She gives a little shrug that might mean anything and melts back into the crowd.

I unclench my fingers from around the strap of my satchel and place my hands flat on the table instead. The grain is rough under my palms, old grooves worn by tankards, knives, fists.

On the far side of the room, someone laughs.

It is a ridiculous sound—bright, bell-like, utterly at odds with the grime. I turn my head.

The first thing I see is light.

Pink, the kind no natural flame produces, limning the edges of a figure currently dancing on the bar.

They are slight and almost too pretty for this place, limbs long and loose, hair a tumble of pale curls catching the glow. Their skin has a faint, unnatural sheen, as though dusted with some phosphorescent powder. They move like water poured through a cracked glass, spilling over the edges of the space allotted to them and reforming lower down.

Coins are being tucked into their belt, their boots, the low neckline of their shirt. Hands reach up; they twist away with practiced ease, smiling, never quite letting anyone catch hold.

“Seraph,” someone near me mutters fondly. “Gonna break their neck one of these nights.”

Behind the bar, a man scowls up at the performer as he polishes a glass with the kind of thoroughness that suggests he’d rather be strangling the person responsible for dirtying it.

He is built like a man who has been hit many times and hit things back more. Broad shoulders. Dark hair pulled into a short tail at the nape of his neck. His left forearm is a lattice of white scars where something with teeth once decided to make a meal of him and failed. His shirt sleeves are rolled up, forearms corded, hands steady.

Cairn, I assume. I’ve seen his name on a handful of incident reports—bar fights, mostly, all resolved with minimal collateral damage and no bodies. A model citizen, if you squint and ignore the fact that he runs precisely the sort of establishment the Tribunal pretends not to patronize.

He tosses a damp rag upward with a flick of his wrist. It arcs neatly through the air and lands over Seraph’s head like a shroud.

“Down,” he grunts. His voice carries even over the din. “You’re scaring the regulars.”

Seraph peels the rag off with exaggerated affront. “They love me,” they protest, spinning it around one finger before dropping lightly to the floor with a cats ease. “You’re just jealous I get more tips than you.”

“I get paid to keep idiots from falling down the stairs,” Cairn replies. “Not to watch you wiggle.”

“You could admit you enjoy the show,” Seraph says, leaning over the bar, glowing mouth close to his ear. “Just a little. For morale.”

Cairn’s ears redden. He glares at the glass as though it personally offends him.

“Pour something or shut up,” he says.

Seraph laughs again, softer now, and swans away into the crowd, pink glow trailing.

They look… free, in a way that makes my teeth ache. Or at least honest about the chains they’ve chosen. The currency they’re trading is visible here, unsubtle. You pay with coin, attention, a little dignity. No one pretends it’s anything else.

The bottle arrives with two chipped glasses. The server slaps them down on my table and names a price that would make a junior clerk choke. I count coins into her palm without flinching. Small corrections. The only kind available to me right now.

She leaves. I pour.

The whiskey is the colour of varnished bone. It smells of smoke and something faintly sweet, like burnt sugar. I take a sip.

It hits like clarity.

Fire tracks down my throat, bloomed out into my chest. My pulse slows, then evens. The knot between my shoulders loosens half a fraction.

Around me, the tavern heaves and breathes. A warden at the next table slams his cup down and makes a joke about marrow-rations. A woman in a beaded headscarf argues amiably with a man over the odds of a god-spawn outbreak in the upper Wreath. Seraph drapes themself over the back of Cairn’s barstool; Cairn shrugs them off with the tired patience of a man who has long ago lost the battle and continues fighting it on principle.

The world keeps turning.

My world has just tilted on a new axis.

They don’t want an audit. They want a brochure. A stamped, sealed assurance that the monster they plan to parade in front of wealthy donors will bare his teeth on command and not at anyone important.

I take another swallow. The whiskey goes down easier this time.

A bulk of a man stumbles past my table, jostled by someone else’s argument. His shoulder clips mine hard enough to slosh whiskey over the rim of my glass.

“Watch it,” he grunts, then actually looks at me. His eyes narrow. “You lost, quill-pusher?”

The term would be insulting if it weren’t accurate.

I set my glass down, wipe my fingers on the edge of my sleeve—another ink stain to join the collection—and meet his gaze levelly.

“Just thirsty,” I say. “Problem?”

He holds my stare for a moment too long, waiting for the flinch, the tell. Tribunal types in places like this usually apologize, or simper, or flee.

I don’t move.

His attention flicks to the ledger strap peeking from my satchel, the cut of my robes, the plainness of my face. He snorts.

“Your funeral,” he mutters, and lurches away.

My heart is beating a shade faster than before. I take that information, file it, and return my attention to my drink.

If Thessen owns the Tribunal—and today has made that feel less like hyperbole and more like a line item—then the only way to block him is with the one thing he still needs from us: procedure.

He needs my signature. My wording. My neutral report that isn’t, in fact, neutral at all if I can help it.

I can’t stop the Gala. Not openly. The machine is already moving. But I can lace the paperwork with enough conditions, caveats, and precedent that when the time comes to use Cyran the way Thessen wants, the law will be there like a tripwire.

Restricted capacities. Duty-of-care clauses. Historical cases where donor contact with high-risk assets resulted in liability for the Contract Lord, not the Tribunal.

They taught me the codes to keep the system running. No one said I couldn’t use them to jam its gears.

On the far side of the room, Seraph climbs back onto a table, barefoot now, leading a raucous toast to something I can’t quite hear. Cairn rolls his eyes and keeps pouring drinks. Someone plays a mournful tune on a bone-fiddle, the notes threading through the laughter.

It’s messy. Loud. Imperfect.

It’s honest about the ways people use each other and the ways they don’t.

I toss back the rest of my glass. The whiskey burns, but it sharpens the edges instead of blurring them.

I pour one more measure. I don’t drink it.

On the table, beside my hand, the condensation ring my first glass left has already dried into a faint, uneven circle. Imperfect but present. Evidence that I was here.

I take three coins from my pouch and leave them under the bottle’s base—more than the remainder of the whiskey is worth. Payment for the space, perhaps. For the reminder that there are places in this city where people are allowed to choose their own chains.

Cyran has never had that.

When I stand, my legs are steady. The room tilts for a heartbeat, then rights itself.

On my way out, I pass close enough to the bar to hear Cairn say, in that gravelly deadpan of his, “If you fall off that table and crack your head, I’m not cleaning it up.”

“Liar,” Seraph purrs. “You’d mop up every drop.”

I step back into the street before I can hear his answer.

Vein-smog curls around me, thick and warm. Above, the dead god’s ribs arc overhead, strung with lanterns just starting to flare brighter as the afternoon leans toward night. Somewhere up there, beyond layers of bone and bureaucracy, the Spine’s windows reflect a different kind of light.

I draw my satchel strap higher on my shoulder and start walking toward the transport capsule.

To save Cyran, I can’t be the person who shouts in meetings and gets escorted out. I have to be the one who smiles politely, writes contextual, and quietly builds a cage of words that Thessen can’t see until he crashes into it.

I speak the language of neutral bureaucracy. Today it tastes like ash.

Tomorrow, I’ll make it taste like justice.
