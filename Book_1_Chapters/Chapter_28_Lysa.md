# Chapter 28 — *The Note on the Pillow*
### Lysa | POV

The bed is cold.

That’s the first betrayal.

Not the worst I’ve ever suffered—those tend to involve tribunals and contracts and gods—but it lands in the same place. In my ribs. In the soft, foolish part of me that, last night, let itself unclench.

I come awake slow.

For once.

No alarm spell, no pounding at the door, no panicked runner from Vigil Administration. Just the spill of thin morning light through the narrow window, the creak of bone as the Spine settles, the faint scratch of quills from the scribe’s dormitory two doors down.

And warmth.

Residual.

Across my stomach, my thighs, between my legs.

The other side of the mattress is indented where his body should be.

My hand reaches for him before my brain catches up.

Fingers close on empty linen.

The spot where his chest was—solid, cool‑warm in that inhuman way—has already gone flat and chill.

I blink at the ceiling.

Fine cracks run through the carved legal text there—precedents etched above every Tribunal sleeping pallet. My own addition, the one I liked enough to cut myself, sits over my pillow: *Consent given under duress is void.*

Reciting it, silently, usually settles me.

Not now.

My stomach drops.

“Cyran?” It comes out rough, half‑sleep, mouth dry.

No answer.

Of course there’s no answer.

The room is too still.

My braid pulls at the back of my neck as I jerk upright. The sheet slides down, cool air licking sweat‑damp skin, reminding me exactly how unclothed I am. Bruises are forming on my hips already where his hands held me.

Heat flushes my face.

It has nothing to do with embarrassment.

I scan the room.

Quill‑littered desk.

Stacked case files.

Robes thrown carelessly over the back of my chair. His shirt—a ruined, road‑dusty thing—on the floor where I’d shoved it off his shoulders. Two wine cups on the window ledge, one knocked sideways and dripping a thin trail of red down the stone.

The door is shut.

The latch is set.

He’s gone.

There’s a square of white on his side of the pillow.

For a second, my brain refuses to name it.

I stare.

The city creaks around me, vertebrae settling, someone shouting faintly in the hall. My heart thuds once, hard enough to hurt.

Then I reach.

My hand shakes.

Ink stains stand out dark on my fingers against the pale fold of paper.

The note isn’t sealed.

Of course it isn’t.

He’s not a letter writer.

He’s a performer.

A disappearing act.

I unfold it.

There are only seven words, cramped and uneven as if writing them cost him more than any speech he’s ever made on a stage.

*I won’t be the thing that destroys you.*

That’s it.

No signature.

He doesn’t need one.

My throat goes hot and tight.

Not with tears.

Tears require a kind of softness I can’t afford for more than a breath at a time.

This is something else.

Rage.

Not the bright, theatrical kind he uses when charming an audience. Mine is colder. Denser. A weight behind the sternum that makes every inhale feel measured.

“I see,” I say aloud.

My voice is very calm.

Very even.

I fold the note again, precisely along the same crease he made.

My hands are steady now.

That’s worse than the shaking.

He thinks he’s poison.

He thinks he’s sparing me.

He thinks he knows better than I do what I can survive.

Of course he does.

The Temple taught him that everyone else’s will matters more than his. That his body, his wants, his *no* are all irrelevant against an order. When he loves, he writes himself out of the equation.

It’s familiar.

Abusers and martyrs use the same grammar.

I stare at the folded paper a heartbeat longer.

Then my body moves.

I swing my legs out of bed.

The stone is cold under my bare feet, the thin layer of bone dust grinding against my skin. A quill has rolled off the desk; I nudge it aside with my toes, not gently. My robe lies crumpled near the chair. I step over it.

There’s a clay vase on the shelf by the door.

A gift from Scribe Vesper, years ago. “So your quarters look less like an interrogation cell,” she’d said, dumping a bundle of dried marrow‑flowers into it. I’d kept it mostly because I didn’t know what else to do with something decorative.

It’s been sitting there long enough I don’t consciously see it anymore.

Now I do.

My fingers uncurl from the note.

They curl around the cool curve of the vase’s neck instead.

For a flicker of a second, I think I might set it gently on the floor. A conscious act of restraint.

I don’t.

The weight leaves my hand before I know I’ve decided.

It hits the far wall with a satisfying, explosive crack.

Clay shards burst outward, skittering across stone. Dried flowers, brittle from neglect, shatter underfoot. One sharp fragment bounces back and slices a thin line along my ankle.

I barely feel it.

“Idiot,” I say.

Not a scream.

Not a howl.

Just one precise word flung into the empty room, sharp enough to cut.

I stand there, chest heaving once, twice.

Then everything slots into place.

They moved the hearing up.

He walked out just before dawn.

He left seven words and a cold pillow.

He thinks surrender will satisfy Thessen, Varos, the Prime Contract, like any of them will stop once they smell blood on someone this rare.

He thinks giving himself up will erase my file from their ledgers.

He thinks my consent is negotiable.

I bend, pick up the note from where it fell near my toes.

The paper has a smear of clay dust now, gray smudging the black ink.

I crumple it into my fist.

Not neatly this time.

Not with care.

The edges bite into my palm as I squeeze until the joints in my fingers ache.

Then I move.

Dressing is less methodical than usual.

No careful smoothing, no checking seams for telltale loose threads. I drag my shift over my head, yank on the cleanest robe I can find, belt it hard enough the knot protests. I don’t bother rebraiding my hair; I twist it once and stab a pen through to hold it up, ink‑sticky barrel digging into my scalp.

My badge is where I left it on the desk, next to an open case file.

I strap it to my wrist with quick, practiced motions.

The metal kisses my skin, cold as a geased promise.

I catch sight of myself in the distorted reflection on its surface.

Eyes bloodshot.

Jaw set.

There’s a bruise forming along my throat where his mouth was last night.

I touch it.

Just once.

Then I turn away.

I can’t stay here.

They’ll come looking for me soon enough. Tribunal couriers, Temple representatives, polite invitations remastered as threats. If I’m in my quarters when they knock, they get to dictate the timing.

If I’m already three steps ahead, they’re chasing my shadow.

I shove the door open.

The corridor outside is busier than it should be at this hour—scribes and Auditors murmuring to one another in clusters, the air thick with the metallic sting of rumor. Someone looks up as I pass. Their gaze flicks to my throat, then away, carefully blank.

I walk faster.

Down the Vertebrae steps, through the vein‑bridge toward the Vein Markets, my boots ringing on bone.

The city is damp from night rain.

Bone dust clumps underfoot, gray mud smearing the soles of my boots. The air smells of wet stone and the persistent, underlying sweetness of decay. Vendors in the lower Spine are only just setting up, pulling tarps off stalls, muttering to each other about an odd hum from the Ribcage in the night, the way the god’s heart seemed to stutter.

I keep my head down.

My badge is visible.

No one stops me.

The further I get from Tribunal territory, the looser my shoulders feel.

By the time I reach the nerve‑cable lift that carries goods and people down toward the Vein Markets, my initial spike of fury has cooled into something harder.

Not acceptance.

Never that.

Strategy.

The lift operator—a bored teenager with a patchy bone tattoo peeking out from under his collar—eyes my robes, shrugs, and pulls the lever. The cable creaks. We descend through layers of bone and light until the ordered architecture of the Spine gives way to the tangled arteries of the lower city.

The Vein Markets always smell like last night’s bad decisions.

Stale beer, spilled marrow‑wine, frying something that was probably alive this morning. The sound hits next—a low roar of haggling, laughter, shouted threats, and the occasional crash when someone’s temper or display stand gives out.

At this hour, it’s quieter.

Merchants are wiping down counters, counting coins, sweeping refuse into the gutters where the god’s lymph still trickles sluggishly, carrying away what the city doesn’t want to look at.

Cairn’s bar squats at the far end of one of the main arteries.

The Hollow King.

The sign is a slab of old bone hung from iron hooks, the carved crown above the name worn down where drunk hands have reached up to knock on it for luck. One of the lanterns above the door has gone out; the other swings gently in the residual breeze from the lift shaft, flame guttering.

The door is closed.

I push it open anyway.

The interior is dim.

Tables sit empty, chairs upended on top of them. Dust motes spin in the thin shafts of light knifing through the high, narrow windows. The smell of last night clings to the air—ale, sweat, smoke, the copper edge of a fight that didn’t quite get cleaned up before dawn.

Behind the bar, a massive figure is hunched over a stack of glasses, polishing with a rag that has seen better decades.

Cairn looks up as the door creaks.

One surviving eye—amber, ringed with old crow’s‑feet—fixes on me. The left side of his face is a map of scars, pale lines twisting down from hairline to jaw. One ear is missing its top half; the lobe has a jagged notch like something took a bite once and never gave it back.

“We’re closed,” he growls, in a voice like gravel and cheap whiskey.

“I need whiskey,” I say. “And silence.”

His gaze flicks to my badge, to the crease between my brows, to the faint mark at my throat. He takes me in the way a warden takes in a crime scene.

His mouth twists.

“Tribunal doesn’t usually drink down here before noon,” he says. “Short of staff meetings, I mean.”

“Consider this professional development,” I say.

For a moment, I think he’ll argue.

Then he shrugs.

Sets the glass down.

Reaches under the bar.

A bottle thumps onto the wood.

He pours.

The whiskey is the cheap kind—amber, harsh, the sort they serve to drunk Vigil wardens who don’t know the difference and don’t care as long as it burns.

Good.

I don’t want nuance.

I want fire.

He slides the glass toward me.

I take the stool.

My legs remember how to fold, even if the rest of me feels like splintered bone and thin resentment.

The first sip hits like a punch.

It scorches my tongue, my throat, my chest, radiating out in a rough line all the way down. I breathe through it.

My hands still shake.

I set the glass down to hide it.

The rag whispers against another glass.

Cairn watches me without looking like he’s watching me.

He’s good at that.

Two years ago, when I first came down here on business, I made the mistake of thinking he was just another bar owner who’d lucked into a prime spot. Then I watched him talk three potential brawls down with nothing but a raised eyebrow and one quiet sentence.

He has the same air Cyran does when he’s on patrol and not performing.

Predator.

Retired, but not declawed.

“Temple got him?” Cairn asks eventually.

Not *what’s wrong*.

Not *who*.

Temple.

Got.

The words click together with a tired certainty that tells me I’m not the first person to sit on this stool with this look on my face.

My fingers tighten around the glass.

“He gave himself up,” I say.

The bitterness in my voice could strip bone.

“Ah,” Cairn says.

He puts the glass down.

Puts the rag down.

Looks at me properly now.

“Noble,” he says. “Stupid.”

The words land with irritating precision.

I grit my teeth.

“He thinks he’s toxic,” I say. “That getting too close to him is a death sentence. That the best way to love someone is to disappear before they see the rot.”

“We all do,” Cairn says.

He taps the side of his throat.

His own brand—faded now but still visible under the stubble—catches the light. An old Temple mark, I’d bet money on it. His file would be interesting reading.

“It’s the brand,” he continues. “They burn it into the skin and the skull. You don’t survive long in a system that calls you monster without taking notes.”

I swallow more whiskey.

It doesn’t go down easier.

“He left a note,” I say.

Cairn lifts an eyebrow.

“Of course he did,” he says dryly. “Did he quote poetry?”

“Seven words,” I answer. “‘I won’t be the thing that destroys you.’”

Cairn’s face does something complicated.

Half amusement.

Half pity.

All recognition.

“And you believed him?” he asks.

“No,” I say.

The word surprises both of us.

I stare at the whiskey.

The amber surface trembles faintly with the tremor in my hand.

“I know precisely how dangerous he is,” I say. “To other people. To systems. To anyone who thinks calling a person property will end well. He is not dangerous to *me* in the way he thinks. I’ve read his contracts. All of them. The Temple didn’t just use him. They rewrote the meanings of words in his head. Love means obedience. Care means control. Sacrifice means silence.”

Cairn nods once.

“And walking away?” he asks.

“Walking away is noble martyrdom,” I say, voice going flatter. “The one thing that still feels like a choice when you’ve been told your whole life that you’re a weapon, not a person. If he leaves first, he can pretend it’s his decision.”

Cairn leans his elbows on the bar.

The wood creaks.

He’s a large man.

Not Cyran’s predatory grace.

A brawler’s bulk, worn down at the edges by time and debt and a lifetime of being hit more than is healthy.

“So,” he says. “He walked away. You going to let him?”

The question hits harder than the whiskey.

My first impulse is knee‑jerk adherence to autonomy.

“He made his choice,” I say.

It sounds reasonable.

Measured.

The kind of thing I’d say in a hearing when someone wants to be released from a contract they signed willingly.

Cairn snorts.

“No,” he says.

No explanation.

Just that.

My eyes narrow.

“I beg your pardon?” I ask.

“You don’t,” he says. “You’re not the begging type. You’re the type who comes in before opening hours and scares my dust into better behavior. You also like to be right. So let me help you with that.”

He taps the bar between us.

“He made *a* choice,” Cairn says. “Not *his*. Yours. There’s a difference.”

“He—”

“Decided for you that you couldn’t handle the fight,” Cairn says over me, voice still quiet but gaining an edge. “That your career, your safety, your little Tribunal nest up in the Spine are so fragile that the only way to keep them intact is to rip himself out of your life without consulting you.”

My jaw tightens.

“That is not—”

“That’s not partnership,” he says. “That’s management.”

The word rings.

Ugly.

Accurate.

I think of Cyran in the Hall of Truths, telling me to go home, that he was handling it. Of him calling me *Auditor* with that awful, performative cruelty like we were strangers again. Of last night, his hands on my body, his mouth on my throat, the way he shook.

Of the note.

I won’t be the thing that destroys you.

He framed himself as inevitable disaster.

Framed me as fragile collateral.

Erased any possibility that I might choose him *with* that risk, not in spite of it.

“You sound like you’ve rehearsed this,” I say slowly.

Cairn’s mouth twists.

“Maybe I’ve been on both ends of that table,” he says. “Maybe I walked away from someone once, all righteous and tragic, because I decided I was bad for them. Maybe it didn’t fix a damn thing for either of us. Maybe that’s none of your business.”

Fair.

He’s giving more than he owes me already.

I swirl the whiskey in my glass.

Amber waves.

“It isn’t about damsels,” he adds.

I look up.

He’s watching me with that same assessing gaze he uses on rowdy patrons—calculating capacity for harm, for self‑destruction, for change.

“You walk in here,” he says, “eyes like you watched something sacred break, badge still on, jaw stuck all stubborn. You’re not built for being left behind, scribe. You’re built for walking *into* the fire with a rulebook and a knife. So I’ll ask you again.”

He tilts his head.

“Are you a damsel,” he says, “or a partner?”

The silence after that question is louder than any bar brawl.

My fingers loosen on the glass.

I set it down carefully, the base making a dull, final sound against wood.

A damsel waits.

Lets the story happen to her.

Let’s the Temple write its version of events, lets a traumatized man decide she’s better off without him, lets the city nod sympathetically and move on.

A partner sharpens her quill.

Gathers evidence.

Kicks down doors.

I have spent three months carefully straddling a line—inside the system enough to change it, outside it enough to see the rot. I have told myself that if I just stay *reasonable*, if I play nice with Contract Lords and High Priests and Tribunal committees, I can adjust the levers without snapping the whole machine.

They moved his hearing up without notice.

They staged a show trial in a Hall supposedly built for truth.

They waved fabricated ledgers in his face with my name tucked neatly in the margins as leverage.

He responded by sacrificing himself to their narrative.

If I let that stand, I become exactly what I’ve feared since I signed my first audit: complicit.

Cairn watches my face.

Something in it must change.

Resolve has a particular weight.

It slots into old grooves.

“I’m not a damsel,” I say.

My voice is quiet.

Precise.

“No,” he says. “Didn’t think so.”

I stand up.

The stool scrapes back.

The motion feels right—like rising in a hearing to deliver a verdict.

I drop the crumpled note on the bar next to the glass.

Cairn glances at it.

Doesn’t touch it.

“Thank you for the drink,” I say.

He nods.

“Put it on the Temple’s tab,” he says dryly. “They owe me for half the repairs in this place.”

My lips twitch.

Then flatten.

I tap my badge with two fingers.

The metal is warm now.

Claimed.

“I’m the Auditor,” I say.

Not to remind him.

To remind myself.

“I write what happened. Not what they wish had happened. Not what looks neat on a ledger. What I *saw*.”

Cairn inclines his head.

“Then go,” he says. “Write.”

“I’m going to do more than write,” I say.

The anger that was cold earlier has found its vector.

“I’m going to audit that Temple into the ground. I’m going to pull every contract, every ledger, every sealed reliquary file they thought no one would ever look at, and I’m going to find where they made him theirs. Then I’m going to prove it was never legal. Not by our law. Not by any.”

My hands have stopped shaking.

The rage has crystallized into something sharper.

Purpose.

“Ambitious,” Cairn says.

“Necessary,” I correct.

I turn toward the door.

“Hey,” he calls after me.

I look back.

He’s picked up his rag again.

Polishing the same glass.

Always polishing.

“You go to war with a god‑house,” he says, “it’s going to hit back. Hard. Make sure he’s worth it.”

He’s testing me.

Seeing if my spine is rhetoric or bone.

I don’t hesitate.

“He is,” I say.

Cairn’s mouth curls.

“Good luck, Auditor,” he says.

I push the door open.

The Vein Markets hit me with sound and smell and motion—carts rattling over bone, hawkers shouting, steam from some questionable stew stall billowing into the damp morning air.

Above it all, the god’s ribs arch.

The dome of the Heart‑Chamber gleams faintly, a tumor against the sky.

From here, it looks unassailable.

Perfect.

I know better now.

There are cracks.

I adjust my robes.

Straighten my spine.

My feet find the path back toward the lifts, toward the Spine, toward the Temple and its walls of precedent and bone and holy lies.

They think they took him from me.

They think they turned this into a tragic love story with a noble, doomed monster and a righteous, bereft scribe.

They forgot one thing.

I don’t do tragedy.

I do revisions.
