# Chapter 10 — *The Safe House*
### Lysa | POV

There is a spot on my neck that tingles. A phantom pulse.

For a moment I lie very still and catalogue that single point of awareness, the way I would note a hairline crack in a Tribunal wall or a missing page in a file. It’s hot, compared to the rest of me. A tiny, insistent burn just below my jaw, like I fell asleep against a candle-flame and only remember the light.

Then the rest of my body checks in, and the list gets longer.

Head: heavy. Mouth: dry, metallic, as if I’ve been chewing on old coins. Limbs: present but filled with wet sand. The world beyond my eyelids has that soft, smudged quality of bad charcoal sketches—edges blurred, darks and lights bleeding into each other.

I open my eyes anyway.

The safe house looks different in daylight.

Last night, what little light there was came from a single guttering lamp and the glow of his Marks under his skin. Everything else was storm and stone and the sharp, immediate fact of him bleeding out at my feet. Now, thin morning light filters through the cracks between the bone-planks someone nailed over the high slit-windows. It comes in slanting bars, painting dust motes in the air and striping the walls in pale grey.

I’m not on the floor anymore.

Someone—there is only one someone it could be—has dragged over a pile of old furs and padded them with what looks like a bundled cloak and several folded, questionably clean blankets. I’m lying on my side in the middle of the heap, robes loosened, boots off. My satchel sits within arm’s reach, neatly placed, as if I might wake and go straight back to note-taking.

I try to sit up.

Dizziness hits me like a bucket of cold water.

The room tilts; the bones of the ceiling sway. Black creeps in at the edges of my vision, crowding the dust motes. My stomach lurches in delayed protest.

I grit my teeth and force my elbows to lock, refusing the impulse to flop back down like a fainting heroine in some morality play.

Instantly, he is there.

“Careful,” Cyran says, and his hands are on me—one steadying my shoulder, the other hovering near my ribs, as if asking permission even now.

I blink up at him, trying to reconcile the man in front of me with the one who had a hole in his side the width of my hand an hour—no, more than that—ago.

He looks… whole.

Whole and wrong, because I’m used to his wrongness being a matter of ethics, not physics.

The tear in his leathers is still there, a ragged line up his side, but the skin beneath it is smooth and unbroken, pale as carved candle-wax. His shirt is half-open; red-tinged sigils gleam faintly along his ribs, still warm from whatever god-fire just stitched him back together. There’s dried blood on his gloves, on the hem of his coat, in dark spatters on his throat—but none of it is fresh.

He is watching me with a expression I do not like.

He looks at me like I’m one of the ghoul-kin that comes staggering out of the punishment halls after a Mark is stripped—fragile and over-handled, one wrong movement away from breaking.

It’s annoying.

“I’m fine,” I say.

My voice comes out hoarser than I’d like. I tuck my feet under me, test my balance. The room wobbles but doesn’t pitch.

“Liar,” he says, but there’s no usual music in it. No amused edge. Just flat assessment.

His eyes flick to my throat.

I resist the urge to clap a hand over the tingling spot. Instead, I lift my chin a fraction, as if I’m deliberately offering him the view.

“Am I bleeding?” I ask.

He swallows. The movement drags my attention, unwisely, to the line of his throat.

“No,” he says. “Not anymore.”

His voice is careful. Every word placed gently on the floor between us, so neither of us will trip.

He shifts back, onto his heels, creating a little more space. Then he produces a chipped tin cup from somewhere outside my immediate field of vision and holds it out to me.

“Here,” he says. “Sip. Slowly. If you fall over again, I will be offended.”

I take the cup. My fingers brush his glove, cold leather against my skin.

The liquid inside is cloudy and pale, barely the shade of weak tea. It smells faintly of sugar and whatever passes for clean water in this district.

“Sugar water?” I ask.

He lifts one shoulder. “It’s all the safe house stash had, unless you’d prefer vintage marrow-rot.” He nods toward a cracked jug in the corner that I very much do not want to investigate more closely.

I taste.

It’s lukewarm and far too sweet, granules not entirely dissolved, but the effect is almost immediate. My stomach clenches in interest; my fingers stop feeling quite so much like empty gloves.

I take another small sip.

“You’re pale,” he says.

“So are you,” I reply.

“I’m always pale,” he says. “It’s part of the aesthetic. You, on the other hand, are approaching archival parchment.”

“Archival parchment survives for centuries,” I say. “It has its uses.”

He actually huffs something like a laugh. The sound eases something tight between my shoulders.

We sit in silence for a few moments. I focus on the mechanics of swallowing, of making my hand lift the cup, tilt it, lower it again without spilling. The furs under me are scratchy, stiff with age, and smell faintly of old smoke and whatever animal they used to belong to. The air in here is stale, ossuary-dry, but there’s a faint trace of incense and copper overlaying it.

My own blood. His mouth.

Heat crawls up my throat, unrelated to the sugar.

I clear it.

“You’re okay,” I say, because stating obvious truths is sometimes the only way I can believe them. “The wound is—”

“Gone,” he supplies. “Yes. Thoroughly. You… did that.”

“That’s what blood is for,” I say. “In your case.”

His gaze drops to the spot on my neck I’ve been trying not to think about.

“And in your case?” he asks, very softly.

“In my case,” I say, “I will be fine.”

He looks unconvinced. Guilt smolders behind his eyes like banked coals.

“It was necessary,” I add.

“It was dangerous,” he says. “And you were supposed to be the one in this arrangement who didn’t make ruinous decisions with their body as collateral.”

The words land somewhere between my ribs.

“Ah,” I say. “We’re back to that.”

His jaw flexes. “Why did you refuse?” he blurts out. “In my quarters. When I—”

“Offered yourself like a bribe,” I finish, because if we’re going to drag that moment into the light, we might as well name it accurately.

His mouth twists. “Yes. That.”

I roll the now-empty cup between my hands, feeling the thin metal flex.

“Because you were paying me,” I say. “Or trying to.”

“Everyone pays,” he says. “That’s how it works.”

“Not with me.”

His eyes narrow, like he’s trying to parse some unfamiliar dialect.

“You still… want me,” he says slowly. It isn’t vanity; he sounds almost… confused by the fact.

There’s no point lying. Not here. Not after I’ve had his mouth on my throat and his hands on my waist and the taste of my own blood still ghosting my tongue.

“Yes,” I say.

The admission lands in my chest like a stone dropped into a still pool. There it is. Ripples and all.

His fingers curl against his knees.

“And yet,” he says, “you said no then, and yes later. Explain the arithmetic, Investigator.”

Investigator. We both hear the armor in that title.

I set the cup down on the fur and, after a brief internal argument, reach out.

My hand finds his.

His whole body goes very still, like a cat surprised into immobility. Under my palm, his fingers are cool and rigid, tendons tight.

“When you invited me to your bed,” I say, “you weren’t offering yourself. Not really. You were offering the mask they made for you. The perfect weapon-ornament. The thing you know how to sell.”

“That’s still me,” he says. There’s a raw edge under the dryness. “At least, it’s the only version that seems to generate any return on investment.”

“I’m not an investor,” I say. “I’m not here to buy a performance.”

“Then what,” he asks, “were you doing last night?”

The question isn’t cruel. It’s honest. That makes it harder.

“Last night,” I say, “you were dying.”

I squeeze his hand, not gently.

“Last night,” I continue, “I was giving blood to heal a friend, not paying for access to a body. Those are not the same thing, no matter what the temple tried to teach you.”

He flinches minutely at friend. I pretend not to notice.

“Intent matters,” I say. “Context matters. Consent matters. You know this, Cyran. You stopped when I asked.”

His eyes flick up to mine, quick and sharp.

“You noticed that,” he says.

“I was conscious,” I say dryly. “And very aware of how badly this could go if you didn’t.”

His gaze drops to our joined hands.

“I thought I was going to fail you,” he admits. “Lose myself. Take too much.”

“You didn’t,” I say. “That’s the part you should be paying attention to.”

He lets out a slow breath.

Silence stretches, almost comfortable.

Dust motes drift lazily in the light. Somewhere above, beyond the muffled bone-walls, a marrow-crow gives a hoarse caw, greeting the corpse-city’s morning. The safe house creaks as the temperature shifts.

I look at his hand in mine. At the faint red tracery of Marks along his wrist, the places where temple knives once bit deep. At the careful stillness he’s holding, as if releasing it might break something fragile between us.

“Cyran,” I say. “What do you want?”

He blinks.

“At the moment?” he says. “To drag you back down into those furs and bury my face in your neck again until I can’t taste anything but you.” He smiles, quick and sharp. “In the broader sense, a bottle of decent wine and a city that doesn’t eat its own children.”

“Not performance,” I say, and my voice goes quieter. “Strip that away. No donors. No temple. No Tribunal. No Marks. If you didn’t have to pay for anything with your body or your blades… what would you want? For yourself.”

He looks at me like I’ve asked him to recite a prayer in a language that doesn’t exist.

His gaze fixes on the spot where my fingers circle his. His mouth opens, then closes.

“I don’t know,” he says.

The words are very small.

The person the Tribunal file describes—a volatile, limited-function asset with amenable disposition—that person would have an answer ready, even if it was a lie. The man in front of me looks like someone who has just had the floor taken out from under him.

“How could I?” he adds, a moment later. “Every time I’ve… wanted anything, it’s been catalogued, priced, and sold back to me with interest.”

My thumb brushes, almost of its own accord, over the back of his hand.

“That wasn’t an accusation,” I say. “It was information.”

“About what?” he asks.

“About where we start,” I say. “If you don’t know what you want, that’s the first piece of work. Not another ceremony. Not another contract. Figuring out what would make you feel like a person, not a thing.”

He snorts softly. “That sounds suspiciously like therapy.”

“Think of it as an audit,” I say. “But this time, you’re the one deciding which parts of the record matter.”

He actually smiles at that, a real one, brief and crooked.

Before I can decide what that does to my heartbeat, a harsh scraping sound cuts through the quiet.

Both of us jolt.

A small rectangular slot near the base of the door—one I’d registered and filed under architectural curiosity when we came in—rattles. Something cylindrical rolls through it, bounces once on the stone, and comes to rest with a hollow clack.

We stare at it.

“A morning blessing,” Cyran says, but his voice has gone tight.

He rises in one smooth motion, the ease of it a jarring reminder of how thoroughly my blood has healed him. He crosses to the cylinder and stoops to pick it up.

It’s carved bone, of course everything is, but finer than the rest of this place—polished, inlaid with thin bands of dark metal. A message tube. Official. The wax seal at one end is a violent, unmistakable purple.

Thessen.

I’m on my feet before I realize I’ve moved. The rush of blood away from my head makes the room wobble; my knees buckle.

He’s beside me instantly.

His arm goes around my waist, catching me before I embarrass us both by kissing the floor. My hand ends up on his chest, fingers splayed over the place that was gaping open a few hours ago.

“Easy,” he murmurs.

The warmth of his body against mine is distracting. The purple wax is more so.

I straighten, carefully. I do not pull away from his support. This is not the moment for pride.

“Open it,” I say.

“It’s addressed to me,” he says.

“Yes,” I reply. “That’s what worries me.”

He breaks the seal with his thumb.

The parchment inside slides out with a faint crackle. The smell of expensive ink and something floral hits me, at odds with the ossuary air. I have smelled that scent before, in Vosss office, steeping out of his bergamot tea. In the briefing room, clinging to Thessen’s velvet sleeves.

He scans the text. As he reads, his expression drains of any remaining softness.

“Well?” I prompt.

He snorts without humour.

“Contract Lord Thessen,” he recites, “requests the honoured presence of Vigil Relic Cyran at a private devotions ceremony, this evening, at the Seventh Spine Chapel. Attendance is mandatory. Preparatory rites will commence at dusk.”

Devotions. Rites. Mandatory.

“That’s donor language,” I say. “Temple vocabulary.”

“Yes,” he says. “It’s the kind of ceremony where they light too much incense and call it piety while I pretend I’m not being auctioned off under a different name.”

My stomach turns.

I think of his quarters dressed like a stage. Of Thessens grapes. Wealthy patrons like to see what their contributions support. And now the storm, the missing donors, the ambush. All steps on a path someone has been charting for a long time.

I push away from him too fast.

The world lurches left; spots dance in front of my eyes. His hands close on my arms, steadying.

“No,” I say.

It comes out lower than I intend. Deadly soft.

“No,” I repeat. “We are not doing this.”

“Lysa,” he says cautiously. “He owns my contract. He owns half the Tribunal. If I don’t go, they’ll revoke Marks until I’m dust in the gutter.”

“I am the Tribunal,” I snap.

His brows shoot up.

“Correction,” I say, forcing my voice back down. “I am a Tribunal officer with clearance and a signed mandate to evaluate the conditions of your service. That includes extracurricular abuses disguised as duty.”

“They won’t see it as abuse,” he says. “They’ll call it donor relations. Tradition. Necessary sacrifice.”

“They can call it holy matrimony if they like,” I say. “The contract code still has things to say about coercion and undue risk. You are a high-value asset under audit. Any additional strain or non-essential obligation imposed during that period requires written justification and Tribunal oversight. I can make that oversight… inconvenient.”

The law snaps into place in my head like a familiar puzzle. Clauses. Precedents. Cases I’ve read where Relic exploitation got too blatant even for this corrupt machine.

“What if they ignore you?” he asks. There’s no defeat in it, just pragmatic pessimism.

“Then they have to do it loudly,” I say. “On record. With signatures.”

“And that helps us how?”

“Because loud things leave traces,” I say. “Paper trails. Witnesses. You’ve spent thirty years letting them keep you in the dark. Let me drag this into the light.”

He studies my face.

“You’re pale, shaking, and high on the thrill of righteous fury,” he says. “It’s… alarming. And attractive. Mostly alarming.”

“Cyran,” I warn.

He sobers.

“You’d do that,” he says. “Risk Voss. Thessen. All your pretty ledgers. For me.”

“This stopped being a neutral audit the moment he tried to turn it into a brochure,” I say. “And the moment you stopped when I said enough.”

He looks down at the scroll in his hand, then back at me.

“Together, then?” he says.

The word hangs there. Bigger than it has any right to be.

Together means conspiracy now. Means if this goes wrong, we fall together. I lose my position, maybe my freedom. He loses—again—the fragile hope of legal personhood.

Together also means I’m no longer standing on a balcony recording his suffering from a distance. I’m in the room, in the line of fire.

It is terrifying.

It is the easiest decision I’ve made in months.

“Yes,” I say. “Together.”

His shoulders drop, some tension I hadn’t even fully registered easing out of them.

“Very well,” he says. “Let’s go make ourselves profoundly unpopular.”

I snort.

He moves to the door, unbarring it with a practiced flip of the heavy bone. When he opens it, cool Ribcage air rolls in—damp with lingering bone-storm residue, carrying the distant clatter of market-stalls setting up for the day.

A figure is just passing in the alley outside—a bone-runner, one of the half-feral courier children the districts use when the message tubes clog. Skinny, quick, a band of bright cloth tied around one arm to mark allegiance to some merchant-house. He glances at us, eyes snagging on my robes, Cyran’s leathers, the purple smear of wax on the discarded tube in Cyran’s hand.

Whatever he sees there makes him look away fast. He scuttles on, bare feet slapping bone.

“Stay away from the Seventh Spine Chapel today,” Cyran calls after him, voice casual.

The boy half-turns, startled.

“Why?” he asks.

“Because the wrong sort of gods will be there,” Cyran says. “And they’re all very hungry.”

The boy shivers, makes a warding sign, and vanishes into the morning haze.

Cyran looks back at me.

“Small kindnesses,” he says, with a tilt of his mouth. “Change begins with irresponsible advice to impressionable youth.”

“Change begins,” I say, stepping over the threshold to stand beside him, “with someone saying no.”

I touch my wrist, feeling the raised circle of my Tribunal mark under my sleeve. Then I look at him, really look at him—at the way he’s standing slightly between me and the open street like it’s reflex, at the faint nervous flex of his fingers where they rest on the scroll, at the way his eyes soften for a heartbeat when they meet mine.

“What are we to each other now?” I think, and don’t say.

For now, we are this: an overworked scribe with a head full of case law, and a weapon who doesn’t yet know what he wants besides survival, stepping out of a forgotten ossuary and into a city that will not thank us for what we’re about to do.

“Ready, Relic?” I ask.

He offers me his arm, lampooningly courtly.

“After you, Investigator,” he says. “Let’s go teach your masters the difference between devotion and debt.”

I take his arm.

Together, we walk into the bone-light.
