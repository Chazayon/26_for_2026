# Chapter 30 — *The Archivist's Warning*
### Lysa | POV

The Halo Ward Archives smell like dust and paranoia.

Dust is expected.

Paranoia is harder to catalogue.

I stand just inside the threshold, hand still on the cool bone of the doorframe, and let my eyes adjust to the dim.

Halo Ward is the city’s memory—everything too dangerous, too obscure, or too politically inconvenient to leave in the ordinary Tribunal stacks gets shunted up here, to the ring of vertebrae that circles the Heart like a halo of calcified regret. It’s higher than my usual haunts; the air is thinner, colder, and laced with the faint ozone tang of too many wards layered badly on top of each other.

Behind me, the door closes with a soft, decisive click.

I hear the scrape of internal bolts sliding home.

Of course.

You don’t run an archive like this without having feelings about exits.

My palm is damp against the strap of my satchel.

Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth and stubborn hope, is Ilan’s note—a jagged scrap of case file pulled from a stack he slid across my desk weeks ago, when we were both still pretending the system could be fixed by the right memo.

*If the Temple’s contracts stop making sense,* he’d written in his tight, cramped hand, *go to Halo. Ask for the man who barricaded himself in the Concordance Tower. He owes me three favors and a nervous breakdown.*

*Name: Solen.*

Beneath that, a sketch.

A stylized diagram of the Temple complex, ribs and vessels and the pulsing heart at its core.

A circle marked HEART‑NODE.

Lines radiating out from it like veins.

And there, at the bottom corner, a hand‑drawn triangle.

I didn’t understand the triangle then.

I don’t now.

I don’t have time to.

Every clock in my body is set to the same hour: tonight, the Ritual.

Varos’s Binding.

Cyran on an altar under a god that has already taken too much.

My shoes squeak faintly on the polished bone floor as I move deeper into the archives.

Shelves tower on either side of the narrow aisle, built from stripped vertebrae, stacked so high they vanish into shadows above the reach of the lamps. Scrolls spill from their pigeonholes in loose drifts of parchment, some tied, most not. Books sit in precarious spines‑out piles along the tops of cases, like they’ve been thrown up there to keep them from biting anyone.

The lamps themselves swing on nerve‑cords from the ceiling, their light sickly and uneven. Each one is ringed in a halo of scribbled glyphs—protection wards, energy funnels, personal neuroses turned into magic.

Paper rustles in the distance.

A quill scratches.

Someone mutters to themselves, too low for the words to carry.

This is not like the clean, ordered shelves of the Tribunal Spine.

This is what the inside of my mind would look like if you turned it inside out and left it alone for a decade.

I should feel comforted.

I don’t.

“State your business,” a voice says sharply.

Too close.

I stop.

Movement to my right—the glint of metal between two book‑towers.

My heart thumps.

Slowly, carefully, I turn toward the sound.

A man stands half‑hidden in the shadow of a leaning column of ledgers.

He’s holding a hand‑crossbow.

It’s pointed at my chest.

Respectable.

The man himself looks like someone who has been fighting a paper war for far too long and is losing on points but refusing to concede. His hair is dark and wild, shot through with early gray at the temples. Thick lenses sit skewed on the bridge of his nose, held there by a strap that looks like it was repurposed from some sort of harness. Ink stains his fingers up to the second knuckle.

And yes—he has six fingers on the hand not holding the crossbow.

All of them tense.

“Hello,” I say.

My voice comes out calmer than I feel.

“Stop,” he snaps. “Don’t move. Name, affiliation, and whether or not you are currently in service to any known or unknown divine entities.”

“Lysa,” I say. “Tribunal Scribe. Affiliation…” I exhale. “Pending revision. And no. Not knowingly.”

The crossbow doesn’t lower.

His eyes dart to my wrists, checking for brands.

“I don’t see a badge,” he says.

“I resigned,” I say.

He blinks.

“Today,” I clarify. “It’s been a long morning.”

“People don’t resign from the Tribunal,” he says warily. “They retire, or they ‘transfer,’ or they get quietly executed in a basement file room. Resigning implies a level of free will the Council doesn’t like to acknowledge.”

“Then perhaps they can file a complaint,” I say dryly. “I’ll add it to my stack.”

One of his mouths corners twitches.

Very slightly.

“The crossbow,” I add, eyeing it. “Is that strictly necessary?”

“Absolutely,” he says. “You wouldn’t believe how many people try to kill archivists once they realize knowledge cannot be bribed. Or you might. You have that look.”

“What look is that?” I ask.

“Someone who’s read too many contracts to trust anyone who writes them,” he says.

He studies me for another taut heartbeat.

“Why are you here, Lysa‑who‑resigns?” he asks.

I fit my hand into my satchel.

Slowly.

No sudden moves with paranoid men and loaded weapons.

I draw out Ilan’s note.

Hold it up.

“The Temple,” I say. “Cyran. The Binding Ritual Varos is planning tonight. I need to know what they’re really trying to do to him. Ilan said you’d help.”

At the name, something in his shoulders unknots.

Not entirely.

Enough.

The crossbow dips a fraction.

“You know Ilan,” he says.

It isn’t quite a question.

“I’ve read his reports,” I say. “And some of his… less official notes.”

“Poor bastard,” he mutters. “Always did have a talent for tripping over the big questions and then being told to file them under ‘miscellaneous.’”

“He sent me to you,” I say.

“That does sound like him,” Solen says.

He lowers the crossbow fully.

Does not put it away.

Progress.

He steps out from behind the ledger tower.

Up close, the paranoia is even more obvious.

His eyes flick constantly to the corners, to the shelves, to the lamps. His shoulders hunch like he’s bracing for the building to collapse. There are talismans tied around his wrists—little knots of bone and wire, each etched with personal sigils.

He smells like old ink and sleeplessness.

“Come on, then,” he says brusquely. “Before someone notices you’re up here and assumes I’m plotting a coup again.”

“Again?” I ask.

We weave through a narrow canyon of books.

“Long story,” he mutters. “Involves a misfiled prophecy, an overreaction from the Prime Contract, and a very flammable index. I’m banned from three entire stacks.”

“I thought you ran this place,” I say.

“I haunt it,” he corrects. “There’s a difference.”

The aisle opens suddenly into a wider space.

A sort of central clearing, if you can call a room filled with paper a clearing.

At its center squats a broad bone table, more scarred than smooth. It’s covered in a perfect, impossibly precarious chaos—scrolls unfurled and weighted with ink bottles, open books overlapping, loose sheets pinned under daggers that look more functional than decorative.

Above it hangs a single, much larger lamp, its light steadier, its surrounding wards updated so often the bone above it looks more glyph than structure.

Solen moves to the table.

Clears a space in the middle with three deft sweeps of his many‑fingered hands, scattering paper in what might be a mathematically calculated pattern or just the practiced dance of someone who knows his own mess intimately.

Underneath, revealed like a body under a shroud, lies a diagram.

At first glance, it looks like a city.

At second, like a disease.

Ribs arch in black ink lines.

Vessels thread between them.

At the core, a massive, stylized heart.

Even on paper, it almost seems to pulse.

“The Temple complex,” I say.

“Mm,” Solen says. “And its… aftermarket modifications.”

I lean closer.

The heart is ringed in sigils I half‑recognize from my work auditing Temple oaths. Binding glyphs, protection clauses, efficiency charms. Between them, newer marks worm their way in—thin, spidery scripts that don’t match any standard Tribunal notation.

Solen taps a point near the heart’s base with one ink‑blackened finger.

“Here,” he says. “The Heart‑Node. The Prime Contract’s anchor in the corpse. Where all the little agreements you people sign bleed into something hungrier. The old Temple rituals just circled it. Prayers. Sacrifices. Performances.”

His mouth twists.

“This is new,” he says. “This is Varos.”

New lines radiate from the Heart‑Node, etched over older ones in a darker ink.

They converge on a stylized human outline at the diagram’s edge.

Chains of sigils link that figure back to the Node.

“Cyran,” I say quietly.

“Your Relic,” Solen says.

“Partner,” I correct automatically.

He looks up at me.

His mouth quirks again.

“Right,” he says softly. “Partner. Good. He needs one.”

He taps the human outline.

“Varos wants to plug him in here.”

“Like a battery,” I say.

“Like a filter,” Solen says. “The Heart‑Node is choking on corruption. Too many bad contracts. Too much double‑counted blood. Its own clauses are starting to contradict. You know what happens when you stress a system like that.”

“It breaks,” I say.

“Or adapts,” he says. “Varos’s solution is to route the excess through something that can survive it. Something designed to take on other people’s sins and not die. Something already bound to the Prime Contract’s core script. A living regulator.”

“A living regulator with a face donors find pretty,” I say bitterly.

“Never hurts the tithe numbers,” Solen says.

He traces a line from the stylized Cyran up to the Heart‑Node.

“If he goes in here,” he says, “every corrupt clause, every stolen mark, every little cruelty wrapped in legalese will run through him. The Prime Contract will keep eating. The city will keep functioning. On the surface, everything looks better. Smoother. The machine stops jamming.”

“And him?” I ask.

Solen’s eyes flick up to mine.

“It will eat him,” he says.

The words are clinical.

Precise.

They land like a punch.

“How long?” I ask.

“Days,” he says. “Maybe weeks, if the initial load is lower than I project. He’ll process the worst of it first. The backlog. After that, he’ll still be leaking their sins until something gives. Mind. Body. Soul. Whatever word makes you listen.”

My stomach roils.

I grip the edge of the table until my knuckles ache.

I picture Cyran on that bed in the Temple, silk and gold and gilded cage, pretending not to care that the priesthood is sharpening their knives again. I picture the way he laughed when I told him I loved him, half‑disbelieving, half desperate, as if the words themselves were a rope thrown over a pit.

“Of course they called it Redemption,” I say. “Or Service. Or Sanctification. Anything but what it is.”

“Extraction,” Solen says. “They’re mining him. They always have been. This is just… a more efficient rig.”

“How do we stop it?” I ask.

No preamble.

No polite throat‑clearing.

There isn’t time for it.

“The contract is legal,” Solen says.

The words are a curse.

“On paper,” he adds. “Mistake plenty of people make is assuming paper is where it ends. But in this case, unfortunately for us, magic is following law. The Binding Varos designed piggybacks on pre‑existing Temple clauses. The ones that made Cyran property in the first place.”

“I’ve seen them,” I say.

“Saw, perhaps,” Solen says. “Most of those ledgers burned during your little Heart‑Chamber incident.”

“That wasn’t me,” I say automatically.

He gives me a look over the top of his glasses.

“Fine,” I concede. “Not solely me.”

He snorts.

Moves around the table, muttering to himself.

“The thing about conglomerated contracts like this,” he says, riffling through piles, “is that when you try to untie the knot after a while, you just end up with a bigger snarl. Every new clause uses the last as precedent. You pull one, ten more tighten. It’s elegant, in a horrifying way.”

“That’s not reassuring,” I say.

“Good,” he says. “You shouldn’t be reassured.”

He finds what he’s looking for—a scroll darker and older than the others, its bone rods yellowed with age, its binding cords wrapped in seals I don’t recognize. He brings it back to the central table like it might explode.

“Most people forget there was law before the Temple,” he says. “Before the Prime Contract. Before the idea that signing over your soul was just part of being a good citizen. They call it Old Law, when they remember it at all. Founder clauses. Emergency precedents. The kind of thing that makes modern Contract Lords wake up sweating.”

He sets the scroll down.

Unwraps it carefully.

The parchment inside is brittle.

The ink is not.

It’s a deep, vicious black that seems to drink the lamp light instead of reflecting it.

“It says,” Solen recites, eyes skimming the script with the ease of someone who has read it far too many times, “that any contract signed under duress, coercion, or while the signatory is not fully alive, cognizant, and capable of informed consent is, in the eyes of the Founders, null.”

I know this.

In theory.

The clause has been buried under centuries of exceptions and Temple legalese, but it’s there.

“Duress is notoriously hard to prove,” I say. “They’ll argue that Cyran gave himself willingly. That he was honored to serve. We both know that’s a lie, but the Temple has always been very good at pretending coercion is devotion.”

Solen’s gaze cuts to me.

“Duress is muddy,” he agrees. “Almost impossible to quantify. Intimidation. Social pressure. Economic necessity. That’s why most Contract Lords are comfortable pushing into that gray—lots of deniability. But the second criterion…”

He taps the scroll with one finger.

“‘Not fully alive,’” he says. “That’s harder to spin.”

My pulse kicks.

“He was,” I say slowly, “technically dead when they signed him.”

“Very much so,” Solen says. “I’ve seen the Ledger entries. Cross‑referenced with Heart‑Chamber vital logs. His pulse stopped under the knife. They let him bleed out on that altar and then dragged him back with the Prime Contract’s power specifically so they could sign a piece of paper with someone who couldn’t legally say no. That’s not just immoral. It’s shoddy craft.”

Heat rises up my throat.

“Then that contract is void,” I say. “All of it. Every clause built on top. Every amendment. Every Binding that uses it as a spine.”

“In theory,” Solen says.

“In law,” I correct.

He inclines his head.

“In law,” he allows. “But you know as well as I do that words on a page mean nothing if no one with teeth hears them. Magic respects the oldest law in the room. The Prime Contract is old. This is older.”

He taps the scroll again.

“You need to bring this into the Heart‑Chamber,” he says. “You need to stand in front of the Prime Contract’s Node while it’s trying to eat your partner and say, in very clear, very precise language, that its meal ticket is invalid. That it never had the right to sign him in the first place. That its power in this instance is stolen.”

My mouth is dry.

“And if I do that,” I say. “If I prove, in front of it, that its contract is void?”

He smiles.

It’s not a nice expression.

“The magic snaps,” he says. “All that weight, all that pressure, all those centuries of clauses tugging on the wrong name—they recoil. It will hurt. Possibly a lot. But it will break the Binding.”

“And Cyran?” I ask.

His smile fades.

“He’ll still be tied in,” he says. “You can’t rip someone out of a system like that without consequences. But the direction of the flow changes. Instead of him feeding it, it starts bleeding into him. If he survives the shock, he’ll have… leverage.”

“Leverage,” I say.

“Over the Prime Contract,” Solen says softly.

The idea is obscene.

Terrifying.

Tempting.

I think of Thessen offering Cyran ascension.

Of Varos trying to turn him into a living altar.

Of Cyran’s face when he told me he would rather be a man at my side than a god alone.

“How do we get into the Heart‑Chamber?” I ask.

“Tribunal authority,” he says. “You still have a badge?”

“Not anymore,” I say.

He winces.

“Then you’ll need a different key,” he says. “A blood‑key. Easier for him than for you.”

I don’t flinch.

“We’ll manage,” I say.

He searches my face for a moment.

Whatever he sees there must satisfy him.

He reaches to the bottom corner of the map.

Taps a tiny glyph there.

At first I think it’s just a maker’s mark.

A triangle.

Sharp and simple.

Three lines, three corners.

Inside it, the faintest suggestion of an eye.

“And watch out for him,” Solen says.

“The Prime Contract,” I say.

He shakes his head.

“No,” he says. “This. Whoever scrawled this into the margins has been poking at the system longer than Varos has been alive. He calls himself Cipher sometimes, when he calls himself anything at all.”

“Cipher,” I repeat.

“What is he?” I ask.

“Curious,” Solen says. “Hungry. Very bad at leaving things alone. He likes patterns. And debt. And games. If he’s watching this ritual, he’ll be… invested.”

“In Cyran?” I ask.

“In anyone who tries to break the rules loudly enough,” Solen says. “Which, judging from your file, includes you.”

“My file,” I say.

He shrugs.

“I read a lot,” he says. “Occupational hazard.”

“How do I avoid him?” I ask.

“You don’t,” Solen says. “You just don’t sign anything if he smiles at you.”

I exhale.

“Excellent,” I say. “An anonymous god of contracts and a meddling… whatever that is. Anything else I should worry about while I’m down there?”

“Drowning in congealed heart‑blood, being ripped apart by conflicting clauses, spontaneous ascension,” he says. “Pick your poison.”

“Reassuring,” I say again.

He rolls the Old Law scroll back up with hands that are suddenly, noticeably gentler.

Ties it with a strip of leather from around his wrist.

Offers it to me.

The parchment is heavier than it looks.

I take it with both hands.

The seals tingle against my skin.

“Use it,” he says. “Words matter. Especially when you say them in the place that taught everyone else to lie with theirs.”

“Thank you,” I say.

He waves a hand, as if brushing the gratitude off like dust.

“Don’t thank me yet,” he says. “If this works, you’ll have made a very powerful enemy very, very interested in you. And in him.”

“He already is,” I say.

My fingers tighten on the scroll.

Cyran’s face floats up in my mind’s eye.

His laugh.

The way he said *I choose you* like the words tasted unfamiliar and perfect all at once.

His throat under my hand in the graveyard, warm and vulnerable and his.

“What’s one more god on the list?” I add.

Solen huffs out something that might be a laugh.

“Spoken like someone who hasn’t read enough apocalypse reports,” he says. “Go. The longer you stand still, the more likely the world is to collapse on a different axis entirely.”

I tuck the scroll into my satchel.

It fits there like it was waiting.

I turn to go.

“Lysa,” he says.

I look back.

He’s already half‑turned away, reaching for another stack of books, mind racing ahead.

But his voice, when he adds, “Bring him back,” is quiet.

Earnest.

“I intend to,” I say.

I find my way out of the labyrinth by instinct more than memory.

The wards prick at my skin as I pass under them.

The door opens onto a brighter corridor, the hum of bureaucratic life filtering back in—clerks arguing over ink allocations, an Inquisitor berating a junior over misfiled petitions, the distant clatter of a bone‑lift.

Everything looks the same.

Nothing is.

I adjust the weight of the satchel on my shoulder.

The Old Law’s presence is a solid, living weight against my hip.

A weapon carved in ink instead of steel.

Knowledge has always been my chosen blade.

Today, it has teeth.

I take a breath.

The air tastes of old bone and new resolve.

Then I run.

Down through Halo Ward, into the Spine, toward the Temple and the Heart, toward a ritual that thinks it knows its own script.

Cyran is waiting at the center of it.

Bound.

Branded.

Braced to be eaten.

He doesn’t know I’m coming yet.

He will.

Because they wrote his name in a ledger when he was dead and called it holy.

I have the law that says otherwise.

If the Prime Contract wants to argue, it can do so to my face.

In its own heart.
