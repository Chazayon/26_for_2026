# Chapter 5 — *Patrol with a Witness*
### Cyran | POV

The city feels different when someone is watching.

Usually, the Ribcage is my stage and my cage in equal measure. Tonight, it feels like a ledger line I could smudge with one wrong move.

Bone arches rise overhead, ribs of the dead god slick with marrow-mist, their inner curves strung with exhausted moss-lanterns pulsing a dull green. The streets under our boots are more fracture than solid surface—bone slats, cartilage patches, the odd vertebra half-sunk into the muck like a knucklebone someone dropped and never picked up.

I set a fast pace. Of course I do. If she can’t keep up, that’s hardly my fault.

Behind me, Lysa’s footsteps match mine. Not hurried, not stumbling—just there, steady as a metronome marking time on this little performance of ours. She wore practical boots. I hate practical boots. They say too loudly that their wearer came expecting trouble.

“Your report will note,” I say over my shoulder, “that I am prompt and diligent in beginning patrols on time despite the unnecessary weight of Tribunal oversight.”

“Your report,” she replies, “will note whether you walked where you were supposed to, Relic. Not how you felt about it.”

Relic. Not Cyran.

Last night she said my name like a scalpel. Tonight she uses the title like a slap of cold water. Apparently we’re rotating our forms of address.

“Careful, Investigator,” I murmur. “If you get any drier, the marrow will start flaking off the ribs.”

She says nothing. When I glance back, I find her adjusting her spectacles with one ink-stained finger, face composed. Her hood is down tonight, dark hair braided tight. The indigo of her robes is muted here under the corpse’s half-light, hem already gathering grime. She’s belted them higher, though. There’s a slit at one side I didn’t notice before, enough to give her legs room to move.

Interesting.

The memory of her hands on me in my quarters—cool fingers firm on my arm, the careful way she’d refused to use my body like coin—flickers through me like a draft. I push it aside. New night, new act.

We turn along one of the Ribcage’s main arteries, the street curving under three successive ribs like a tunnel ribbed in bone. To our left, a row of shanties clings to the gods inner wall—patchwork structures built from scavenged cartilage and temple castoffs. To our right, the drop into the lower levels yawns between vertebrae-bridges, the mist below glowing red in pulses where the cisterns breathe.

I gesture with two fingers toward a narrow side alley as we pass it.

“That’s where the marrow-thieves hang, if you ever want to audit someone with even less career stability than I have,” I say. “They’re very enterprising. Steal holy sludge, sell it cut with god-knows-what to idiots who think drinking it will make them immortal.”

“Has it ever worked?” she asks.

I grin. “If it did, do you think I’d still be working for the Vigil?”

A door bangs open ahead. A trio of citizens—two women in patched coats and a man with a basket of fungus loaves under his arm—step out, laughing at something only they can hear. The laughter dies the instant they see me.

It’s an almost musical little murder.

The man’s eyes widen. One of the women makes a quick, fumbling gesture over her heart, some local ward-sign mangled from temple liturgy. They don’t scream. They don’t bow. They just veer.

Crossing the street is too kind a phrase. They bolt sideways into the traffic, nearly colliding with a cart, hugging the far wall like it might swallow them if they press hard enough. The fungus basket tilts; two loaves spill, bouncing once on the bone slats and rolling toward the gutter.

I stop.

For a beat, everything hangs there: the loaves, them, Lysa’s gaze between us.

Then I make a show of offering a little courtly half-bow to their retreating backs, one hand flourished to my chest.

“Adoring fans,” I say, loud enough that the nearest one flinches. “Truly, fame is a burden.”

The man glances back once, terror thick in his scent, then hurriedly looks away. None of them return for the fallen bread.

Steps beside me. The scratch of quill on paper.

Of course she’s taking a note.

I don’t let my smile slip, but my teeth itch with it.

“What devastating observation are we inscribing now, love?” I ask, starting forward again. I nudge one of the loaves with the toe of my boot, sending it into the gutter. “Public terror? Relic as civic eyesore?”

“Citizen response to your presence,” she says. “Useful context for any claims about community safety.”

“Fear is safety,” I say lightly. “Ask the Contract Lords.”

“I’m not auditing them,” she says. “Yet.”

Now that’s a delightful little word to drop into a conversation. Yet.

We move on.

The closer we get to the Vein Markets, the louder the city hums. This late, the main trade is winding down; stalls shuttering, debt markers being argued over with increasingly shrill conviction, the smells intensifying as the night ripens—spiced gristle skewers charring over coals, sweet-sour broth ladled from enormous bone cauldrons, the underlying rot of refuse heaps left too long.

Lysa keeps pace without complaint, notebook occasionally flicking open in her hands like a shield. She doesn’t huddle it to her chest like a nervous clerk; she wields it. Every time I risk a sidelong glance, her eyes are up, tracking me, not the page.

The city notices her, too.

A cluster of teenagers loitering under a crooked rib—bone-dice clicking between their fingers, cigarettes smoldering—fall abruptly silent as we pass. One of them mutters, “Tribunal,” under his breath. The others look between my carved sigils and her ink-mark, trying to decide which is the greater threat.

I bare my teeth at them in a grin. They flinch, which is satisfying, and also a little more bitter than it should be.

“Comforting to know I’m not the only one ruining evenings,” I murmur.

“That implies you’re ruining mine,” Lysa says. “You’re not.”

“You wound me,” I say. “Again.”

Her mouth quirks, very slightly. It’s gone in a heartbeat, but I see it.

The market square opens up ahead, a sudden widening of the bone street into a rough circle ringed in stalls. Ribs arch overhead like the cage of some colossal amphitheater. Lanterns hang from their inner curves—glass globes filled with slow-burning phosphor fungus, casting a jaundiced light over everything.

Vendors are in the final throes of commerce: a woman with arms like rope hauling in nets of pale cave-fish; a gristle-seller flipping the last of his skewers over dying coals, calling hoarsely about discounts; a charm peddler shoving bone-amulets into reluctant hands, promising protection from precisely what I am.

We step into the square. Conversations stutter. Heads turn.

Silence doesn’t fall all at once. It unravels, thread by thread, picked loose by my presence until the whole plane of noise sags. The hissing of the gristle coals sounds suddenly loud. Somewhere a bone-bird on a rooftop gives one harsh, startled caw and then is quiet.

The hairs on the back of my neck rise.

It’s not the silence. I like silence. Silence is an old lover; we understand each other. This is different. This is absence.

The wrong sounds are missing.

No scuttle of gutter-rats in the refuse piled against the far wall. No wet slap of waste being slopped into the communal trough beside the char-house. The air, a moment ago thick with cooking smoke and sweat, shifts—gains a new note. Musk. Old meat. Ozone.

My hand goes to my daggers before I decide to let it.

Behind me, Lysa stops.

“Stay here,” I say, already moving forward into the square’s center.

“You don’t give orders to Tribunal investigators,” she says crisply.

I half-turn in time to see her lift something from the inner fold of her robe—a compact baton of dark metal, etched with faint sigils along its length.

She thumbs a catch. The air around the tip crackles, a faint corona of blue-white charge licking outward.

Well.

“Absolutely not,” I say. “Put that away.”

“Absolutely not,” she echoes, and that’s the first time I’ve heard steel in her voice aimed at anyone but an absent superior.

“You’re here to observe, not get eaten,” I say. “Let me do my job, darling, or my Merit account is going to get very odd remarks attached to it.”

“If I see a threat, I respond,” she says, stepping to the edge of a fruit barrow. “That’s my job.”

Across the square, one of the refuse heaps moves.

Slowly, at first. A sag in the pile of broken crates and offal-sacks. Then, as if something underneath it has remembered it has limbs, the whole mound heaves.

A wet, tearing sound like rotten cloth giving way. Splinters rain down. A stall-holder swears, snatching back a hanging string of marrow-sausages as the refuse erupts.

The thing that unfolds from the waste is roughly boar-sized, if boars were assembled from mismatched carcass scrap and divine waste. Plates of bone jut at uneven angles from grey, ropey flesh. Its head is wrong twice over—a blunt snout with too many nostrils, eyes marbled and milk-filmed, jaw unhinging too far back to accommodate two overlapping rows of teeth. Black ichor dribbles from between them, smoking slightly where it hits stone.

A scavenger-beast. Half-feral god-spawn the Contract Lords insist aren’t their problem because they didn’t make this one on purpose.

People scream.

There’s always a moment, right here, that decides how bad it gets. Fight, flight, freeze. The beast lunges toward the nearest dense knot of bodies—the fishmonger’s stall. The crowd does all three at once. Some bolt. Some stand petrified. Some slam into others and create a lovely little pile of potential mauling.

I move.

The world narrows to angles and distance. Cart, stall, open lane. Beast velocity versus human panic versus my own speed. The air tastes metallic, thick with adrenaline and the tang of its musk.

“Over here, you half-cooked mistake,” I call, drawing one dagger, then the other. The bone hilts are reassuringly solid in my palms, sigils cold against my skin. “Wrong buffet.”

It snaps its head toward me at the sound. Good. Come on, then.

I vault a cart stacked with bruised fruit, boots barely skimming the slick wood, and land in a roll that sets me directly in its path. Stall-holders scatter to either side, curses flying.

The beast slams down where I’d been an instant earlier. Bone claws, long and hook-tipped, carve furrows into the stone. Chunks of refuse and half-rotten fish explode outward. The reek hits like a fist.

“Sloppy!” I sing, stepping in and letting one blade kiss along the joint of its front limb. Black ichor wells, hisses where it falls.

It bellows, a sound more steam vent than lung, and swings sideways. I duck, feeling the rush of air as claw passes an inch above my spine. The square constricts around us—stalls, walls, people. No open room for the pretty, sweeping spins I could manage down in the cistern. We’re pressed in close here, fighting in a bone cage.

“Everyone back!” Lysa’s voice cuts through the chaos from somewhere behind me. Cool, precise, sliced off at the edges. “Clear the square! Move!”

Surprising. Most Tribunal types start quoting codes when frightened. She’s giving orders that might actually get someone out alive.

The beast swipes again. I pivot, weight on the balls of my feet, letting the claws whistle past. Another nick along its side, another gout of smoky gore. We make a circle of it, the two of us—monster and slightly worse monster—turning around a shared center.

I am aware, distantly, of Lysa at the periphery, not where I told her to stay. Her heart is beating a fraction too fast for comfort. There’s a crackle of that baton again, a warning thrum of caged lightning.

“Do not engage,” I throw over my shoulder. “You’ll only encourage it.”

“I am not—” she starts.

Something small darts at the edge of my vision. Not the beast. Lower.

A child, perhaps eight, maybe nine, darting from beneath a toppled stall. Wrong place, wrong time—little body jolted loose from wherever it was hiding by the shudder of our dance. He—or she, it hardly matters now—sprints three steps, then freezes.

Right in the scavenger’s next path.

For one suspended second, all the lines change.

I can’t get there with enough momentum for a killing blow before those claws come down. I can see the geometry as clearly as if Thessen had chalked it on temple stone. The beast is coiled, limb already in motion. The child is a soft, bright mark on the map, eyes wide, feet rooted.

If I dodge, I live. The child doesn’t.

If I keep circling, maybe I get a cleaner opening. Later. After.

My body decides before I do.

I lunge.

The child’s eyes are huge and dark, whites bright against marrow-dusted skin. For a lunatic heartbeat, my mind overlays another set of eyes there—the boy in the cistern, wrists bruised, back pressed into machinery.

No.

I slam myself into the space between the beast and the child, turning as I do so that the thickest part of my leathers takes the brunt. There’s no time for elegance. No time for song or mockery.

Claws hit.

Pain is a white-hot streak across my side and arm, a rake that tears through leather and into flesh underneath. It shudders all the way up to my teeth.

I make a sound I don’t mean to, half-snarl, half sharper thing, and the force of the impact drives my boots back a step. The child shrieks behind me, high and thin and very alive.

Good. Stay that way.

The beast tries to follow through, to tear and rend. I jam my left arm forward, let the claws bite there instead of deeper, flesh shredding, and use the proximity.

The right-hand dagger goes up.

Under the jaw is a soft spot, if you know where to feel for it. Even rushed, even half-blinded by pain, my hand remembers.

I drive the blade up, hard. I feel cartilage give, then the crunch of bone, then the sudden horrid slack of something vital parting. Black ichor floods over my knuckles, hot and stinking. The beast convulses, claws raking one last time as its legs lose coordination.

We go down together. I twist, wrenching the dagger free with a wet sound, and shove off with one boot so its collapsing bulk misses the child by a handspan.

The carcass hits the stone with a meaty, final thud. Cracks spider across the nearest flag. The square’s remaining voices go out like snuffed candles.

For a moment, there’s only the ragged rasp of my own breath, the high keening of the child’s, and the obscene, wet ticking of cooling ichor dripping to the street.

Then, cautiously, the city remembers how to make noise.

Someone whimpers. Someone sobs. A vendor starts swearing in a stream so inventive I almost applaud. The smell of burned gristle, fish, and ruptured god-flesh sits heavy on the air.

I stagger back a step from the corpse, boots sliding in a slurry of offal and blood—black from it, red from me. My left arm burns from shoulder to wrist; my side is a hot, wet throb. The Marks along my skin buzz faintly, a kind of static under the pain, healing threads already knitting, but slowly. This is going to be an ache I remember for at least a few hours.

The child is still there, pressed against the upended stall. A boy, this one, skinny as gutter-children tend to be, wearing a shirt two sizes too big. His eyes are fixed on the beast’s head where it lolls at an angle God never intended.

I force my mouth into something that might be called a smile if you were feeling generous.

“Run along, little morsel,” I say, voice rough. “Find somewhere less exciting to stand next time.”

He blinks, looks up at me, at the blood on my arm, and then bolts, bare feet slapping on bone. Good. Two children in two nights; I should start a club.

“You’re hit.”

Lysa’s voice comes from closer than I expect.

I turn my head and find her at my elbow, closer than any sensible Tribunal investigator should be to a fresh god-spawn corpse. Her hood is back; hair has shaken loose from its braid, strands clinging damply to her temples. There’s a streak of something on her cheek—soot, or beast ichor, or both. Her eyes jump to the torn leather at my side, then to my forearm where the claws made their prettiest work.

“Tis but a scratch,” I say automatically, leaning a shoulder against the nearest intact crate. My legs have gone slightly unreliable. “Adds character.”

The look she gives me could sour holy wine.

“Sit,” she says.

“I am standing with flair,” I correct. “It’s different.”

“Sit.”

Her hand is suddenly on my elbow, fingers surprisingly strong as she pushes. I let myself slide down until I’m perched on the edge of the crate, more because I don’t entirely trust my knees not to buckle than because I take orders well.

The square is reasserting itself around us. The fishmonger is yelling about his ruined stall. Someone else is dragging the beast’s carcasshoof? paw? limb?out of the common walkway with the practiced irritation of someone who’s done this more than once. I hear a warden’s distant whistle, the shrill call answered by another further off.

Let them be late. I’ve already earned my paycheck.

Lysa crouches at my side, the hem of her practical robes darkening where it meets the damp stone. She drops the stun-baton without ceremony beside her, the charge still humming faintly, and rummages in her satchel. When her hand emerges, it’s holding a roll of clean bandage and a small vial that smells sharply of alcohol when she uncorks it with her teeth.

“Trying to get me drunk in the street, Investigator?” I murmur. “How scandalous.”

“Hold still,” she says.

I do not.

I flinch when the cool air hits the torn strip of flesh along my forearm, and again when she pours the alcohol over it. The burn is swift and bright. I hiss between my teeth, more out of habit than actual distress.

Her hands are efficient. She cleans the worst of the blood away with a bit of linen, then sets the edge of the bandage against my skin and begins to wind it around, neat loops covering the claw-marks.

Her fingers are steady.

Her hands are shaking.

Not much. Just a faint tremor at the tendon when she pulls the bandage tight. Someone who didn’t live by reading bodies might miss it.

“You’re trembling,” I say lightly. “Afraid of the blood, darling? It’s very fashionable this season.”

Her grip on the bandage tightens sharply. Pain spikes as the fabric bites into raw flesh.

“I’m afraid,” she says, voice low and dangerous, “of stupidity. You could have died.”

Ah.

Not fear of me, then. Fear for me.

That’s… new.

I shrug, or try to; it comes out more as a half-tilt of the shoulder. “I’m the shield,” I say. “That’s the job.”

“The job,” she says, tying off the end of the bandage with decisive force, “doesn’t require you to stand still and take claws that you could have dodged.”

“There was a child,” I point out, a little bewildered by this line of argument. “Small, squishy, breakable thing? You might have missed him while you were composing your incident report.”

Her gaze snaps up to mine. The tremor in her hands has moved to her jaw.

“I saw him,” she says. “I also saw the angles.”

Of course she did. She’s been mapping my patrol routes in her head since the audit started.

“You made a choice you didn’t have to make,” she continues. “You spent your body like it was disposable.”

I blink at her. There’s a short, bizarre moment where I genuinely don’t understand the problem.

“That’s what it’s for,” I say, slow. “What else am I supposed to spend?”

She looks at me for a long heartbeat that feels like being laid on an altar again, examined for flaws.

Then she looks away, reaching for my side. Her fingers go to the torn leather there without warning, and my body reacts before my mind can remind it where we are. I go very still, a temple-trained freeze, breath catching.

She notices. Of course she does. Her hand pauses on the fringe of ripped armor.

“I’m going to look at your ribs,” she says, carefully neutral. “Is that acceptable?”

Two words I never expected to hear from anyone doing anything to my body: is that acceptable.

“Yes,” I say, because it is, and because the alternative word—no—hasn’t always been believed, but I have the strangest suspicion she might actually listen to it.

She peels the torn leather back with clinical care. There are four long, parallel gashes raked across my flank, shallow but bloody, oozing more for show than for any real damage. The Marks under my skin tick with the effort of knitting; it will scar faintly, if at all.

She cleans these, too, with methodical thoroughness. The alcohol stings. I grit my teeth and make some flippant comment about liking it rough, because that is what I do when anything gets close to resembling genuine care.

She ignores it.

Her hands still shake.

“You know,” I say, as she winds the second bandage around my ribs, fingers brushing bare skin with each pass, “I don’t bite without invitation.”

The words come out more defensive than I intend, a half-remembered script. Offer reassurance, make yourself the safe predator. It’s almost funny.

Almost.

Her hands stop.

The bandage hangs loose for a beat, her fingers fisted in the linen. When she looks up, her eyes are very, very bright.

“Then consider this a permanent lack of invitation,” she says, each word enunciated with surgical precision.

Ah.

That stings more than the claws.

I laugh, because I don’t know what else to do. It’s a short sound, pressed out of me.

“Cruel,” I say. “I might weep.”

“You might learn,” she counters. “Not everyone wants what you’ve been taught to offer.”

Her voice is softer on that last part, almost too quiet to hear over the returning noise of the market. Almost.

She ties off the bandage in a final, neat knot and sits back on her heels, putting a fraction more space between us. The contact breaks. The warmth of her fingers lingers against my skin longer than it has any right to.

I look down at the white loops around my forearm, the tidy stripes around my side. They’re competent work. Not Temple-tidy, no ornamental flourishes, but solid. Caring, in a way that has nothing to do with artistry.

“Thank you, Investigator,” I say, because the words are required, and because my old teachers would flay me for forgetting manners.

“You’re welcome,” she replies. Then, after a heartbeat, “Try not to make me do that again tonight, please.”

“No promises,” I say. “The monsters here have no respect for my need to appear effortlessly invincible.”

She huffs. It is not quite a laugh. But it isn’t not one, either.

The wardens arrive in a clatter of boots and spears, all breathless importance and the lingering smell of cheap tobacco. One of them—a woman with a nose crooked from too many breaks—eyes the carcass, then me, then the bandage with a mix of respect and irritation.

“Could have waited for backup, Relic,” she says.

“Could have,” I agree. “But then your charming jawline might be decorating the cobbles, and the Tribunal frowns on that sort of rearrangement.”

She snorts. “Fair enough. We’ll get the remains to the rendering pits. Contract Lords’ll want their tithe.”

“Careful,” I murmur. “This one bites.”

She grins and goes to shout orders at her squad. The square begins to lurch back toward a semblance of normalcy. People peel off in twos and threes, muttering, clutching their salvaged goods. Someone rights the fruit cart I vaulted; bruised produce rolls, some of it crushed under boots.

Lysa stands. When she offers me her hand, it’s practical, palm out. No ceremony.

I take it.

Her grip is firm. She hauls me up with more strength than her frame suggests, then releases my fingers at once, as if remembering who I am. Or who I’m supposed to be.

We stand there for a second, too close, the smell of blood and burned gristle between us.

“You’re bleeding through,” she says, nodding at my arm where red is already seeping faintly into the bandage.

“I’ll live,” I say.

“Yes,” she says quietly. “You will. I’m not finished auditing you.”

We start walking again. The square falls behind us, the sounds of cleanup fading into the deeper creaks of the Reliquary’s bones.

She says nothing. I say nothing.

The silence between us is heavier now, thick with things not said. She saw me choose the hit. She saw me bleed. She bandaged me with hands that shook not from revulsion, but from the possibility of loss.

And then she told me, again, that my body is not hers to take.

I don’t know what to do with that particular equation. The temple never taught me this math.

Ahead, the Ribcage tunnels away into shadow and mist, patrol routes sketched in my muscles. Beside me, practical boots whisper on bone. Somewhere behind us, in a different part of the corpse, a file with my name on it is waiting for her next annotations.

The city watches.

The god’s heart beats on.

I adjust my stride, just enough that her shorter legs don’t have to work as hard to keep up.

She doesn’t comment.

We walk.
