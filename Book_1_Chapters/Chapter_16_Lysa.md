# Chapter 16 — *The Map on the Floor*
### Lysa | POV

We are plotting treason over breakfast. It feels appropriate. 

The safe house smells like burnt coffee, cheap soap, and the lingering metal-ozone of last night’s overload. Sunlight forces its way in through cracks in the warped shutters, slicing the little front room into bright bars and dust motes. The floor is cold bone under my knees. 

Charcoal stains my fingers like guilt.

I drag another line across the floorboards, connecting one ripped scrap of parchment to another.

“Thessen,” I mutter, labeling the central scrap with a quick, ugly scrawl. “Connects to the Tribunal here.”

I draw the line down and left, to a piece marked High Warden’s Office. Then another, angling off to a square I’ve labeled PRIME CONTRACT EXCHANGE, circling it twice for emphasis. 

Network of power. Network of rot.

If I were a dutiful little bureaucrat, I’d write this all up in triplicate and submit it to a subcommittee.

Instead, I’m kneeling on the floor of an illegal bolt-hole, building a conspiracy web in charcoal and stolen stationery.

Behind me, the floorboards creak.

I don’t jump.

I slept for maybe three hours in the narrow bed in the adjoining room, back to the wall, knife under the pillow out of old habit. Long enough for the edge of panic to dull. Not long enough for my muscles to stop remembering the jump, the crash, the way Cyran shook under my hands as I wiped gold off his skin. 

He moves more quietly than most men his size, but the house complains around him. Old bone and old wood are honest that way.

I keep my eye on the map.

If I look at him too quickly, I’m not sure what will show on my face.

A mug appears in the corner of my vision.

“Breakfast,” he announces.

The liquid in the mug is black, viscous, and smells like someone burned a sacrificial offering to the concept of caffeine. 

I take it.

“Did you boil this,” I ask, “or interrogate it?”

He makes a small offended noise.

“I will have you know,” he says, “that during my illustrious career of being property, I learned to make exactly one thing in a kitchen. Horrible coffee. You are the fortunate beneficiary.”

I sip.

It’s terrible.

I drink it anyway.

He lowers himself to the floor across from me, with less of the theatrical lounging he usually favors and more of a practical fold of limbs. His hair is damp, curling slightly where it wasn’t earlier, a few stubborn gold flecks still caught at his temples. The kohl is gone. The collar is gone. He’s in a roughspun shirt two sizes too big and equally battered trousers rolled at the ankle. 

Human clothes.

Not asset clothes.

He looks tired. He looks better.

“I made enough for a second mug,” he offers. “In case you want to punish yourself further.”

“This is adequate self-flagellation, thank you,” I say, and take another swallow.

The map takes up most of the floor between us now. Scraps of parchment, folded and refolded from Tribunal memos and old case files, held down with whatever I could find—an ink bottle, a spare knife, a little bone idol someone left in a corner. Lines crisscross between them in charcoal: Thessen’s Villa, Tribunal Chambers, Prime Contract Exchange, Devotions Hall, Unmarked Warehouse in the Spine. 

I’ve circled the last three so many times the floor under them is grey.

“So,” Cyran says, wrapping his long fingers around his own mug as if the heat matters. “We’re fugitives.”

“We’re independent investigators,” I correct, automatically. “Who temporarily vacated the premises of a donor event for safety reasons.”

“In a hail of broken glass and panic,” he adds, lips twitching.

“Details,” I say.

He huffs a small laugh.

The sound hangs in the air between us, uncertain, then settles.

The quiet this morning is different than the silence of last night.

Then, he sat on the floor of the washroom with his back to the wall, my hands on his face, words like I was an ornament and they didn’t break you, they just sharpened you sitting between us like fresh blood. 

Now, there is space. Not distance; space.

Room to move.

Room to plan.

I tap the Devotions Hall scrap with my charcoal.

“At the Gala,” I say, “you heard him. When he was talking to Lord Vane about the Prime Contract yields.”

Cyran’s expression tightens, the amusement leaching out.

“Unfortunately,” he says.

“He wasn’t just selling shares of your future kills,” I continue. “He was selling something else. He called them Control Keys.” 

The word tastes wrong in my mouth.

I underline it on the floor until the letters are thick and black.

“Temple magic,” Cyran says.

It’s not a question.

His knuckles whiten around the mug. The damp hair at his temples clings to his skin. He’s gone very, very still—one of those predator-freeze moments his file flagged as a bad sign. 

“You know what they are,” I say.

“Command words,” he replies. Theatrical edge stripped clean out of his voice. “Phrases bound to the blood and bone of a ‘Relic’ at the time of their making. In case we get ideas above our station.” 

“Like autonomy,” I say.

“Exactly like that.”

I drag the charcoal in a short, sharp line.

If Thessen has a Control Key for Cyran, he doesn’t need to persuade him, or entice him, or negotiate. He can simply say the word and drive him like a puppet.

Stomach acid crawls up the back of my throat.

“Do you have one?” I ask.

It comes out gentler than I expected.

Cyran’s jaw works.

“I don’t know,” he says, after a beat. “I have… gaps.”

He taps his temple with one fingertip, a quick, irritated motion.

“They did things with chant and cut and blood,” he continues. “Liturgies. Sequences. There are whole nights I only remember as… impressions. Pressure. Voices. Cold. Then I woke up on a pedestal with a collar and a script.” 

His mouth half-smiles at that last word, but it’s a humorless thing.

“If there’s a key,” he says, “they never told me what it was. That would defeat the purpose, wouldn’t it?”

“Yes,” I say.

My quill-hand itches for paper, for a form, for a line to check that says this is illegal, fix it.

The law has no checkbox for mind-control failsafes carved into people.

“Thessen bragged,” I recall. “‘Temple-favored protocols. Direct compliance. No need for messy negotiations.’” 

Cyran’s shoulders shudder, almost too slight to see.

“That’s one way to describe it,” he says.

I blow out a slow breath.

“If he has the key,” I say, naming the thing even though every hair on my arms rises at it, “then we’re not talking about influence. We’re talking about override. He could put you back on a pedestal with one word and walk you into any room he wanted.”

His eyes meet mine over the forest of parchment.

There’s fear there.

He masks it with a tilt of his mouth, a lazy arch of brows.

“Getting ahead of ourselves, aren’t we, love?” he says lightly. “I’ve only just escaped one murder attempt and had a very cathartic scrub. Must we ruin my morning coffee with the prospect of being made into a marionette?”

“Yes,” I say. “Because that’s my job. Ruiner of mornings.”

He snorts.

It’s a small, unwilling sound.

I press.

“If he has a key, we need to know,” I say. “Not guess. Not hope. Know. And if a key exists, there will be a record. The temple loves its ledgers.”

“Records,” he repeats slowly.

There’s a strange light in his eyes now. Not hope. Not exactly. Something like it, but darker, more jagged.

Where we made you, I think, and bite it back.

“Then,” I continue, “we need to find where those records are, and whether they contain a way to undo a key. Or at least to prove it exists. The Tribunal won’t move on rumour and panic attacks alone.”

“How do you fight a man who owns the law?” he asks, quiet.

“With history,” I say. “With old contracts older than his.” 

“And where,” he asks, “would one find such thrilling bedtime reading?”

“The temple archive?” I hazard.

He shakes his head once.

“Not the pretty one,” he says. “Not the public stacks with their sweet little censored copies for the devout. The real one. The Hollow Archive.” 

He says the name like it tastes of old blood.

“The what,” I ask.

“In the Skull,” he says. “Deep. Below the visible shrines. That’s where they keep the records they don’t want even their own priests to read without permission.”

“Which would include the making of a Blood Relic,” I say.

“Yes.”

“It’s forbidden,” I note.

“Of course it is,” he says. “So are a great many interesting things.”

He takes a careful sip of his horrible coffee.

“They took me there,” he says, after a moment. “Once. Maybe twice. I’m not… clear. I remember bone shelves. Scrolls wrapped in skin. Sigils carved into the walls so deep they hummed when I walked past.” 

His hand curls, unconsciously, into the fabric of his trousers.

“It’s where they made me,” he finishes. “Or at least where they wrote down what they were doing while they made me. If there’s a Command Key, it’ll be in those records.”

Going back there will cost him.

I can see it already. The micro-tremor in his fingers. The way his pupils have blown slightly, breath gone a fraction too shallow at the memory.

No one made me promise to keep him from walking back into his own nightmares.

I decide to do it anyway.

“Cyran,” I say.

His gaze slides to mine.

“Going back there…” I start, then correct myself. “Going in there at all—are you sure you can do that?”

He holds my eyes.

Theatricality drains out of his posture by degrees, like water from a cracked cup.

“No,” he says.

Plain. Unadorned.

“But I am very sure,” he adds, “that I don’t want to be a puppet again. If there’s a backdoor in my head and Thessen has the key, I would like to know before he tries to walk through it.”

My throat tightens.

I nod once.

“Then we go,” I say.

I reach for another scrap, draw a rough circle around the word SKULL DISTRICT. Add a little skull icon next to it because apparently the part of my brain that does gallows humour has decided to wake up. 

“Tonight,” I decide. “Before Thessen stops panicking about his gala long enough to start counting his toys. If he notices you’re gone, he’ll look to the Vigil first. The safe house won’t stay safe once they pull your patrol records and realise this address is in a file.”

“Efficient,” he murmurs. “And here I thought I was the paranoid one.”

“Paranoia is just accurate risk assessment in a hostile environment,” I say.

He blinks.

“Remind me never to play cards with you.”

“Noted.”

My charcoal squeaks faintly as I draw lines—our route from here through the Ribcage, up to the Skull. Streets we can take without obvious patrols. Back alleys I know from years of trudging between low-level shrines and Tribunal satellites. Points where Thessen’s influence is strongest and where we might slip between his fingers. 

Cyran leans forward, setting his mug aside.

Our knees touch over the map.

He doesn’t pull away.

He reaches out, fingertip tracing one of the charcoal lines I’ve drawn.

“You’re risking your career,” he says, without his usual teasing lilt. “For a Relic.”

I sit back on my heels.

My spine cracks, protesting the time I’ve spent folded over the floor. Sunlight catches in the charcoal dust along my knuckles, turning the smears almost silver.

“I’m risking it,” I say, “for a partner.”

The word hangs there.

I feel it land in the air between us like the first step off a glass floor.

His expression shifts—something small, almost imperceptible. The way his mouth softens at the corners. The way his shoulders, always held just a little too ready for impact, relax a fraction. 

“Partner,” he repeats.

His voice is quiet.

“I like that,” he says.

“You contribute more than teeth and cheekbones,” I say matter-of-factly. “You know things. You see patterns in rituals I never learned. This isn’t me dragging a victim along on my righteous crusade. This is… joint treason.”

He laughs, startled.

“A shared crime,” he says. “How intimate.”

“It’s very romantic,” I agree dryly. “Conspiracy is the new courtship.”

He exhales a sound that might be an actual laugh this time.

“There are worse ways to flirt,” he says.

“That was not flirting,” I say.

“It felt a little like flirting,” he says.

“Drink your coffee,” I tell him.

“Yes, Investigator,” he says, but there’s no edge of old obedience in it now. Just warmth.

We work.

He adds notes to my map—small corrections about which alleys flood in marrow-rain, which shrines keep late hours, which stairwells in the Skull district dead-end in locked gates and which lead to forgotten bone galleries. 

His knowledge of the temple’s under-architecture is terrifying and invaluable.

My knowledge of the Tribunal’s blind spots fills in the rest.

We argue, briefly, about whether to skirt the old ossuary on Skull Row.

“It’s haunted,” I point out.

“So are you,” he says. “And I would still take you over a main thoroughfare any night.”

“Flattery will not improve your coffee,” I inform him.

“I have to try something,” he replies.

We compromise. We always do.

By the time the sun has shifted enough that the light-bars on the floor have moved, the map looks less like a messy web and more like a plan. The Skull circled. Our route marked. Key hazards annotated in my precise hand: Watch for bone-wardens. Avoid shrines 7–9 (Thessen donors). Possible Vigil patrol shift change here. 

I sit back, stretching my calves, and smudge a line accidentally with my heel.

Cyran makes a small sound of exaggerated horror.

“You’ve ruined my art,” he gasps.

“It’s not art,” I say. “It’s evidence.”

“Even worse,” he says. “Imagine what they would say at the Tribunal if they saw you defacing perfectly good conspiracy diagrams with your boots.”

“They’d probably promote me,” I say. “Assuming they weren’t trying to have me quietly executed for blowing out a donor’s magic node.” 

He sobers a touch at that.

“They’ll come for you,” he says. “When this settles. Thessen doesn’t like losing control of his favourite toys. And now you’ve… interfered. Repeatedly.”

“I’m aware,” I say.

“And you’re still here,” he says.

“Obviously,” I answer.

He looks at me like I’ve said something impossible.

“Why?” he asks.

“Because I’m not them,” I say. “And because you deserve one person in your life who doesn’t treat you as an investment or a lost cause.”

His throat works.

He looks away first this time, gaze dropping to the floor.

Charcoal dust has drifted onto his bare feet. It darkens the calluses at the base of his toes, the faint scars along the top of his arch. Last night, I watched those feet hit a servants’ balcony ten feet below us and turn potentially lethal impact into an ugly but survivable tumble. 

Now, one of them scuffs slowly over a line of my map, smearing Thessen’s name into a grey blur.

“We should clean this up,” I say, after a moment.

“Is that a metaphor,” he asks, “or are you finally admitting my aesthetic sense is offended?”

“Both,” I say. “If anyone finds this, it is direct evidence we’re planning unauthorized access to a forbidden archive. And I refuse to let you die because of my messy handwriting.”

He presses a hand to his heart.

“I feel so seen,” he murmurs.

We move.

He fetches a rag from the wash alcove, still damp from last night. I knock over the half-empty ink bottle before it can tip onto anything important. Together, we start wiping the floor.

Charcoal smears turn to vague shadow, then to faint ghosts, then vanish under our combined efforts. My scribbled labels blur into nothing. Thessen’s name disappears under the damp cloth. The circles around the Prime Contract Exchange and the Skull District fade. 

“Some archivist somewhere is weeping,” Cyran says. “All that lost information.”

“It’s in my head now,” I say. “I’ll transcribe it later in a less incriminating location.”

“Remind me never to underestimate your ability to hide a war inside a filing cabinet,” he replies.

“It’s a skill,” I say.

We reach the center at the same time.

Our hands brush as we both go for the same stubborn patch of charcoal.

He stills.

So do I.

It’s nothing—skin over bone, a little catch of breath. We’ve been closer. We’ve done stranger things in more cramped quarters. I wiped kohl from his lashes with my thumb last night; he carried me off a balcony like I weighed nothing.

This is different.

There’s no crisis to justify it. No blood. No magic. Just morning, and burnt coffee, and the quiet sounds of a city waking up outside.

He looks up, lashes lowering, pupils dark.

“Lysa,” he says.

“Yes,” I answer.

“This—” He gestures faintly at the fading lines under our hands, at the rough bone walls, at the two chipped mugs on the floor. “This is the strangest version of domesticity I’ve ever experienced.”

“It’s not domesticity,” I say. “It’s destruction of evidence.”

“Let me have my fantasy,” he murmurs.

I snort once, quietly.

“You have terrible fantasies,” I tell him.

“On the contrary,” he says. “Tonight we break into a forbidden archive under a dead god’s skull to steal the secrets of my own making.” His smile goes small, sharp, real. “That’s practically a dream date.”

“This is not a date,” I say.

“It’s a plan,” he agrees.

He sits back on his heels, wiping the last of the charcoal from his fingers onto his trousers.

“All right, Investigator,” he says. “We have a target. We have a route. We have deeply questionable coffee. Anything else we need before we go courting the ghosts of my past?”

“Sleep,” I say. “Food. You need blood before tonight. Real blood, not what you licked off a knife for theatrics.” 

His lips thin.

“I’m fine,” he lies.

“No,” I say, quiet and precise. “You’re functional. That’s different. If we walk into the Hollow Archive with you already running on fumes, you’ll be easier to… push.”

He flinches at the word.

“Do not,” I add, “make me write in my report that my audit subject compromised an operation because he was too proud to eat.”

He grimaces.

“You weaponize bureaucracy in truly terrifying ways,” he says.

“I work with what I have,” I say.

He nods once, slow.

“Later,” he concedes. “We’ll… sort that.”

“We will,” I say.

We will.

Because this is what partnership looks like, I’m realizing. Not grand declarations. Not dramatic sacrifices on temple altars. Just the mundane logistics of survival. Routes and rations and risk assessments. Planning treason on the floor and then scrubbing the aftermath off the boards together.

I straighten, spine popping.

“Get some rest,” I tell him. “We go at first full dark.”

He rises more fluidly than I do, in one long unfurl, then offers me a hand up.

I take it.

It’s warm, steady, calloused in ways that have nothing to do with ornamental work and everything to do with blades and walls and bone. 

He pulls me to my feet.

For a moment, we stand too close in the bright strip of sunlight, my charcoal-stained fingers wrapped in his clean ones, the map gone, the plan set.

“Partner,” he says again, almost to himself.

“Yes,” I say.

And for the first time since I put on a Tribunal robe, the word feels like something I chose, not something assigned.
