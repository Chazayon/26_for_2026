# Chapter 11 — *The Art of Being Decorative*
### Cyran | POV

The problem with the living is that they are so dreadfully obvious about their heartbeats.

The Hanging Gardens are full of them.

Midday light knifes down between bone-arches and hanging vines, turning the upper tier of the Ribcage into a riot of colour and shadow that makes my head pound. Awnings in every offensive shade of red and yellow snap in the thin, corpse-warm breeze. Traders shout, knives chop on bone-blocks, children shriek with the sort of joy that sounds far too much like terror if you’ve heard enough of the latter.

And underneath all of it, like a drum someone has put right up against my ear, a thousand mortal hearts thud-thud-thud in staggering, graceless time.

I walk through the middle of it like a dropped stitch in an otherwise tidy tapestry, and the fabric of the crowd warps around me.

They part. Of course they do. They always do. People see the Vigil black, the sigils on my bracers, and something old and useful in their spines remembers what happens to those who do not get out of a predator’s way.

I am accustomed to that.

What I am not accustomed to is the fact that the woman actually in front of me does not seem to notice any of it.

Lysa Vaskin moves through the mortal market like she belongs here. Dust-coloured Tribunal robes, sleeves rolled to her elbows in outright defiance of decorum, boots already stained with whatever passes for soil in this district. Her satchel bumps lightly against her hip, full of ledgers and charcoal and the sort of laws people like Contract Lord Thessen think they’ve already corrupted beyond use.

Her shoulders are set with intent the way they were last night in the safe house, when we sat hip to hip in an ossuary while my blood sizzled back together under her skin and she said words like given, not taken as if they weren’t blasphemy.

My goal, today, is simple enough that it might even be achievable: guide her through this mess without anyone stabbing her, sacrificing her, or selling her something with extra limbs; and at the same time prove, to both of us, that I belong in the shadows and not at her side.

All she has to do is see it. The way they look at me. The way the light hits the old temple scars when my collar shifts. The way children’s mothers clutch them closer when I pass.

She will see. Eventually.

Everyone does.

She walks a half-step ahead, which is adorable, as if she could possibly be the one clearing a path. I let her have the illusion. In practice, my body keeps slipping fractionally between her and the worst of the crowd without consulting me at all.

An argument at a butcher’s stall to the left—someone waving a cleaver like punctuation. I angle us right.

A slick young cutpurse with too-bright eyes melting out of an alley to the right. My gaze finds him, follows, and a flicker of my Mark-glow along my bracer is enough to send him scuttling for a safer mark.

Above us, on the third-tier balcony of a rib-hung townhouse, a Vigil rifle glints, barrel resting on bone crenelation. Sniper. Ours. He watches the market with bored precision, the way I used to before I became the show instead of the security.

“I can feel you cataloguing threats,” Lysa says without looking back, voice carrying clearly over the murmur and clatter. “You’re twitching.”

“I do not twitch, Investigator,” I say. “I glide. There’s a difference.”

“Your jaw is grinding.” One ink-stained hand lifts, and she adjusts her spectacles, head tilting to squint at something ahead. “You’re going to crack a tooth.”

“My teeth and I have an understanding.” I shift my shoulders against the weight of the daylight, the back of my tongue still tasting faintly of her. It has been a full day since the ossuary, and I am not even slightly hungry. That is almost worse. “Besides, if I didn’t grind something, how else would I cope with… this?”

I gesture with two fingers at the market in general: the colour, the sunshine-bones, the very loud, very fragrant press of humanity.

A woman with a basket of bruised fungi on her arm glances up as my hand moves. Her gaze hits my face, then drops to the carved sigils on my forearm where my sleeve has ridden back. The colour drains from her cheeks. She yanks her child closer by the wrist so sharply the boy yelps.

And then, of course, he bolts.

Small, pale blur, legs and elbows and ragged tunic, darting across our path in a diagonal that would be suicidal even if there weren’t a Blood Relic in it.

My body moves before the part of my mind that has decided to be dignified today remembers to object. One step sideways, weight light, hand catching air where a collar might have been if I’d been faster. The child shoots past, barely brushing my coat. His scent hits me in a ridiculous little flare of sugar and sweat.

His mother makes a noise like someone’s just poured boiling water over her.

She lunges, snatching him up under the arms, scrambling backward until her spine hits a rib-wall with a hollow thud. The basket swings, fungi go flying. Her eyes squeeze shut as if that will help. She turns her face away, mouth working soundlessly, one palm splayed over the boy’s hair as if expecting me to pounce.

The crowd nearest us hushes in a little ripple. Not silence, not here, but a thinning of sound as people pretend not to look.

There it is. Exhibit A.

Something in my chest tightens, reflexive and sharp. The old wound where pride and humiliation sleep side by side surges awake.

Ah yes. This.

I incline myself in a sweeping, courtly bow, one hand at my waist, the other cutting a little arc through the air. A performance, so polished I could do it in my sleep.

“My apologies, madam,” I say, honey poured over razors. “You’re quite right to be cautious. The monsters they let into the market these days are terribly undertrained.”

The word monsters hits the air like a tossed coin.

Her fingers spasm in the child’s hair. She nods, a jerky, terrified motion, eyes still screwed shut, as if answering a priest.

The boy peeks around her arm, eyes huge, trying to reconcile the pretty, bowing thing in front of him with all the stories he’s been told about teeth and blood and dark alleyways.

“Cyran.” Lysas hand closes on my sleeve just above the wrist. “We’re going to be late.”

She doesn’t tug hard. She doesn’t have to. The touch is a small, solid anchor in the middle of all that flinching.

I let her pull me upright and forward.

As we pass the woman, Lysa says, without heat, without kindness either, “Hold on to him because he’s yours. Not because of him.”

She doesn’t look at me when she says it. She doesn’t have to for me to know exactly who that last pronoun is.

The mother makes a strangled little sound that might be agreement, might be an aborted prayer. We are already swept back into the current.

I roll my shoulders once, shake the stiffness from my fingers. The performance still sits under my skin like a rash.

“You see?” I murmur, falling back into step at Lysas side. “Public relations at work. I frighten the children so the Tribunal doesn’t have to.”

She huffs. Not quite a laugh. “You bow to terrified mothers,” she says. “That isn’t frightening. It’s petty.”

“Petty can be a survival strategy.”

“Mm.” Her eyes flash sideways. “Or a wall.”

I am saved from having to decide whether to answer that by the fact that we’ve reached our destination.

The old archway looks smaller in the harsh light than it did the last time I saw it.

Once, it would have been imposing: a rib of god-bone split and levered into place, carved with High-Divine script so deep the grooves would catch sacrificial blood and smoke. Now, ivy has colonised the hollows, dark leaves and pale tendrils choking the sigils, softening the edges. The bone is chalky with age, flaking where the script has weathered thin.

I know exactly what it used to say. My fingers remember even if I will not.

“Here.” Lysa steps forward, oblivious to the way three merchants at the nearest stall abruptly decide now is an excellent time to abandon their stock and vanish into a side alley, hands hiding their faces. “Look at this.”

She drops her satchel to the ground with a thud, pulls out her notebook, and flips it open to a fresh page. The charcoal pencil appears out of nowhere, like a conjuring trick, already smudged along her fingertips from previous work.

She reaches up with her free hand, brushing ivy aside. Bone-dust sifts down in a little puff; she inhales it without flinching, spectacles catching the light in a brief, sharp flash.

I take up my post by the arch like an obedient statue, back pressed against cool, pitted bone. The ozone stink of dead magic clings to it, a faint, sharp tang under the cumin and frying fat. Once, the script here would have thrummed with power, command phrases coiling through my blood, noose-tight.

Now it smells like an old scar. The kind people pretend not to see.

Lysa leans in close, breath fogging the bone, and traces one eroded line with the tip of her finger before she starts sketching, translating groove into graphite.

Something in my chest loosens at the sight.

She is just… working. Totally absorbed. Robes hitched a little higher on her calves because the market dust is clinging; curls have escaped the severity of her braid and are stuck damply to her neck from the heat. The sun—or whatever counts as it in this carcass—glints off the edge of her spectacles.

She looks like what she is: a scribe who has decided the truth matters more than the comfort of the men paying her wage.

My head still aches from the light. My senses are too sharp; every shout is a jab, every clatter of dropped marrow-bones a little explosion. Daytime is a persistent insult to creatures like me. We are meant to be beautiful in candlelight, not squinting in full glare.

But I would stand here all damned afternoon if it means watching her draw another line beneath Thessens throat.

“Third shrine this week,” she murmurs, mostly to herself. Charcoal scratches briskly. “Sigils match the ones in the Halo Ward marginalia. Jurisdictional overreach.”

“You say the most romantic things,” I say. “Say more about jurisdiction. I’m almost moved.”

Her mouth twitches. She does not look away from the page. “You’re going to have to listen to a great deal of jurisdictional argument if this works,” she says. “You may as well acclimate now.”

“Mm. I’ll acclimate to anything if it keeps me out of a ceremonial collar again.”

The word collar comes out sharper than I intend. My hand has already moved, fingers finding my throat, rubbing at the place where the leather of my cravat hides the pale ring of temple-brand scar.

Lysa glances over, quick and assessing, then goes back to her sketch.

“You can go stand in the shade if the light is making your head worse,” she says. Practical, as always. “I don’t need you to—”

“There’s a pickpocket three stalls down eyeing your satchel,” I interrupt. “I’m afraid you’re stuck with me.”

Her pencil stills. “Describe him.”

“Tall for his age, more elbows than sense, shirt used to be blue, now trying for brown. Left hand keeps drifting toward his beltknife when he thinks no one’s watching. His accomplice is the moss-seller pretending not to look at us.”

She doesn’t turn. “Is he still looking?”

“Not anymore.” I let my gaze slide over the boy, let him really see me seeing him. He goes sheet-white under the grime, mutters something to his friend, and vanishes.

Lysa resumes sketching. “You realise you could just arrest him,” she says. “You are technically the law here.”

“Darling, the last thing this district needs is me dragging some starving child in front of a Magistrate for petty theft.” I let my eyes drift up to the rib-arches overhead, where the sniper still waits, bored, gun a dark line against pale bone. “I’m much better used as a deterrent and occasional executioner.”

“That’s not a job description,” she says. “That’s an indictment.”

“Oh, Investigator.” I smile, sharp and thin. “Everything about me is an indictment. I thought you’d read the file.”

She goes quiet at that. Not cowed—she doesn’t do cowed—but measuring.

Somewhere behind us, two merchants are hissing to each other in the sort of stage whisper no one ever thinks anyone will actually hear.

“Thessens hounds were sniffing around the old shrines again last night,” one murmurs.

“Hush,” the other hisses. “You want that Relic thinking you know things? Keep your head down.”

Hounds. Lovely. As if the Contract Lord didn’t have enough teeth.

Lysa’s shoulders draw a little tighter at the word, but she doesn’t turn. Instead, she shifts her grip on the pencil, blows bone-dust off the page, and gives the arch one last, intent look, as if she can wrench the secrets out of it by will alone.

Her stomach growls.

Loudly.

She freezes for half a heartbeat, as if offended by her own biology, then exhales through her nose and rubs at her midriff with her free hand.

I bite the inside of my cheek to keep from smiling properly. It’s… endearing, that she can stand in front of the bones of an ex-god-legality dispute and still be betrayed by something as mundane as hunger.

“That,” I say, “was not structural instability.”

“Don’t.” The word is flat, but her ears have gone very slightly pink. She tucks the notebook away, dusts her hands on her robes, and gestures with her chin toward a stall where a column of smoke is rising in an inviting, greasy plume. “We’re getting food.”

“Are we?” I arch a brow. “You do realise I derive no nutritional benefit from charred mystery-meat.”

“You derive benefit from not collapsing in front of half the district,” she says. “And from pretending to be something resembling mortal while we work. Come on.”

She has a point. I hate that she has a point. I follow anyway.

The stall is a simple thing: a brazier cobbled together from bent metal and bone, skewers of meat lined up along the edge, fat hissing as it drips onto coals. Cumin and burnt salt, a hint of dried fungus and old smoke. Flies buzz, bold and hopeful.

The man tending it is middle-aged, with a burn scar crawling up from the corner of one eye into a nest of creases at his temple. His apron is a crime scene of grease. He looks up at the sound of our approach.

His gaze hits Lysa first. Tribunal robes. Crest at her throat. His mouth starts to form the automatic smile of someone who knows which side of the law to flatter.

Then he sees me.

The colour drains from his face. The iron tongs slip from his fingers and hit the side of the brazier with a clatter, sending a spray of sparks into the air. He jerks back, nearly tripping over his own charcoal bin.

“Ml–mlord Relic,” he stammers. His hands are shaking so hard I half-expect the skewers to leap off the grill on their own out of sympathy. “Forgive—I didn’t—You weren’t—”

“Breathing?” I supply sweetly. “Don’t worry, I forget to do that sometimes myself.”

He makes a strangled noise.

Lysa steps neatly into the space between his panic and my performance.

“Two gristle-skewers, extra cumin,” she says, utterly calm, as if she orders food beside walking weapons every day. “And whatever you gave the last labourer who looked like he hadn’t slept.”

He blinks at her, confused by the normalcy. “I—yes. Yes, Auditor. At once.”

He snatches up the tongs, nearly drops them again, and applies himself to the skewers with frenetic energy.

I drift to the side of the cart, close enough that the heat of the coals washes over my already overstimulated skin, and set a Tribunal silver down on the warped wood.

He flinches as if I’ve slapped him.

“Th–that’s too much,” he blurts, then clamps his mouth shut, eyes darting to my face and then away. “Apologies, mlord. I didn’t—I mean, as you wish—”

“Consider it hazard pay,” I say, voice going very smooth. “For the mortal terror.”

His laugh is a pathetic, strangled thing that dies in his throat.

Lysa’s eyes flick to the coin, then to me. Something in them narrows. She doesn’t call me on it—not here, not in front of him—but I can already feel the ledger forming in her head. Excessive. Theatrical. Mask.

He hands over two paper-wrapped skewers with hands that have not stopped trembling. He keeps his gaze safely fixed on a point somewhere around my knees.

“Thank you,” Lysa says. She takes both, cones of hot grease and spice steaming in the air between us, and steps away from the crush of the crowd.

I fall into place beside her, the line of her shoulder drawn tight with something that is not just hunger.

We find an alcove under the curve of a massive rib, where some long-dead architect decided to hollow out a space for storage and time decided it was better used for loitering. The rib overhead throws us into shade, mercifully muting the glare. The air in here is a degree cooler, still stinking of market, but less like someone shouting directly into my bones.

Lysa folds herself down onto the ledge as if it’s the most natural thing in the world to sit in a god’s chest and eat lunch. She hands me one skewer without looking.

I take it because it’s either that or let it drip on her boots.

The wood is hot against my glove, heat bleeding easily through the leather. The meat wears a thick coat of dark-red spice, charred at the edges, grease already seeping through the paper to smear my fingers. Up this close, the smell is… intense. Burnt animal and cumin and something faintly sour trying very hard to pretend it isn’t.

Her blood tasted like lightning and iron and rage. This smells like a stall trying to cover the fact that it bought the scraps no one else wanted.

Lysa bites in without hesitation.

Her teeth sink into the meat, tearing through the stringy fibres. Grease and red spice smudge the corner of her mouth. She chews, swallows, makes a small, satisfied sound that hits me in an entirely inappropriate place.

“You’re going to have to stop looking at me like that when I’m eating,” she says, deadpan. “Or the vendors will assume their wares are… more interesting than they are.”

“Perish the thought,” I murmur.

I bring my own skewer to my lips, more for the performance than any expectation of pleasure. The first bite is… not good. The meat is overcooked and under-cleaned, the spice so heavy it’s almost gritty on my tongue. It tastes like ash and old fat.

I swallow it down anyway, let my face stay carefully neutral.

“Thoughts?” she asks.

“It’s…” I make a show of considering, eyes on the traffic beyond the alcove. “A bit stringy.”

“Mm.” Another bite. The spice smear grows. “That’s because it’s mostly tendon. Cuts cheaper.”

“Ah.” I roll the next piece between my teeth, trying to find anything about it that isn’t offensive. “That explains why it feels like chewing through a god-spawn’s inner thigh.”

There. That ought to do it. Most of the Tribunal donors who ever shared casual conversation with me went green at half that level of imagery. One memorable senator had to sit down.

Lysa doesn’t gag. She doesn’t flinch. She doesn’t even pause.

She chews, swallows, and lifts one brow.

“You must have had particularly stringy god-spawn,” she says. “These are nowhere near that bad.”

My smirk falters.

“Investigator,” I say gently. “You are supposed to chastise me for ruining your appetite. There’s a script here.”

“I’m not here to follow your script, Cyran.” She licks a smear of spice from the pad of her thumb, entirely unselfconscious. My fangs ache in sympathetic recognition. “If you’re trying to prove you’re monstrous over street food, you’re going to have to try harder.”

“That was a very good god-spawn comparison,” I protest, stung in ridiculous places. “I even included texture.”

“Yes,” she says. “And I’ve seen you elbow-deep in a half-born in the cisterns. This is not more disturbing than that.”

I stare at her for a beat too long. Something cold and a little panicked slides into my gut.

This was supposed to be easy. I make a little joke about devouring things, she recoils, she remembers what I am, and we both inch back into our safe, mutually beneficial positions: her with her ledgers, me with my claws.

Instead, she sits in a gods shadow eating tendon like it’s the most natural thing in the world, calling my bluff.

I take another bite. It tastes fouler than the first. My body doesn’t need it; the meat hits my stomach and sits there, sullen and heavy.

I shove the skewer into the little brazier someone has wedged in the corner of the alcove to burn scraps. It lands with a hiss, spice flaring bright for a second before going to dull ember.

Lysa watches the motion, then turns back to me.

The air has changed. The little social performance of street food and shared filth is over. The space between us feels smaller, tighter, despite the fact that we’re not touching.

“What are you doing?” she asks.

“Failing to assimilate,” I say lightly. “Apologies. My humanity must have been lost somewhere between the temple altar and the Redemption Office.”

She doesn’t smile.

She shifts, turning on the ledge so that her knees are angled toward me. Her boots bump my shin. Her skirts brush my thigh. She is close enough now that I can see the faint scatter of freckles across the bridge of her nose, the smudge of charcoal on the side of her index finger where it rests against the paper cone.

“You know that arch,” she says.

The words are not a question.

My spine goes cold.

There’s a beat—a single, suspended instant—where I could pretend not to understand. Where I could tilt my head and say something charmingly evasive.

Instead, every nerve in my body gives me away before my mouth can even form a lie.

My shoulders tense. My hand, halfway to some lazy, dismissive gesture, curls into a claw against my knee. My gaze flicks, without my consent, back toward the ruined script half-choked in ivy.

Lysa sees all of it. Of course she does. That is her job.

“How long,” she asks, voice very calm, “did Varos keep you in this district?”

I take a half-step back without meaning to. The wall is closer than I thought. My spine meets bone with a dull thud.

The taste of the skewers vanishes under the sudden, clean tang of old fear.

“Darling,” I say, reaching blindly for the mask, “if you wanted my tragic backstory, you should have bought a cheaper lunch. I do those for free with a bottle of bad wine and a more flattering angle.”

“Cyran.” She leans forward. Not looming—she doesn’t loom, she’s too small and too precise for that—but entering my space with the kind of deliberate intent that makes evasion feel ridiculous. The paper cone crackles in her hand. Grease freckles the dust by her boot. “I need to know.”

“Need is such a strong word.” My laugh comes out a little too sharp. “Surely want would suffice? All the best gossip is based on want.”

“Thessens hounds are already sniffing around those shrines.” Her tone doesn’t change, but the words land like blows. “If he can tie you to them, he will. He’ll frame you as a willing participant in whatever Varos did there. I need context if I’m going to keep you from being—”

“Reset?” I supply, mouth twisting. “Reconditioned? Retired? Pick your favourite euphemism. The temple was very fond of language. They wrote all sorts of lovely phrases for what it means to take a boy and turn him into a centrepiece.”

The word hangs between us, heavier than it has any right to be.

“Centre…piece,” she repeats.

I flash her a grin I do not feel. “Altar decoration. Sacred ornament. Pick your art critique. I lit up beautifully under the right blood-light. People left their prayers and their shame at my feet. It was all very moving.”

The air in the alcove goes very still.

Her eyes search my face, mapping the lines for cruelty, for mockery, for the usual little glint that says I’m only half-serious, darling, why are you so upset?

She doesn’t find it. I know she doesn’t because I can’t find it either.

“That’s not…” She exhales slowly, anger shading the edges of the sound. “That’s not a joke, Cyran.”

“Everything about me is a joke, Investigator.” My voice is too bright. I can hear it. Can’t stop it. “Did no one brief you on that? I’m the city’s favourite horror story. The temple’s little legacy. ‘Be good, children, or the pretty Relic will come and—’”

“Stop.” The word is quiet. Precise. It hits harder than a shout would.

I do. The habit is old, muscle-deep. The command phrases may have been rebranded and re-scripted by the Tribunal, but some wires were set in bone too early to be entirely undone.

Lysa sees that too. Her gaze drops, for a bare second, to my throat. Then to the way my fingers have crept up toward it again, rubbing at the place where metal used to bite.

She sets the half-eaten skewer carefully on the ledge beside her, wipes her hand once, briskly, on a handkerchief, and stands.

Two steps, no hesitation, and she’s directly in front of me.

Close. So close I can see the tiny flecks of ink on her jaw where she clearly swiped at some earlier smudge and missed a spot. Close enough that if I breathed with any regularity, I’d be filling my lungs with nothing but her and spice and the faint ghost of Tribunal soap.

I go very still.

If this were anyone else, the closeness would be prelude to something obvious: a kiss, a slap, a whispered threat. With Lysa, it could be any of those or something worse: a question I don’t know how to answer.

She looks up at me, eyes steady behind the glass.

“Tell me what he did,” she says. No flourishes. No polite softening. “Not the temple version. Not the euphemisms. You don’t have to give me details you can’t bear, but I need the shape of it. How he used you here.”

Panic flares in my chest, hot and bright.

My first instinct is always the same: seduce, distract, deflect. I let my weight tilt forward, let my mouth curve, let my voice drop.

“Investigator,” I murmur. “If you wanted to drag me into old temple rites, you only had to say. I have a few I could teach you that don’t involve paperwork.”

Her expression doesn’t change. Not even a flicker.

“Dark humour is not an answer,” she says. “And it’s not going to work on me anymore.”

“Anymore?” I echo, stung in ways I refuse to name. “Have I lost my touch?”

“You never lost it,” she says. “I just stopped letting you use it on yourself.”

That does something strange and unpleasant behind my sternum.

I look away, over her shoulder, out toward the market. People move past our little pocket of shade, careful not to look directly in. A boy balances a crate of wilted greens on his head. A woman counts coins twice before handing them over. Life, trundling on over the bones of other people’s gods.

“I was here,” I say finally, because the silence feels like a hand closing around my throat, “for six years. On and off. Varos liked the Gardens. The light caught the blood nicely when it dripped down the arches.”

I can feel her watching me. Weighing each word.

“And you…” Her voice is very soft now. Not pitying. Careful. “Were the focus of the rites.”

“I was the garnish,” I say, because if I don’t make it sound ridiculous, I might remember how it felt. “They brought the monsters. They brought the penitent. They brought the donors. I was the pretty thing that made the whole tableau palatable. People will watch a flaying if you give them a charming enough host.”

My mouth tastes like old incense and iron. The market sounds dim, as if someone has put cotton between me and the world.

“I remember…” My fingers have knotted themselves in the front of my coat. I force them to loosen, one at a time. “Standing under that arch with a collar around my throat and Varos’ hand on the chain. I remember the command phrases carved into the bone making my knees hit the floor before my mind caught up. I remember thinking, if I smile beautifully enough, maybe they won’t see the way my hands are shaking.”

Lysa doesn’t move. She doesn’t reach for me. She doesn’t look away.

“Thank you,” she says simply.

That jolts me. “For traumatising anecdote hour?”

“For telling the truth.” She lifts her notebook, which I hadn’t even noticed she’d angled upright against her hip, and taps the spine lightly against my chest. Not hard enough to bruise. Just enough to make me feel the weight of it. “Now when Thessen tries to dress those rites up as devotion, I’ll have language to call them what they were.”

“Abuse,” I say, before I can stop myself.

“Yes,” she answers. “That.”

The word hangs there. Heavy. Final. Something inside me that has been twisting in on itself for decades spasms.

I want to make a joke. I want to say something cruel. I want to kiss her until neither of us can talk.

Instead, I push my shoulder off the bone, away from the wall, away from the press of her gaze. I straighten my cravat with hands that only shake a little.

“I’ve just ruined your lunch,” I say, aiming for lightness and missing by a wide margin. “Do mark that down in your little book. ‘Subject proved unsuitable for casual market dates; tendency toward melodramatic oversharing when exposed to archival architecture.’”

Her mouth does that thing where it almost smiles and then decides against it.

“I’ll make a note,” she says. “Right next to, ‘Subject attempts to weaponise self-loathing, fails.’”

“Fails?” I echo. “How rude.”

She steps back, finally, giving me room to breathe I do not technically need. Then she turns, smooth and decisive, and tucks the notebook away.

“Come on,” she says. “We have three more arches to find before sundown. And I have to get back to the Spine before Voss starts wondering why my field audit includes roasted tendon and god-blood fumes.”

“Mm. Can’t have that.” I fall into step beside her as she steps back into the sun. The heat hits like a slap; my head throbs in time with the distant, sullen beat of the dead gods heart. “The paperwork would be unbearable.”

She pauses at the edge of the alcove and looks back at me over her shoulder.

“For the record, Cyran,” she says. “I’m not going to stop asking. And I’m not going to leave because I don’t like the answers.”

My heart—which is not, technically, supposed to do much of anything anymore—gives one, traitorous lurch.

“Give it time, love,” I say softly. “Everyone leaves.”

She holds my gaze a beat longer, something fierce and unyielding in her eyes, then steps into the crowd.

I trail after her, a shadow stitched to her heels, watching the way the mortals flinch and scurry and avert their eyes as we pass.

I tell myself I am relieved that she still walks ahead, that there is still distance, still room for her to see sense and step away from whatever this is before it ruins us both.

But as the market noise swallows us, as the sniper above shifts position and Thessens invisible hounds keep sniffing at old scars, all I can think is this:

I pushed her. Harder than I meant to.

And she’s still here.

I have no idea what to do with that.
