# Chapter 18 — *The Library of Skin*
### Lysa | POV

In the Tribunal, morning is an assault.

Iron bells, shouted roll calls, the scrape of chairs against stone as clerks and advocates fling themselves into another day of grinding down the city’s bones. 

Here, it arrives as a quiet streak of gray light across his bare shoulder.

For a few disorienting seconds, my brain refuses to reconcile the two.

Gray Spine light. Rumpled white sheet. Cyran.

His arm is heavy across my waist, deadweight in the most literal sense. His hand lies open against my stomach, fingers curled slightly like he fell asleep holding something and forgot to let go. 

Me.

My body aches in a way that is entirely new and painfully obvious. Muscles I didn’t know I owned are making themselves heard. There is sweat dried in the hollow of my throat, and the inside of my thighs feels… used. In a good way. Sore, but not harmed. That distinction matters more than I expected it to. 

The room is cold.

My breath fogs faintly in the air between us. The hearth has burned down to a stubborn clutch of embers, red eyes peering through gray ash. Outside, the marrow‑rain hasn’t stopped; it patters against the bone‑glass like fingers drumming impatiently.

I should get up.

Rebuild the fire, put water on, start listing statutes in my head so I don’t forget the arguments I meant to make in my written refusal. Check the time. Count how many hours remain on the seventy‑two I bought with ink and insolence. 

Instead, I lie there and look at him.

Cyran, asleep, is a different creature than the one who prowls Tribunal corridors and marrow‑alleys. The tension has drained from his face. No theatrical arch to his brows, no practiced smirk waiting at the corners of his mouth. Just a man, lashes dark against his cheeks, lips parted slightly on slow, unnecessary breaths. 

The faint silvery lines of old sigils catch the weak light along his shoulder, ghostly script etched into pale skin. I resist the urge to trace them. Last night I touched every line he let me, with his full consent and mine. This morning, I don’t have that permission yet. Not while he’s like this, mind elsewhere, defenses down.

He is actually, genuinely asleep.

I didn’t think he could. Not really. Not the way mortals do, abandoning the watch for more than a handful of minutes. Even in the safe house he always seemed half‑coiled, the kind of rest you nap into on a battlefield, boots still on.

Now he’s sprawled across my mattress, taking up most of the blankets, dead weight pinning me comfortably in place. 

There is no script for the morning after you cross a line you spent weeks defending.

I inhale, slow.

His scent is all over me: cold air, iron, the faint ghost of temple myrrh that clings to his nerves, and underneath it, now, smoke from my hearth and the clean, surprisingly ordinary smell of my soap. 

Mine, some traitorous part of me thinks.

His fingers twitch.

He goes very still.

Then his whole body jolts like someone yanked a chain.

His arm tightens around my waist—too tight, bruising—before flinging itself away as if burned. He scrambles backward in a clatter of bones and linen, eyes snapping open, pupils blown wide in the gray light. The sheet tangles around his hips; he claws it up, dragging it over his chest like armor.

For a heartbeat, he is not here.

I can see it in the way his gaze skates over the ceiling, the window, the door, as if cataloguing exits and altars.

“Cyran,” I say.

I keep my voice low. Steady. I do not reach for him.

His head jerks toward me.

The panic in his eyes is so raw I feel it like a physical impact.

The last time he woke like this, it was to temple handlers and command phrases. I know that now. His muscles are braced for a blow, a command, a pair of hands dragging him somewhere he doesn’t want to go.

“Good morning,” I add.

Two simple words.

They land like a wedge in the spinning gears of his fear.

He blinks.

The room reassembles itself around him. My narrow bed. The single sagging shelf. The heap of yesterday’s robes on the chair. The fact that the only other person here is me, sitting up slowly, sheet pulled to my chest more for warmth than modesty.

“Right,” he says hoarsely. “Morning. I should—”

He gestures vaguely toward the floor, where his leather trousers lie in a rumpled heap.

“Go,” he finishes, voice turning brittle around the edges of the word. 

There it is.

The exit.

The neat, sharp line he can cut between last night and every morning after he’s ever had: you get dressed, you leave, you pretend it was a transaction or a mistake, and the world tilts back onto its old, cruel axis.

I sit up, drawing the quilt around myself. The cold air hits my bare skin; gooseflesh prickles along my arms. I welcome it. It keeps me sharp.

“Why?” I ask.

He freezes halfway to bent, trousers in hand.

He looks at me like I’ve just asked him to recite the founding statutes backwards.

“Because…” He flounders. “Because that’s what one does after…” A helpless little wave of his hand, taking in the bed, the sheet, the lines on my neck he left there with his mouth.

I arch a brow.

“After sex?” I supply.

His mouth twitches.

“Yes, Auditor,” he says, brittle humor scraping through. “After that.”

I keep my tone bland.

“It’s raining,” I say. “And I have coffee.”

He stares.

“You…” His voice drops to something thin and disbelieving. “You want me to stay. For breakfast.”

“I do,” I say.

No explanations. No hedging.

He stands there, trousers bunched in white‑knuckled fists, looking thoroughly undone by the idea that the morning after could contain anything other than exit strategies. 

Before he can manufacture a reason to argue, someone tries to pound my door off its hinges.

The knock is sharp, rhythmic, entirely too chipper for this hour.

Cyran drops into a defensive crouch so fast the mattress barely has time to bounce. Every line of his body snakes toward the sound: fangs descended, muscles bunched, eyes gone predator‑dark.

“It’s just the ration runner,” I whisper, already swinging my legs over the side of the bed.

My feet hit the cold floor; the shock of it bites through last night’s warmth.

He melts into the shadows near the wardrobe, a trick I’ve seen him use in alleys and corridors. One step sideways, and he’s half‑absorbed by the gloom, pale skin and pale scars turned to ghost‑stripes instead of glaring targets. 

I grab my thick wool robe from its hook and shrug into it, pulling it tight around me. The fabric is coarse, the kind of institutional gray that never looked good on anyone, but it’s warm and it covers enough that Olan or anyone else in the corridor can’t make a reportable story out of it.

I crack the door, keeping my body between the opening and the room.

Pippin stands there with his usual too‑wide smile, cheeks red from the marrow‑chill, curls plastered damply to his forehead. He can’t be more than sixteen. Tribunal livery hangs off his thin frame like he borrowed it from an older brother. 

“Morning, Auditor Voss,” he chirps. “Two cave‑fowl eggs, marrow‑milk, and bread.” He hefts the metal ration tin a little higher.

Behind me, I can feel Cyran’s attention like a drawn bow.

“Thank you, Pippin,” I say, taking the tin. It’s still warm from the kitchens. “Here.”

I slip a copper chit into his chapped hand.

His eyes go wide.

“You don’t have to—”

“Yes, I do,” I say. “Go home before you freeze.”

He ducks his head, grinning, and scampers off down the corridor, boots squeaking on damp stone. I shut the door, slide the bolt home with a solid clack.

Only then do I exhale.

Cyran steps out of the shadow as if someone peeled him from the wall.

He’s pulled his trousers on in the time it took me to deal with Pippin. Bare chest, bare feet, hair a wild, dark snarl around his face. The daylight is less forgiving than the fire; the circles under his eyes are darker, the edges of old scars more pronounced. 

He eyes the tin in my hands like it might bite him.

“That’s how you live?” he asks slowly. “Like an inmate?”

I huff a laugh.

“It’s efficient,” I say, setting the tin on the small hearth ledge.

“Said every jailer ever,” he mutters.

He’s not wrong.

The ration tin clatters as I flip the latch. Inside, two eggs loll in a shallow pool of pale marrow‑milk, pale yolks wobbling obscenely. A hunk of hard, dark bread lurks in the corner like a sullen rock. Tribunal issue. Nutritious, flavorless, geared toward keeping scribes upright and docile rather than happy.

I move to the hearth and crouch, reaching into the ashes with the poker to coax the embers back to life. They glow brighter under the disturbance, then cough up a fragile tongue of flame when I add a small marrow‑log.

The process is soothing.

Simple cause and effect: fuel, air, spark.

Unlike law.

Unlike feelings.

Behind me, I can hear Cyran hovering.

It’s an absurdly specific sound—weight shifting from foot to foot, the faint rasp of a hand running over the back of a chair, a man with nowhere to put himself.

“You can sit,” I say over my shoulder. “The furniture won’t bite.”

“Forgive me if I don’t trust Tribunal craftsmanship,” he replies.

Still, when I glance back, he’s lowered himself onto the edge of the lone chair, hands braced on his knees, eyes tracking my every motion like I might vanish between one blink and the next. 

The eggs hit the pan with a wet plop.

I crack them one‑handed, shell fragments clicking against iron. The sizzle comes a moment later: hot fat, bubbling marrow‑milk, the quiet, obscene little sound of sustenance transforming. 

The smell fills the room slowly—rich, heavy, undercut by the sharp, bitter edge of the dark Tribunal coffee I spoon into the dented pot and set beside the pan. My stomach grumbles. I ignore it.

“Sit properly,” I say. “I can’t concentrate on not burning this if I keep seeing you perched like an anxious gargoyle in my periphery.”

He snorts.

“That’s racist,” he says. “To gargoyles.”

Still, he shifts, settling more fully into the chair, spine loosening away from its defensive angle by a fraction of an inch.

The robe’s hem brushes my ankles as I move between hearth and table, between stove and cupboard. The floor is cold under my bare feet; the sensation keeps me rooted in my body when my mind tries to sprint ahead—to Thessen’s ledger, to Voss’s frown, to the ticking hours.

Not yet.

One thing at a time.

I salvage two mismatched ceramic plates from the shelf, their edges chipped in different places. Two cups. One proper mug, thick‑handled and sturdy, and one thin, cracked thing that leaks unless you drink fast.

I give him the proper mug.

If he notices, he doesn’t comment.

By the time the eggs have set and the bread has crisped in the pan’s leftover fat, the fire has rallied into a decent blaze. The room is still chilly, but the air directly around the hearth is pleasantly warm, flushing my cheeks and making wisps of damp hair curl at my temples. 

I balance the plates and cups to the table, set them down between scattered ledgers and capped inkwells, and gesture with my chin.

“Sit,” I repeat.

He obeys this time without protest, sliding into the chair opposite mine.

The table between us is scarred bone‑wood, its surface carved with the ghosts of a hundred old calculations and idly scratched sigils. It feels like neutral ground—halfway between the bed’s too‑intimate tangle and the door’s imminent threat.

He looks down at his plate like it’s an unsolvable riddle.

“I don’t… eat,” he says, almost apologetic. “Not this.” He nudges the eggs with his fork. “It won’t do anything.”

“I know,” I say, tearing off a piece of bread and dipping it into the yolk. “But the coffee will at least warm your hands.”

He wraps both around the mug like I suggested, fingers stark against the dark ceramic.

It’s such a small thing, but the sight of his elegant, scarred hands clinging to something as mundane as a mug does something to my chest I don’t have language for.

The silence that follows is… dense.

Not hostile. Not exactly awkward. It’s the silence of two people who have ten thousand things they could say and no clear idea which one won’t send everything shattering. 

I chew.

I focus on the texture of the bread, the heat of the eggs, the bitter edge of the coffee cutting through the greasy film. My body remembers how to deal with food; my mind uses it as a shield.

Across from me, Cyran stares into his mug as if trying to read his future in the dark liquid.

His hair falls around his face, still slightly damp, shadowing his eyes. Without his usual styling and silk, he looks younger. Not young—he hasn’t been that in a very long time—but less… composed. As if some of the centuries have finally slid off and revealed the exhausted soldier underneath. 

I don’t rush him.

If I push, he’ll retreat.

He sips the coffee eventually, mouth twitching at the taste.

“Gods,” he says. “That’s vile.”

“It’s efficient,” I say automatically.

He huffs.

“We’re going to have to have a long conversation someday,” he mutters, “about your definition of that word.”

“Add it to the list,” I say.

Another sip.

Another stretch of quiet.

Outside, the marrow‑rain drums.

Inside, the fire pops softly.

Somewhere in another part of the building, an iron bell clangs the quarter hour, muffled by walls and wet air.

He sets the mug down carefully.

His fingers linger on the handle, knuckles white.

“I kept waiting,” he says.

The words are very soft. I almost miss them under the rain.

“For what?” I ask, just as quietly.

He doesn’t look up.

“For you to want something,” he says. “You never did.”

There it is.

The ledger.

“From you,” he clarifies, as if I could possibly misunderstand. “A price. A favour. A promise. A cut.” His mouth twists. “That’s how it works. Has always worked. People don’t… do things like that—” a tiny gesture that somehow encompasses last night, this morning, everything between—“without an invoice attached.” 

His voice isn’t bitter.

It’s bewildered.

I set my fork down.

The clink of metal on ceramic sounds very loud.

“I did want something,” I say.

His head snaps up.

His eyes search my face with naked alarm.

“Ah,” he says, voice turning brittle to protect the soft underbelly. “There it is. Shall I fetch my cheque book?” His smile is a poor, painful imitation of his usual smirk. “Or do you accept blood, souls, firstborns—”

“I wanted this,” I cut in, before he can make himself bleed further with jokes. “I still do.”

He blinks.

“‘This’ being…?”

The question is genuine.

Not cynical. Not mocking.

His world truly has no category for desire that isn’t inherently transactional.

“You,” I say simply. “In my bed. At my table. In my investigation. In my life. That is not a debt, Cyran. It’s a choice. On both sides.”

He swallows.

Hard.

Some of the color drains from his face, then rushes back.

“You expect me to believe,” he says slowly, “that you get nothing out of this. That this is—for you—pure charity?” There’s real anger under the question now, thin and sharp. “Do not start patronizing me, Lysa. I can stomach your pity less than I could stomach their lust.” 

“Don’t be dense,” I say, sharper than I intended.

His eyes narrow.

Good.

Anger is easier to work with than despair.

“I get a great deal out of this,” I continue. “Pleasure. Companionship. Someone competent to watch my back when Voss tries to stab it. You are not a charity case. You are a partner.” 

The word seems to startle him more than if I’d reached across the table and slapped him.

“A partner,” he repeats.

“Yes.” I pick up my mug, letting the heat bite into my palms. “Equal risk. Equal stake. Equal say. In bed and out of it.”

His laugh is short. Bitter.

“Equal?” he echoes. “You hold the quill that can end me. Parchment is sharper than any blade they ever put in my hand.”

“You think I don’t know that?” I ask.

Silence.

“I am painfully aware,” I say more quietly, “of exactly how much power the Tribunal has given me over you. I am also painfully aware that the only moral use of that power is to dismantle it. Which is what we are doing.” I tip my chin toward the pile of ledgers we left last night. “Clause by clause. Sigil by sigil. That work doesn’t stop just because the sun came up.”

He stares at the papers.

Then at me.

Something in his shoulders loosens, just a fraction.

“And last night?” he asks. “In that framework?”

“Last night,” I say, “we stepped outside their framework entirely. That terrified you. It terrified me. We did it anyway. That is not a transaction, Cyran. That is an act of rebellion.” 

His lips flicker.

“Trust you to make sex sound like a legal insurrection,” he murmurs.

“If the shoe fits,” I say.

A breath of a laugh escapes him.

Then another.

The sound is small and incredulous and so unlike his polished drawing room chuckle that it pulls an answering warmth through my ribs.

He looks down at his hands.

The last of the violent tension leaches out of his knuckles.

“Not a debt,” he says, more to himself than to me.

“No,” I say.

“Then…” He hesitates. “What happens now?”

I glance at the window.

The light outside is the particular flat, gray pallor of a Spine morning. Somewhere above us, the Sanctum is stirring. Clerks waking, bells chiming, Contract Lords rolling out of silk to plot their next acquisition.

“Now,” I say, “you finish your coffee. I finish my eggs. We wash these dishes. Then we go back into the marrow and find proof that your former High Priest is alive and colluding with Thessen before he kills someone else and hands you back to him on a gilded leash.”

He grimaces.

“I liked it better when ‘breakfast’ was the scariest part of that sentence,” he says.

I reach across the table.

Not far. Just enough that my fingers can brush the back of his hand.

He tenses.

Then, slowly, turns his hand over under mine, letting our palms meet.

“We will not let them,” I say. “You and I. Together.”

His throat works.

“You are very sure,” he says.

“I have to be,” I reply. “One of us does.”

Another beat.

Then he exhales, a long, shaky thing that seems to carry the last scraps of last night’s panic with it.

He looks at my plate.

At the half‑eaten piece of toast soaked in yolk.

Deliberately, with the air of a man performing a ritual he doesn’t entirely understand but is willing to commit to, he reaches out with his free hand, picks up a corner of the toast, and takes a bite. 

It is a tiny, ridiculous gesture.

It knocks something loose in my chest.

Shared bread.

For all the Tribunal’s posturing, there are older laws than theirs.

Older contracts than ink on bone‑paper.

He chews, face contorting at the texture.

“This is terrible,” he says.

“It’s efficient,” I repeat.

He snorts, half a mouthful of burned bread threatening to escape.

We both laugh.

The sound rings oddly bright in the little room, cutting through the gray light, the cold, the damp.

Outside, the city sharpens its teeth.

Inside, we sit at my scarred table, bare feet brushing under the wood, hands tangled over cold ceramic and cooling plates, and, for one stolen, fragile morning, build a new normal out of coffee, eggs, and the dangerous, intoxicating possibility that he is allowed to stay.
