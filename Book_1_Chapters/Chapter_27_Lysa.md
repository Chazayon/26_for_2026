# Chapter 27 — *The Temple Falls*
### Lysa | POV

The god is angry, or maybe just indigestion.

Either way, the floor is pitching like a ship.

The Heart‑Chamber lurches under my feet, bone tiles buckling as a fresh crack splits the altar steps where Thessen’s body lies. Black blood seeps out from under him in irregular pulses, oozing into the fractures like tar seeking the weakest points.

The Heart beats.

Once.

Twice.

Too hard.

The sound reverberates through the ribs of the dead god, up the walls, down into my teeth. Lamps swing, throwing huge, jerking shadows of frozen Guardians across the dome. Dust sifts from the ceiling in a fine, choking veil.

“Cyran,” I say.

He’s still standing, somehow.

Burned, blood‑spattered, breathing like he’s just sprinted the length of the Spine. The obsidian dagger drips black from his grip. His shirt is scorched through over one shoulder, skin beneath it raw and blistered. There’s a smear of someone else’s blood—Thessen’s—across his cheek.

His eyes are on the body at his feet.

He looks… empty.

“Hey.” I step closer, reaching for his unburned arm. “We need to go.”

He blinks.

Looks at me.

The bond between us hums with a dull, exhausted shock.

Then a chunk of ceiling the size of my desk breaks loose from the dome, slams into the floor just shy of the nave. The impact sends a shockwave through the chamber, knocking several low‑ranking priests off their feet. Donors in the balconies scream. Again.

The Guardians that hadn’t already shattered are perfectly still.

Frozen mid‑swing, mid‑step, mid‑threat.

Whatever animating magic Thessen was feeding them died with him.

Small mercy.

“Ah,” Cyran says hoarsely. “Yes. Structural failure. My favorite post‑ritual epilogue.”

He sways.

I catch his forearm.

He’s heavier than I remember, or maybe my muscles have decided to file formal complaints now that the immediate attempt on his life is over.

Varos moves.

I’d almost forgotten he was there.

He hauls himself up from where he crouched behind the altar when the god‑sword came out—a surprisingly undignified position for a High Priest. Dust coats his pristine robes; one sleeve is torn. He brushes bone grit off his shoulder with fastidious little pats, eyes fixed on Thessen’s corpse.

“He meant to kill me,” Varos says.

His voice is a low, stunned thing.

He takes a step closer to the body, careful of the black puddle spreading across the stone. His gaze traces the line of the dagger wound, the pool of corrupt blood, the fractured halo of cracks radiating out.

“Correction,” I say, because precision is how I breathe. “He meant to kill *everyone*.”

Another rumble shudders through the chamber.

The main doors at the far end of the nave are closed, barred from the inside. Someone thought that was a good way to keep the chaos contained. Now, fists and spear‑butts pound against them from the other side, a frantic staccato under the deeper groan of shifting bone.

“High Priest!” a voice shouts muffled through the doors. “Open— the dome is—”

The rest is swallowed by the sound of something heavy giving way up in the ribs.

Cracks blaze across the dome like lightning, each one edged with faint, sick red where Thessen’s blood has seeped into the god‑bone network. Tiny shards rain down, pricking exposed skin.

The Heart beats again.

Slower.

Strained.

This chamber is the temple’s heart‑node, and we just stabbed its favorite enforcer with its own knife.

Consequences were inevitable.

“We can’t stay,” I say, unnecessarily. “Unless you’d like to find out what the procedural penalties are for being buried alive in a compromised ritual site.”

Varos drags his gaze away from Thessen.

For the first time, he really looks at us.

Cyran—burned, bloody, barely upright, still subconsciously standing between Varos and the worst of the falling debris.

Me—filthy from sewers and soot, Tribunal badge hanging crooked on my wrist, one hand locked around Cyran’s arm like I will let the ceiling hit me before I let him fall.

The expression that crosses Varos’s face is… complicated.

He lifts his chin.

“Behind there,” he says abruptly, nodding at the altar. “The Reliquary Passage.”

I stare at him.

“Why,” I say carefully, “is there a reliquary passage behind the execution altar?”

“In case of… emergencies,” he replies.

The corner of my mouth twitches despite myself.

“Of course,” I say. “The gods provide for fire exits.”

Cyran makes a strangled sound that might be a laugh, might be pain.

“Save your wit for later,” Varos snaps, composure snapping back into place like a mask. “We have minutes. Perhaps less.”

“Why help us?” Cyran asks.

His voice is raw but clear.

Varos looks at him.

At the empty space where a High Contract Lord used to stand. At the black blood. At the shattered Guardians. At the Spectacle that refused to finish the script.

“You dropped the knife,” Varos says simply. “Thessen wouldn’t have.”

A fresh shower of dust hisses down from the dome.

One of the balcony railings cracks; a donor clings to it, sobbing, before being pulled back by someone with more sense.

We do not have time to litigate Varos’s sudden moral clarity.

“Show me,” I say.

He moves with brisk, economical strides to the altar, skirts around Thessen’s body without stepping in the black ooze. Up close, the altar’s back side is less ornate—smooth bone, faint seams. Varos slides his hand along its edge until his fingers catch on a nearly invisible groove.

He presses.

Stone grinds against stone.

The entire rear slab of the altar shifts, pivoting on hidden hinges with an ugly scrape, revealing a narrow opening just wide enough for a person to squeeze through sideways.

Cold air seeps out, carrying the smell of old stone, stale incense, and something metallic.

“Reliquary Passage,” Varos says. “It leads to the lower catacombs. From there, there is an exit into the Necropolis. Go.”

“And you?” I ask.

The responsible part of me can’t help it.

If I’m writing what happened, I have to include the priest who chose to hold the line.

“I will hold the guards,” he says.

Another crack rips across the dome, this one accompanied by a chunk of bone rib dropping to smash into one of the Guardian statues. The impact sends a shockwave through the floor that nearly knocks me off my feet.

“That seems… optimistic,” I say.

He lifts his pendant.

For the first time since I’ve known him, the gesture doesn’t read as threat.

It looks like faith.

“The Prime Contract will protect its heart,” Varos says. “It always has. Today, I choose who stands closest when it does.”

Our eyes meet.

For years, he’s been the face of the institution I fight—cold pragmatism wrapped in liturgy, contract law used as a blade. He’s proposed using Cyran as a donor appeasement toy with the same tone he might use to order ink.

Now, he’s giving us an exit.

Not redemption.

Not yet.

But a crack.

“Don’t waste it,” he adds.

“Not planning to,” I say.

Another chunk of ceiling slams down not ten feet away, showering us with shards.

“Go,” Varos snaps, voice cutting through the chaos like a gavel. “That is an order, Auditor.”

I don’t take orders from priests.

But I do recognize a sensible retreat when I see one.

“Come on,” I say, tightening my grip on Cyran’s arm.

He resists for half a heartbeat.

Then his weight leans into me.

We half‑stumble, half‑run to the altar’s back.

The opening yawns dark and narrow, barely big enough for his broad shoulders.

“After you,” he says, managing a ragged approximation of a bow.

“Not a chance,” I say. “If you collapse halfway, I’m not dragging you by your ankles.”

He gives me a look that is equal parts fond and offended.

“You think you could?” he asks.

“Yes,” I say. “But I’d resent it.”

The floor tilts again.

No more arguing.

I duck sideways and slide into the gap, stone scraping my shoulders, altar edge catching briefly at my hip. The passage beyond is a tight, roughly hewn corridor descending at a steep angle. Dust coats the floor thickly; my boots slip a little as I find my footing.

Behind me, Cyran squeezes in.

“Mind your shoulder,” I say.

“Which one?” he mutters.

The scraped‑stone sound of the altar grinding shut behind us cuts off the worst of the chamber noise. The pounding on the main doors becomes a distant, accusing heartbeat. The rumble of the collapsing temple is now a muffled growl through the stone.

Darkness presses in.

A faint, sourceless glow seeps from the bone itself—old god‑residue, enough to sketch the outline of the tunnel but not much more.

“Lovely,” Cyran says under his breath. “You take me to the nicest places.”

“Forward,” I say.

We move.

The corridor is narrow enough that our shoulders brush bone on either side. Overhead, the ceiling is low; Cyran has to duck a little, his burned shoulder occasionally knocking against protrusions. Every time he hisses, guilt twinges in my own muscles.

“How’s your head?” I ask.

“Full of terrible decisions,” he says lightly.

His breath is already rough.

He used his body like a battering ram against Thessen. Took blasts meant to kill, then pushed through Key backlash and divine shock. Now the fine tremor in his fingers has spread up his arms, into the set of his jaw.

“Specifically,” I say. “Any dizziness? Blurred vision? Nausea?”

“Is that auditor concern,” he pants, “or lover worry?”

“Yes,” I say.

A small, breathless laugh.

He stumbles.

His weight slams into my back, knocking me forward a step. I brace against the wall, shoulder nudging bone. Dust cascades down in a choking puff.

“Cyran,” I say sharply. “Stay with me.”

“I am here,” he wheezes, forehead briefly touching the back of my neck. His skin is hot and clammy. “Apologies. The god apparently objects to internal renovation.”

“Join the club,” I mutter.

I reach back without thinking, catch his hand.

His fingers close around mine like a reflex.

We move like that for a while.

Me in front, one hand skimming the wall, the other locked with his. Him behind, steps occasionally faltering but always catching up when I tug lightly. The tunnel slopes deeper, curves left, right, left again.

Once, the entire passage shudders.

A crack splits the ceiling ahead of us, dust billowing down, small chips raining. I throw my free arm up over my head, drag Cyran close behind me, both of us pressing into the wall as if that will save us if the whole place decides to come down.

It doesn’t.

Not yet.

“We are never,” I say when the shaking subsides, “doing temple infiltration during lunar alignment again.”

“Agreed,” he says. “Next time, I vote for a nice, quiet corruption case involving… dock tariffs. Perhaps a smuggling ring. Something with ledgers and minimal divine architecture.”

“Don’t tempt me,” I say.

His hand squeezes mine.

There’s a tremor in it.

I squeeze back.

The air in the passage grows thicker as we go—dust and lingering incense, the faint, coppery tang of old sacrifices archived in bone. My lungs burn. Sweat slides down my spine. Somewhere above, the Heart beats a strained, unsteady rhythm.

We turn another corner.

Finally, something changes.

Ahead, the tunnel widens slightly and ends in a heavy bone grate set into the wall. Beyond it, through uneven gaps, I see darkness of a different sort—open space, threaded with faint starlight. The smell changes too.

Stone dust.

Damp earth.

Night air.

“Exit,” I say.

“Or another creative execution chamber,” Cyran offers.

“Optimism,” I remind him. “We discussed this.”

I release his hand long enough to plant both palms flat against the grate.

It’s old.

The bars are thick, carved from fused god‑bone, edges smoothed by time and the passage of priests who believed they owned this place. There’s no visible lock—just corrosion and disuse.

I set my feet.

Push.

At first, nothing.

Then, slowly, grudgingly, the grate shifts.

Stone grinds, loud in the confined space. Pain flares in my shoulders. I grit my teeth and push harder, muscles protesting.

“Here,” Cyran says behind me.

His hands come up around mine, larger, stronger even in his exhausted state. Together, we shove. The grate gives an inch, then another. Dust cascades in little avalanches from the edges. A crack of open air widens, cold and sharp against my face.

One more heave.

The grate swings out just enough to allow a person through, scraping along the stone with a teeth‑itching screech.

I gasp in a lungful of outside.

It tastes like damp stone, old bones, and freedom.

“After you,” I say again.

This time, he doesn’t argue.

He ducks, shoulders bunching, and squeezes through the gap, emerging on the other side with a graceless little stumble. The Necropolis rises around him—rows of bone markers and carved tomb‑arches stacked along the inner curve of the Ribcage, pale in the star‑washed dark.

I follow, catching my robe hem on a jagged edge, wrenching it free with more irritation than is strictly warranted.

The night air hits my sweat‑damp skin, turning it clammy in an instant.

It’s blessed.

I suck in another breath.

Behind us, the grate hangs half‑open, a secret no longer wholly owned by the Temple.

Cyran sways on his feet.

Even in the dim light, I can see he’s at the end of whatever reserves he had left. The bravado that kept him upright in the Heart‑Chamber has drained away with the black blood on his clothes.

“Sit,” I say.

It comes out more command than request.

He obeys without a quip.

That more than anything scares me.

We’re in one of the lower graveyards, near the base of the god’s ribs. The Necropolis here is a maze of tombstones and ossuaries, some simple bone slabs, others elaborate shrines carved into vertebrae. I steer him toward the nearest substantial bit of stone—a heavy sarcophagus half‑embedded in the ground, its lid etched with the worn name of someone who died three systems ago.

He collapses against it with a hiss as his burned shoulder meets cool bone.

“Ah,” he says between his teeth. “Perfect. I always wanted to spend a romantic evening pressed against someone else’s remains.”

“You get what you pay for,” I mutter.

I ease myself down beside him.

For a long moment, we just breathe.

My lungs drag in night, out dust.

The temple looms above the graveyard, a silhouette against the stars—ribs arching, dome bulging like a second, unnatural moon. Even from here, I can see fresh cracks spider‑webbing along its surface, faint red lines where Thessen’s death bled into the architecture. The Guardians that crown the outer walls are motionless, just statues again.

The Heart’s pulse is duller.

Quieter.

Like someone put a pillow over its mouth.

From this angle, the main entrance is hidden, but I can imagine the scene—Guards pounding, donors fleeing in disarray, Varos standing under that cracking dome and holding his ground like a man paying off a very old debt.

“Is it over?” I ask, mostly to myself.

It comes out louder than intended.

“Which part?” Cyran says.

His head tips back against the sarcophagus, eyes half‑lidded, throat exposed. His white hair is dust‑streaked, his once‑pristine robe ripped and stained, but he’s breathing. His heart—whatever it is, however it works—beats steady under the skin where my hand rests on his chest.

“The part,” I say slowly, “where you die on an altar for a god who never deserved you.”

Silence stretches.

The wind moves through the grave markers, making a low, hollow sound like distant chanting.

He huffs out a tired breath.

“Not today,” he says. “Though I’m sure the Temple will file a strongly worded complaint.”

A knot of tension between my shoulder blades eases.

Not much.

Enough.

I look back up at the temple.

This building has dominated my horizon since I arrived in the Ribcage—the literal and metaphorical Heart of the system I serve. For months, I’ve walked its corridors in sanctioned visits, watched its doors swallow people and spit out signed contracts, numbers on ledgers, the occasional corpse.

Now I’ve seen inside.

Seen its collapse.

And a priest—*that* priest—helped me escape it.

Not because he saw the light.

Because he saw the cracks.

“Not all priests,” I say under my breath.

Cyran cracks an eye open.

“Careful,” he murmurs. “That sounds dangerously like nuance.”

“I know,” I say. “It’s unsettling me as well.”

He smiles.

It’s small.

Real.

The kind that tugs higher on one side where the scars pull.

We sit there against someone else’s grave, side by side, listening to the temple creak.

The Necropolis around us is quiet.

Most of its inhabitants have long since finished their business with the living.

Somewhere in the distance, a Vigil patrol’s lantern swings, light bobbing between tombs. They haven’t noticed us yet. We’re just two more shadows among many.

“You dropped the knife,” I say.

He makes a soft, questioning noise.

“Varos,” I clarify. “That’s why he helped us. Because you dropped the knife.”

“Seemed the thing to do at the time,” Cyran says dryly.

His humor is a reflex.

I don’t let it deflect me.

“You broke their script,” I say. “You broke *his* script. He saw that. It matters.”

“It matters that I hesitated when ordered to butcher an old monster,” he says, staring up at the stars. “We set the bar scandalously low these days.”

“Don’t,” I say quietly.

He turns his head, looks at me.

Dust has settled in his lashes, making him look even paler.

“Don’t what?” he asks.

“Make yourself small to make their sins manageable,” I say. “You were a person. They made you a thing. You dropped the knife. He didn’t. That distinction is the difference between us being buried under that dome and sitting here making inappropriate jokes about graveyards.”

He studies my face like it’s another piece of evidence.

After a moment, he nods once.

“All right,” he says. “I’ll allow that.”

“Good,” I say.

I let my head tip back against the sarcophagus.

Bone is cold through my sweat‑damp hair.

The stars beyond the ribs are pinpricks of harsh white, distant and indifferent. For the first time since this began, I feel the space between me and the Temple as something more than metaphor.

We are out.

Not safe.

Not resolved.

But out.

“Now what?” Cyran asks.

Not flippant.

Not teasing.

Just… tired.

The question I’ve been avoiding.

“Now,” I say slowly, “we document. We stabilize you, we secure whatever evidence we can salvage, and we make sure Varos’s sudden fit of conscience goes on record before the Council can spin this into a tragic accident caused by a volatile Relic.”

“Mm,” he says. “So: bed rest, political warfare, and endless paperwork.”

“You forgot medical treatment,” I say. “That shoulder needs a healer before you start smelling like burned pork.”

He wrinkles his nose.

“Charming,” he mutters. “Truly. Your bedside manner astounds.”

“I’m not a healer,” I say. “I’m an Auditor. I specialize in other kinds of surgery.”

He looks down at my hand on his chest.

Covers it with his own.

For a long moment, we just exist like that—contact, breath, the sound of a collapsing god‑house in the distance.

“Lysa,” he says quietly.

“Yes.”

“This isn’t over.”

I know what he means.

The Temple still stands.

The Prime Contract still hums, wounded but not broken, in the bones under our feet.

The Tribunal still signs off on Redemption ledgers built on exploitation.

The people who set men like Varos and Thessen in power are still very much alive.

“No,” I say. “It’s not.”

He turns his hand, laces our fingers together.

“Good,” he says, eyes closing. “I’m not finished either.”

The exhaustion in his face undercuts the bravado.

He’s shaking.

Shock, adrenaline crash, Key backlash—all of it is catching him now that we’re not actively being ordered to die.

“Stay awake,” I say, squeezing his hand. “Just for a little longer.”

“Bossy,” he murmurs.

“Effective,” I reply.

In the distance, the temple dome shifts again.

It doesn’t fall.

Not tonight.

But the cracks are there.

I intend to widen them.
