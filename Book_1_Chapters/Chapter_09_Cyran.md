# Chapter 9 — *Blood Given, Not Taken*
### Cyran | POV

Pain is a familiar room. I know where all the furniture is.

The door into the ossuary slams my shoulder instead of opening the first time. That’s how I know I’m worse off than I thought.

Normally, I’m grace. Fluid, theatrical violence and silk over bone. Tonight I am a drunk with a hole in his side, shoulder-checking dead gods architecture like it insulted my mother.

“Move,” Lysa hisses behind me.

Her palm hits the wood above my hand, fingers finding the right vertebra-knot in the door by memory. The old ward-sigils sputter, then release with a reluctant click. The slab of bone swings inward on protesting hinges, coughing dust.

I stumble over the threshold.

The safe house is an abandoned ossuary carved into the base of a rib—just another forgotten chamber in a city that’s full of them. Bone walls, bone shelves, bone floor, everything the same exhausted pale. The shelves are mostly empty now; the Vigil cleared the worst of the leftover remains years ago. A few unclaimed skulls linger in the alcoves, their hollow eyes catching the lamplight that bleeds in around the edges of the boarded-up slit-windows.

I make it three steps before my knees decide they’ve had enough of supporting the world’s most expensive corpse.

The wall gets there first.

Bone kisses my back hard. I slide down until I’m sitting on chilled stone, hand clamped to my side. Wet warmth pulses through my fingers, sluggish but steady. The copper tang of my own blood floods the air, thick enough that even mortals would taste it.

“Inconvenient,” I mutter, mostly to the skull leering down at me from its niche. “I’m leaking. How unseemly.”

Behind me, the door booms shut. Lysa throws the internal bar, a heavy length of carved femur that slams into its cradle with a satisfying finality.

“They’re gone,” she says, breath sharp. “No footsteps. No echoes. We’re safe.”

“How comforting,” I say. “The murderous horror-beasts are outside instead of inside. I feel much better.”

I let my head thump back against the wall. The room tilts. The ossuary smell—old dust, old bone, a faint lingering sweetness from marrow long scraped out—wraps around the richer scent of my own blood.

She turns.

“Cyran—”

Ah. There it is. The change in her voice. Neutral professional dropping out, sharp fear sliding in. She crosses the room faster than I’ve seen her move without a pen in her hand, robes whispering around her boots.

“Let me see,” she says, already reaching.

“Don’t.” I try to push her hand away.

Try being the operative word.

My palm smears more blood across my own leathers than her skin. The muscle behind the gesture is gone, eaten up by the wound and everything that came before it tonight. The god-spawn’s claws. The chase through the marrow-fog. The bone-storm still hissing along the tunnels outside, blocking every sensible route back to the Spine.

“Don’t touch,” I say again, and this time the word comes out ragged. Not because of the pain.

Because the Thirst is waking up, and I would very much prefer her hands not be anywhere near my mouth when it fully opens its eyes.

It’s been humming along contentedly for days, purring over the minor sips the donors provide. Enough to keep my skin luminous and my claws sharp, not enough to push me into the kind of manic euphoria that makes Commanders nervous. Tonight, though… Tonight the donors didn’t meet us at the usual post-patrol stop. The little Temple liaison priest was conspicuously absent from his corner by the ward-station, his ledger of names and signed permissions closed.

Thessen’s doing, I’d bet a few Marks I don’t have. A tightened leash. Or maybe just a test, to see how long it takes his prize hound to chew through its own leg.

Then the ambush. Filthy, half-born things dropping out of the rib-shadows, carapaces still slick with god-blood, claws too many and too sharp. I’d killed three before the fourth got past my guard, raking up under my ribs with a lucky, vicious swipe.

Normally, that would be an inconvenience. A sting. Something to hiss about while my body knitted itself back together on the fly.

Normally, I’m not this empty.

Hunger licks up my throat now in long, slow strokes, tasting the air. My vision tightens; the edges of the room blur while the details close to me sharpen. Lysa’s throat, for instance. The pale column of it above her collar, pulsing faintly with each quick beat of her heart.

My fangs ache.

“Cyran,” she says again, more sharply. “Move your hand.”

“You’re very bossy for someone who’s supposed to be impartial,” I say, because if I stop making jokes I might start making mistakes.

Her eyes narrow.

“Move. Your hand.”

The tone does things to me that have nothing to do with the Thirst and everything to do with the fact that this woman walked into my quarters last night while I was wearing nothing but this shirt and desperation and told me no.

You made it clear, she’d said. I don’t want to buy you.

I move my hand.

It hurts. A lot.

She sucks in a breath when the wound comes properly into view. The shredding at my side is ugly—ragged, deep, a whole handful of flesh laid open. The blood soaking my leathers is dark, almost black in this light, oozing rather than spurting now. That should be a good sign. It isn’t.

There are places inside me that feel… wrong. Hollow where they should be humming, cold where they should be prickling with god-fire. The Marks etched along my ribs are dull, their usual faint red glow gone to ash.

“Why isn’t it closing?” she demands.

“If you have a very sharp object,” I manage, “and you stab a wineskin that’s already empty…”

“Cyran.”

“I’m trying for metaphor,” I say. “Apparently I’ve run out of wine as well as blood.”

She goes a shade paler, which I do not care for. She’s supposed to be the steady one. I’m the one who panics prettily, not her.

“You need blood,” she says.

“Perceptive,” I say. “Do write that down in your report.”

“I’ll fetch a donor.”

She’s already halfway to the door when I catch her ankle with a clumsy, blood-slick hand.

“Lysa, no.”

She freezes, looks back. Her spectacles are smeared with dust and god-knows-what from the fight, lenses catching the lamplight in opaque flashes.

“Why?”

“Because the charming little welcoming committee we just escaped is still out there,” I say. “And they weren’t improvising. That was an ambush. This ossuary is the only space in three streets with wards that still half-work. You step out that door alone, they’ll peel you like fruit.”

“I can move quietly.”

“I can smell you from here,” I say, and the honesty in it startles us both. “They can too. Blood, ink, fear. You’re… distracting.”

Her throat works.

“This is not about me,” she says. “You are dying.”

“I’ve done that before,” I say lightly. “I’m very good at it.”

Her gaze drops, traces the gape in my side, the blood pooling at my hip, the faint, uneven rise and fall of my chest. Her jaw tightens.

“Can you heal without blood?” she asks. “At all?”

The Thirst laughs, low and delighted, at the back of my mind.

“No reserves,” I say. “They’ve been keeping me very… efficient. In and out. No waste. Factory settings. I can slow it, but—”

I try to call up the regenerative fire, the thing that usually lives under my skin like a coiled serpent of divine voltage. It flickers, feeble, catches along the edges of the wound and then gutters out. The torn flesh steams for a heartbeat, then slumps back into its own ruin.

“No fuel,” I finish.

She looks between my side and the sealed door, weighing probabilities the way she weighs regulations. The bone-storm outside gives a distant, irritated howl, rattling dust from the ceiling. Somewhere in the walls, old ward-sigils hiss, strained.

Then she makes a choice.

Her hands go to the clasp of her robe.

For one absurd moment, I think she’s about to disrobe and I’m not certain I have the mental fortitude to die politely under those circumstances. But she only yanks the top of the robe aside, baring the pale line of her throat more fully.

There’s a faint, dark circle at her wrist—the Tribunal mark—visible where the sleeve rides up. The pulse there jumps.

She crosses back to me, kneels in the dust.

“Drink,” she says.

Every part of me locks.

“No,” I say.

“I’m offering—”

“I heard you,” I snap, sharper than I mean to. The sound of my own voice startles me. It’s too close to the boy they carved on the temple altar, teeth chattering, saying no in all the wrong ways.

I shake my head, swallow against the hunger that is now roaring, delighted, at the smell of her. Sage from her hair, ink on her fingers, sweat and adrenaline and under it all the clean, bright iron of living blood.

“You made it very clear,” I say, trying for flippant and landing somewhere nearer to raw. “You’re not a merchant. You don’t buy favors. Congratulations again on your moral fortitude. Go away.”

“This isn’t trade,” she says. Each word lands like a tiny hammer. “This is medicine. This is choice.”

I huff something that might be a laugh, might be a cough. Blood bubbles in my throat; I swallow it down.

“You keep using that word,” I say. “Choice. As if it’s applicable. You choose to give. I choose to take. The Thirst chooses to drown us both. Not exactly a level field.”

She shifts closer.

The movement brings her right into my space, knees bracketing my thigh where it’s sprawled on the floor. Her robe brushes my hip. I can feel the heat of her through two layers of fabric and a rapidly failing leather coat.

“Cyran.” My name again, in that low, precise way that means I am running out of room to be clever. “Please.”

The word hits me like a slap.

No one says please to weapons. They say use, they say now, they say more. They say pretty, they say good boy, they say kneel.

Please is… bewildering.

“I might kill you,” I whisper.

There’s no joke left to hang it on. The Thirst is pressing up against the inside of my teeth now, panting. It knows exactly how much better she will taste than any dosed, drunk temple donor, and it wants. It wants in huge, heedless swallows.

“I don’t—” My fingers curl against the stone. “I don’t know that I can stop.”

Her eyes are huge behind the smear on her lenses. She reaches up with one hand, takes them off, sets them carefully on a nearby skull as if she’ll need them later.

I fixate, stupidly, on that small act of faith.

“I trust you,” she says.

Three words. No adornment. No manipulation. She’s not saying it for my benefit; she’s saying it because it’s true, and she thinks I need to know that before I tear her open.

Something in my chest, already split by claws, tears wider.

“You shouldn’t,” I say.

“Too late,” she replies softly. “If you want my blood, you ask. If I say no, you stop. That’s the rule.”

“That’s—” I choke on a laugh. “That’s the opposite of every rule I have ever been taught about this.”

“Then learn new ones,” she says. “I’m not them.”

The Thirst snarls. At her. At me. At everyone who ever fed it wrong.

She tilts her head to the side, baring her throat. The movement is small, but it feels enormous. A priest once did that to me, smirking, tapping two fingers against his own pulse point to show me where he wanted my mouth.

Lysa is not smirking.

Her pulse flutters under thin skin. I can see it from here. Hear it all through me, each beat echoing in the hollows where my god-fire should be.

“If you lose control,” she says, “I will say stop. And you will stop.”

“And if I don’t?” I ask, because somewhere inside me the boy on the altar is still waiting for the catch, the trick clause in the contract.

“Then I misjudged you,” she says, simply. “And I die having tried to save someone who deserved saving. I can live with that.”

“That’s—”

“Not up to you,” she cuts in. “This is my choice. Not yours. Not theirs. Mine.”

The Thirst throbs at that, strangely moved. Choice. The idea of blood not demanded, not tithed, not tallied in temple ledgers. Given.

I close my eyes.

For a moment, I just breathe her in. Ink, dust, fear. Determination. The stubborn little iron streak that runs right down her spine when she decides something is right and will be done, consequences be damned.

My hand finds her waist.

Her breath hitches—not in fear, not entirely in fear—and some dark, selfish part of me thrills at that. I pull her closer, not gently. If we’re doing this, we’re not pretending it’s anything but what it is.

Her other hand comes up to my shoulder, steadying herself, thumb catching on the edge of my collarbone where the shirt has stuck to my skin with blood.

“No payment,” she says. “No ledger. Just this.”

“Just this,” I echo.

I turn my head.

Her skin is warm against my lips. The first touch is a kiss, not a bite, though I’ll deny that to my dying day. A soft press at the corner of her jaw, where the pulse is strongest, my breath cool against her. She shivers.

The Thirst howls.

I open my mouth.

Her skin gives under my teeth with a tiny, clean snap. The sound goes through me like a shock. There is a heartbeat’s worth of resistance and then blood.

I have had countless pints of the stuff. In cups, in chalices, slicked over altars. Poured down my throat at ritual banquets. Forced between my lips when I was too drained to swallow on my own. I know blood. It is currency, sacrament, punishment and reward.

I do not know this.

Her blood hits my tongue and the whole world narrows to that point.

It is hot. Shockingly hot. It floods my mouth in a rush, metallic and bright and threaded through with something that tastes like sage and ink and the ozone tang of the Tribunal’s punishment sigils. Underneath, there is fear—sharp and clean—but it is not fear of me. Not exactly. It’s fear of losing me.

The Thirst surges up, ecstatic.

Mine, it screams.

No.

The word is weaker than I’d like, but I hang onto it. I anchor myself in the feel of her hand in my hair—because at some point she’s threaded her fingers there, holding me to her, not pushing me away. In the bend of her body, the way she leans into me instead of flinching.

I drink.

Every swallow is a sin the temple would have either exulted in or punished, I’m not sure which. Heat slams down my throat into my gut, radiating outward in incandescent waves. The dead god’s fire, dormant in my Marks, wakes. The sigils along my ribs ignite, searing-hot lines under my skin.

The wound at my side burns.

I groan against her throat. The sound is half-agony, half obscene relief.

“Cyran,” she gasps.

My name in that voice—rough, breathless—does things to me that have nothing to do with survival.

Her fingers tighten in my hair, nails scraping my scalp. Not pushing. Grounding. Claiming.

I drag her closer, one hand fisting in the back of her robe. She ends up half across my lap, knees scraping bone, the front of her body pressed flush against mine. I feel every line of her—stomach to stomach, chest to chest. Her heartbeat thrums against me like a second heart under my ribs.

The ossuary spins.

Stone under my fingers. Her skin under my mouth. The distant howl of the bone-storm reduced to a meaningless hiss under the roaring in my ears.

The wound at my side is knitting now. I can feel it. Flesh drawing together, steam rising in faint curls where torn tissue fuses. The pain spikes, then drops away so suddenly I gasp against her.

The Thirst goes wild.

More, it demands. More more more—

My jaw tightens.

I slow.

It’s like trying to brake a runaway carriage with my bare hands. Every instinct I have tells me to gorge until the last drop. To empty her, crack her like a marrow-bone and lick her clean inside.

Her blood tastes like no one else’s ever has. There’s no drug-woozy compliance in it, no temple incense dulling the sharp edges. It’s all iron and rage and stubborn hope, and it’s addictive in a way that terrifies me.

“Cyran.” Her voice again, quieter now. The word buzzes against my teeth where they’re still sunk into her throat.

Her free hand finds my face, palm cupping my cheek. Her thumb strokes once under my eye.

“That’s enough,” she whispers.

The Thirst screams.

I stop.

It’s not graceful. It’s not easy. It feels like I’m tearing myself off something vital, like ripping my own mouth off air. My whole body shudders with the effort.

I withdraw my fangs slowly, sealing my lips over the punctures for one last, maddening second to lick the wounds closed. Her skin knits under my tongue, leaving only faint marks that will fade in hours.

I force myself to lean back.

For a moment, I just stare.

Her face is very close. Her cheeks are flushed, eyes dark and wide, pupils blown. Her lips are parted, breath coming in shallow pulls. There’s a smear of my blood along her collar where my hand slid.

She is the most devastating thing I have ever seen.

“Hi,” I say, brilliantly.

It comes out on a shaky exhale. My voice sounds wrong in my own ears—rougher, younger. Less armour on it.

She huffs something that might be a laugh if it weren’t weighed down by adrenaline.

“Hi,” she says.

I realize, abruptly, that I am high.

The room is too bright. The old ossuary wards are singing in colours I don’t have names for. Every nerve in my body is awake and humming, from the tips of my fangs to the soles of my boots. The pain is gone; in its place is an almost obscene sense of wholeness.

I flex the hand that had been clamped to my side. The glove creaks, slick with drying blood, but the skin underneath is whole. I peel my coat back to look.

The wound is gone.

Not even a scar yet. Just smooth, pale flesh, Marks glowing a satisfied, smouldering red under the skin.

“Efficient,” I say faintly. “We should put you on the roster.”

She swats my shoulder. It’s not hard. It still rocks me a little; balance is not my friend right now.

“Don’t make jokes about this,” she says. Her voice wobbles on this.

I catch her wrist before she can pull back, fingers circling the delicate bones there. I’m careful with my grip. My hands feel too strong, too sure now. I could crush marble.

“You stayed,” I say.

“That was the entire point,” she replies, but she’s looking at me like she’s never quite seen me before.

“Most people—” I start, then stop.

Most people would have fainted. Or panicked. Or demanded something in return. Temple donors always did; if not in words, then in the way they looked at me afterward. Like they’d paid for an experience and wanted to make sure they got their money’s worth.

Lysa’s eyes hold none of that.

“You stayed,” I repeat, because I need it anchored somewhere outside my own head, where the blood-high might be fabricating kinder realities.

“I stayed,” she says.

We sit there like that for a long, strange moment. Knees tangled. Her hand in mine, my other still curved, unconsciously, around her waist. Our faces close enough that I could lean forward and…

No.

Not this time.

“This wasn’t payment,” she says quietly, as if reading the direction of my thoughts. “You don’t owe me for it.”

“I disagree,” I say. “Vehemently. But I also know better than to argue consent law with a Tribunal investigator when I am this intoxicated.”

She snorts.

“Cyran.”

“Yes, darling?”

“That,” she says, tapping my chest lightly with one finger, right over the freshly mended flesh, “was blood given, not taken. Try to remember the difference.”

The words land somewhere deep.

Given. Not demanded. Not tithed through a contract sigil. Not coerced as duty or bought as entertainment.

Given, because she chose it. For me.

“I will,” I say.

The Thirst prowls contentedly at the back of my mind, sated but curious. It has never had this, either. It is as bewildered as I am.

“You should lie down,” she says, glancing around the ossuary. “You’re still pale.”

“I am always pale,” I remind her. “It’s part of the aesthetic.”

“Pal-er, then.”

She starts to shift away. Panic spikes—irrational, sharp.

I tighten my hand on hers.

“Stay,” I hear myself say.

She looks back, surprised.

My mask is nowhere to be found. I can feel the absence of it, like a missing tooth my tongue keeps prodding. There is no easy joke lined up, no flirtatious deflection.

“I—” I swallow. “Not for… that. Just… stay.”

She studies my face.

Whatever she sees there must pass her internal ethics test, because after a heartbeat she nods.

“All right,” she says.

She adjusts our positions with practical efficiency, levering me sideways so that I end up half-stretched along the wall, legs out, back supported by old bone. She settles beside me, shoulders touching, our joined hands resting on my thigh. The ossuary is suddenly very quiet.

Outside, the storm mutters. Inside, the dead keep their counsel.

I listen to the sound of her breathing. To the steady, strong rhythm of her heart, a little slower now that the worst is done. To my own, matching it for the first time in… I don’t remember.

Pain is a familiar room. Tonight, for the first time, there’s something new in it.

Trust.
