# Chapter 13 — *The Performance of Blood*
### Cyran | POV

Thessen hands me a sword like he’s handing me a pen. Sign here, in red. 

Technically, it isn’t a sword. That would be gauche. This is a dueling blade—long, slender, glass-steel that catches the light like water and whispers promises along my palm when I curl my fingers around the hilt. Elegant. Precise. Expensive enough that some artisan bled for it before I ever touch it.

The Devotions Hall smells of incense and fear.

Not the temple kind—though that’s here too, cloying resin curling up from braziers in the corners—but the clean, sour stink of adrenaline under perfume. The room is a bone-white amphitheater, circular, with rows of cushioned benches rising in tiers around a central floor. Tonight, that floor is the stage. 

They’ve drawn a circle in the middle of it.

Salt and crushed bone, poured in a careful ring wide enough for two men to dance. The line glows faintly under the magnesium glare of the spotlight hanging above, a single hard beam that turns everything inside it stark and everything outside into suggestion.             

I know this stage.

“You remember the rites, of course,” Thessen murmurs at my shoulder, voice pitched for me and the first two rows only. “Nothing too elaborate tonight. Just a demonstration.”

Of loyalty, he doesn’t say. Of ownership.

He smells of bergamot and smugness, his hand resting on my bare upper arm like I’m a particularly prized piece of sculpture he’s about to unveil. The gold cuff at my wrist hums faintly, resonating with the ward-sigils threaded through the walls. The collar at my throat sits too snug against my pulse, a familiar weight with a new owner. 

Around us, the city’s finest settle into their seats with the soft, expensive susurrus of silk on velvet, of bone jewelry clicking lightly when people lean in to gossip. Laughter ripples and then hushes as Thessen steps forward, raising his glass. 

“Friends,” he calls, and his voice carries cleanly to the rafters. “Patrons. Devout servants of the Prime Contract. You’ve eaten well. You’ve drunk very well.” A chuckle. “You’ve seen the fruits of your investment in the Vigil’s work.”

His hand flicks toward me, a little showman’s gesture. The spotlight sharpens.

“Now,” he says, “I think you deserve… a closer look.”

The crowd hums, pleased.

A thin trickle of cold slides along my spine, under the silk and gold dust.

Thessen turns, eyes on me. In the hard light, his pupils look like pinholes in a field of blue.

“Relic,” he says. “Show them why you are the Vigil’s greatest asset.”

Of course.

I tip the blade in a loose salute, letting it catch the light.

“As my lord wishes,” I purr.

The Ornament is already on his feet, bowing, smiling, lifting his chin just enough that the charms on the collar shift and glint. The Ornament loves this. The Ornament was made for this. The Ornament knows exactly how to stand in a circle and turn slaughter into spectacle.

I step into the ring.

The salt crunches faintly under my boots, mixed with the softer grit of bone. The air inside the circle feels different—thicker, heavier, as if the warding lines remember blood spilled on other nights and are eager for more. 

The crowd’s murmur dims, the way sound does just before a storm hits.

Guards drag the entertainment in from a side door.

He’s younger than I expected. Late twenties, maybe—mortal, gaunt in the way of men who have seen too much of the inside of holding cells and not enough food. His wrists are shackled in front of him, chains clanking as he stumbles. There’s a collar around his neck not unlike mine, but his is iron and welded. 

They’ve drugged him.

It’s obvious in the dilation of his pupils, in the uneven drag of his feet. Whatever they dosed him with makes his movements jerky, uncoordinated. His head lolls once before he jerks it upright, blinking in the harsh light.

He looks terrified.

“Tonight’s… offering,” Thessen announces, “is a City-Killer.” The word rolls off his tongue with relish. “Taken by the Vigil in the lower districts. Four dead, three maimed, no remorse. A perfect example of the filth our Relic protects you from.” 

Little correction there: I kill god-spawn. The Tribunal kills people. I just make it easier.

The crowd makes appropriate sounds.

Tutting. Hissing. A few drawn-out ohs of horrified delight. Lady Morven fans herself rapidly, eyes fixed on the prisoner as if he’s a particularly thrilling playbill illustration come to life. 

The man is no City-Killer.

I’ve met those. They have a particular… texture. A certain deadness in the eyes, a gap where empathy used to live. This one is all panic and animal confusion, chest heaving as his gaze ricochets around the ring.

His eyes land on me.

Stop.

For the first time tonight, the Ornament takes a step back, inward. My own awareness surges up into the space he leaves.

I know how this goes.

The script is simple: Thessen throws a body into the circle, points at me, says kill. I do. The crowd claps. Investors sign a few more lines. Soul futures tick upward another percent. 

Everyone leaves pleased. Except the corpse.

I could do it. I have done it. Temple nights. Rites of devotion. A dozen variations on the same performance.

But I’m not alone on the stage of my life anymore.

Somewhere up in the middle benches, there is a woman in Tribunal blue with her hand around the grip of a concealed baton and a look on her face like she’s memorizing every horror for later prosecution. Somewhere up there, a pair of ink-dark eyes are watching to see if I am still exactly the thing they built. 

I think of her throat under my mouth. Of her hand on my neck, not pushing, not guiding, just there. Of the way she said I trust you and meant that she trusted I would not become what they wanted. 

I step forward, into the center of the circle.

The prisoner flinches.

“Please,” he mumbles. His tongue is sluggish in his mouth, words thick. “I didn’t… I didn’t…”

The crowd leans in to hear his begging. Thessen smiles.

“He claims innocence,” Thessen announces. “As they all do.”

Laughter.

I roll my shoulders, letting the blade describe an idle little arc in front of me, like I have no more pressing concern than making sure the light hits me well.

Inside, my mind is already moving.

Chain. Shackles. Drug. Pulse rate. Weight. Distance to the edge of the circle. Position of the guards. The angle of Thessen’s body, the way his hand rests almost lazily on the back of the nearest bench, close enough to a concealed activation sigil that he could trigger something nasty if he didn’t like the direction this went. 

I’m good at this.

I hate that I’m good at this.

The prisoner whimpers as I begin to circle him.

“Look at me,” I say, voice pitched low.

It sounds like a taunt. The crowd hears cruelty. They want to hear cruelty.

Slowly, his gaze drags up from the floor to my face.

His eyes are blown wide from the drugs, whites showing all around the iris. He’s shaking. Hard. Sweat beads along his hairline, slicks the chains where they bite into his skin. He smells like stale fear and cheap prison soap.

“Listen,” I murmur, letting my lips curl in a predator’s smile so it looks, from out there, like I’m savoring him. “I am going to cut the chain. When I shove you, you swing at me. Understand?”

He blinks.

“I—” He swallows, throat working. “You’re going to—”

“Yes,” I say. “I’m going to kill you terribly. Now nod like you believe it.”

He jerks a shaky nod.

Good boy.

“And when I say three,” I continue, “you fall. Left side. Play dead. If you move after that, these people will do worse to you than I ever could. Do you understand?”

Another nod, quicker. His breath is coming in little panting gasps now, but there’s a flicker of comprehension under the panic.

“Excellent,” I say, loud enough for the front rows to catch.

I spin the blade once and lunge.

The first strike is for the chain.

Glass-steel meets iron with a jarring clang that vibrates up my arm. Sparks snap blue-white in the air as I slide the edge along the link and then twist, leveraging my whole body into the motion.

The chain snaps.

The crowd gasps.

“Fight!” I roar, throwing my head back theatrically. “Come on, then! Show them why they hid you!” 

It’s a performance note. If I play this as execution, it ends too quickly, looks too clinical. Thessen wants spectacle. The investors want fear. So we give them a duel.

The prisoner stumbles back, staring at his freed wrists as if he doesn’t quite believe it.

“Now,” I hiss through my teeth. “Swing at me. Try to hit my head. Mean it.”

He does.

The first swipe is clumsy. He’s not a fighter—some street tough, maybe, or a scapegoat plucked from a line-up—but desperation lends weight to his arm. I dip under it, letting it shear through the space where my skull was a heartbeat ago. 

The crowd oohs.

I give them a show.

We circle. We trade blows. Mostly, I trade them with myself.

Every movement is a calculation: this step here to make him overextend; that parry there to send his arm wide where the front row can see the strain. I nick his sleeve once, just enough to let a thin line of blood trickle down to his wrist. Fear-iron smell sharpens in the air. I let my tongue flick over my teeth, catching the scent.

“Good,” I murmur, closing in as if to hiss a threat. “They like it when you bleed.”

His pulse jackhammers against my awareness.

“On three,” I say, low enough that only he and the Thirst hear it. “Fall left. Do not catch yourself. Slack everything.”

Another sloppy swing. I block it, letting the impact ring in the blade, in my bones.

“One,” I say, pressing him back, letting the tip of the sword kiss his throat just enough to blur a line of red on his skin. The crowd leans forward. 

“Two.” I twist, bringing us around so that my back is briefly to Thessen, to the investors, giving them a lovely view of my shoulders, of the way the muscles jump under gold dust and silk.

“Three,” I whisper.

I thrust.

From the outside, it’s perfect.

The dueling blade slides in under his armpit, just below the line of the collarbone, at an angle that, for most men, would mean the point driving up into the heart. I add a snap of wrist at the end, a flourish that arches his body around the steel.

He screams.

He’s supposed to scream. It sells it.

Inside the circle of his ribs, my point kisses a bundle of nerves and nothing more.

Vampiric precision, they call it, in the old temple manuals. The ability to find and sever connections with inhuman accuracy. I’ve used it to kill more times than I care to count.     

Tonight, I use it to undo.

Lightning jumps under my blade, invisible but real. His whole body seizes, jerks once, twice, then collapses bonelessly away from the steel.

He hits the ground hard.

Left side. Exactly as instructed. Limbs slack. Chest rising in a shallow, barely-there rhythm that would be easy to miss from any distance.

The crowd holds its breath.

I stand over him, panting.

Some of the breathlessness is performance; some is the sharp edge of adrenaline slicing through the sugar and wine from earlier, the awareness that one wrong angle, one misjudged millimeter, and I could have carved him open for real.

Blood—his—is smeared along the point of the blade. Not much. Enough. It trembles, just a little, with my hand.

Silence stretches.

Then someone begins to clap.

It starts in the back—some bored lord who likes to be first in all things—then spreads, rolling forward in waves until it crashes against the front rows and rebounds off the dome. Applause. Genuine, enthusiastic, thick with relief and arousal and the little sparks of cruelty that light up these people’s eyes when they see something die safely behind glass. 

I bow.

Deep and theatrical, sword held out to one side, blood-tip down. The salt grinds under my boots as I bend. Drops fall, darkening the bone-white floor.

Thessen descends from his little throne.

He moves slowly, deliberately, savoring the attention. When he reaches the edge of the circle, he doesn’t hesitate; he steps over the line like it isn’t there, entering the ring with the smug confidence of a man convinced any magic here is his.

He comes to stand beside me, looking down at the body.

“Efficient,” he says.

He tuts.

“But messy.” 

He kicks the man in the ribs.

Hard.

The impact rolls the prisoner’s body a fraction; his head lolls. He doesn’t react. The nerve strike has him locked down completely, muscles refusing to obey even if his panic is still screaming behind his wide, unblinking eyes. 

I feel it, faintly, in the electric net of awareness I cast when I struck him: his terror, trapped in his own skin, praying that my gamble works, that these rich monsters won’t decide to keep playing with him after the show.

“He was drunk on his own blood,” I say lightly. “I did you the courtesy of making sure he can’t get up again.”

Thessen smiles.

“You wanted a show,” I add, wiping the blade slowly along a pristine patch of my own vest, smearing red across bone-white silk where Vhane will hiss about it later. “I provided one.” 

He studies my face.

For a heartbeat, the whole room feels like a held breath aimed at me instead of the corpse.

I let the Ornament look back at him. Blankly adoring. Slightly pleased with himself. Hopefully just arrogant enough that Thessen will think nothing more of it than that his toy likes performing.

“Indeed you did,” Thessen says eventually.

His hand lands on my shoulder.

The cuff at my wrist vibrates with the faint thrum of ward-sigils answering some check-in ritual. A leash tug. He squeezes.

“Take the trash away,” he tells the guards, dismissive.

They enter the circle in a clatter of boots and chain, moving with that brutal efficiency all hired muscle eventually learns: get in, get out, don’t get any on your shoes. One of them curls his fingers into the prisoner’s collar and hauls.

The man’s body slides limp across the salt. 

He doesn’t break posture.

Good.

Very good.

Relief slams through me so hard my knees want to give. I let it out as a little laugh instead, turning it into a breathy exhale that the front rows read as blood-high.

The guards drag the “corpse” out of the hall.

He has a chance now.

Not a good one—Thessen’s dungeons are their own circle of hell—but a chance. Time enough, maybe, for Lysa’s precious precedents and my new-found talent for disobedience to swing something his way.

Or at least, I tell myself that so I can keep standing here under the heat of these eyes.

Thessen lifts my arm like I’m the victor in some tournament. The crowd cheers again, louder this time, clinking glasses, stamping feet.

I look for her.

She’s not at the front. She wouldn’t be; Tribunal officers don’t get front-row seats at donor revels. There—mid-tier, slightly to the left, where she can see the whole circle and the exits at once. 

She’s standing.

Everyone else is sitting.

Her face is pale, jaw tight, hands very still on the rail in front of her. From this distance, I can’t see the details, but I know that posture. She’s writing, even without a quill. Recording.

Our eyes meet.

For a moment, the cheers drop into a kind of muffled roar, as if I’ve ducked my head underwater.

She knows.

Of course she does. She saw the angle of the strike, the lack of blood spatter, the way the prisoner fell too neatly. She’s watched enough executions to know how bodies move when the heart stops. This one didn’t.

Her gaze holds mine.

There’s horror there—temporary, sharp—but it’s not for me. It’s for the fact that I can do this. That I have had to get this good at faking deaths to survive. That this is what her beloved system requires of the people it calls assets.

Beneath the horror is something else.

Understanding. A grim, shared, this-is-what-we’re-up-against acknowledgment.

And—unfairly, stupidly—pride.

At me. For this. For picking a third path when the choices were kill and die.

It hits harder than any applause.

I drop my gaze first.

If I keep looking at her, I will do something foolish. Like cry. Or laugh. Or tell the entire room that the only person I’m interested in performing for thinks they’re dreadful and should all go home and read a contract code.

Thessen is still talking.

“…see, my lords and ladies, the value of a properly managed Relic,” he’s saying, voice oily. “Swift. Obedient. Precise. No collateral damage. Imagine what such assets mean for our investments in the lower districts…”

Yes, imagine.

Imagine if some of us decided, one night, to turn those precise blades the other way.

I let my expression smooth into vague satisfaction, sword held low by my side.

Inside, my hands shake.

The glass-steel hilt feels slick. Not with blood—there wasn’t enough of that—but with the sweat of my own palm. The silk vest sticks to my back. The collar is a band of heat around my throat.

Danger, the old, buried animal in me whispers. This is danger.

It’s right.

I have just proven, in front of everyone who matters tonight, that I can kill on command with terrifying efficiency. Also that I am so good at it they can’t tell when I don’t.

It cements the legend. The Relic. Thessen’s dog. The monster at their beck and call.

It also gives me information.

I can fake deaths.

I’ve done it in back alleys a handful of times, when a petty thief or desperate parent got in my way at the wrong moment, and I decided I could live with lying in a report better than I could live with extra ghosts. Never under this kind of scrutiny. 

Lysa now has to wonder: how many of the kills in my file were true? How many were theatre I performed because I couldn’t bear to write the real thing down, and no one above me wanted to look too closely as long as the numbers came out right?

I glance up once more, quick.

She hasn’t moved.

Her hand lifts, just a little, resting over the Tribunal mark at her wrist. A tiny gesture, invisible to anyone not already fluent in her tells. 

I read it as clearly as if she shouted.

I saw. I will remember. We will use this.

“Smile, darling,” Thessen murmurs next to me, low enough for only my ears. “You’re making them nervous when you brood. They like you vicious, not thoughtful.”

I bare my teeth.

“Of course, my lord,” I say. “Wouldn’t want to scare them.”

He chuckles, thinking it a joke.

“Come,” he says, clapping my back. “We have contracts to sign.”

He leads me out of the circle, through the opening in the benches where a little side path leads up into the shadowed private boxes.

As I step over the salt-line, the hairs on my arms prickle. The circle lets me go with obvious reluctance.

Behind us, the floor is already being swept. Salt and bone and a few drops of blood, brushed into a little pile by a servant who will never be paid enough to clean up after so much sanctified filth.

Ahead of me, ceilings hung with velvet and the low murmur of men who trade in souls.

Below and slightly to the left, a woman in blue who has just watched me play the monster for an audience that clapped.

I tuck the sword under my arm.

My free hand brushes, unconsciously, against the inside of my boot, where a much smaller, much more honest piece of glass waits. Just in case. 

I am a weapon.

Tonight, for the first time, I am also the one choosing which way the blade points.

The applause follows me up into the dark like a curse.
