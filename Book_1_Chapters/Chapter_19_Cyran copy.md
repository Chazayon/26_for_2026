# Chapter 19 — *The Maestro's Debt*
### Cyran | POV

The scent of Lysa’s soap is still clinging to my collar when the smell of old, ritualized death hits the back of my throat.  

It’s a jarring combination.  Warm, stubbornly mortal lavender stitched into the seams of my high Vigil collar from the way she’d fisted the leather this morning, pressed up against me in that narrow bed.  And beneath it now, as we step off the main rib thoroughfare and under the fluttering strip of yellow Tribunal quarantine tape, something else rises—a layered stench of iron and rot and the sickly-sweet ghost of temple myrrh.  

Two worlds colliding in my sinuses.  Domesticity and slaughterhouse.  

“Watch your step,” Lysa says quietly.  

Her voice has that clipped, professional edge she wears in public, every word measured for the ears that might be listening.  She’s back in full Tribunal indigo, robes darkened to near black where the earlier rain soaked the hem, leather gloves snug on her hands, ink-stained fingers hidden from view.  The hood’s down for once; bone-light glances off her spectacles as she ducks under the tape after me.  

The marrow‑alley yawns ahead.  Narrow, rib-bone walls close in on either side, the cobbles slick and greasy with whatever runoff the gods leave behind when they die.  The air temperature drops a few degrees the moment we cross the invisible threshold from market din to cordoned crime scene.  It’s subtle but unmistakable, a localized chill that doesn’t care what the weather’s doing overhead.  

Magic.  

Old magic.  

“Relic.”  

Warden Vesper waits just inside the tape, one shoulder braced against the alley’s mouth like he’s physically holding back the city.  His Vigil leathers are scuffed, the brass badge at his collar dull with use rather than polish.  He’s cradling a dented metal thermos in both hands, steam feathering from the spout into the muggy afternoon.  

He eyes me, then Lysa, then the empty air between us.  His gaze lingers a heartbeat too long on the way my shadow overlaps hers on the slick cobbles.  

“Warden.” I incline my head, voice smooth, bored. “You rang.”  

He snorts, passes the thermos to Lysa without taking his eyes off me.  

“Tribunal insisted on another look before we scrape this one up,” he says.  “Said bring your pet auditor and the Relic what keeps showing up wherever folk turn up bled.”  

“Alleged Relic of interest,” Lysa corrects mildly, accepting the thermos.  “And thank you for the coffee.”  

Not coffee.  Vesper’s special sludge.  The smell of overboiled grounds and a hint of something stronger hits me as she cracks the lid and pours a measure into the collapsible tin cup he produces from a pocket.  

He shrugs. “Figured if I was dragging you away from your ledgers, you might as well not freeze while you poke around my alley.”  

My alley.  

The words sit wrong in my ears.  

“Body?” I ask.  

“Gone,” Vesper says. “Carted to the Skull morgue at dawn. But I left the scene clean as I could.” He jerks his chin deeper into the gloom. “Blood’s dried, but the pattern’s… off. Didn’t like it. Didn’t like that it fell smack in the middle of your patrol swath either.”  

“Coincidence,” I say lightly.  “I’m very popular these days.”  

He gives me a flat look that says he’s not in the mood for my usual charm.  

“Don’t piss on my boots and call it marrow‑mist, Cyran,” he says. “You know as well as I do somebody’s stacking bodies on your route for a reason.”  

A muscle jumps in my jaw.  

“Yes,” Lysa says, before I can sharpen my tongue. “That’s why we’re here.”  She offers him the cup, steam curling between them. “You said you’d hold the perimeter?”  

He takes the drink, his scarred knuckles brushing her glove briefly.  “Three of my lads at the ends of the alley, tape on both streets.”  He sips, winces, but keeps going. “You’ve got ten, fifteen, before the gawkers get bored and start demanding a show.”  

“We’ll be quick,” she says.  

Her gaze flicks to me.

We both know “quick” is optimistic.

“Call out if you need anything,” Vesper adds. “Or if the Relic starts foaming at the mouth and reciting scripture.”  

He means it as a joke.  

The fact that it isn’t far from my actual concern would be funny if it didn’t make my teeth ache.  

“We’ll manage,” I say.

He grunts, pushes off the wall, and goes to lean at the far end of the tape, mug in hand, back ostentatiously turned.  Giving us privacy.  Or plausible deniability.  Hard to say which.  

Lysa lets out a slow breath.  

“Ready?” she asks.

No.  

“Yes,” I say.

We move.

The further we step into the marrow‑alley, the more the city drops away.  The sounds of the Lower Ribcage market—haggling, cart wheels, a baby wailing somewhere under the ribs—fade to a muffled murmur.  The scrape of our boots on the greasy cobbles and the soft clink of vials in Lysa’s satchel become the entire world.  

The stench intensifies.  Blood, yes—old, iron‑rich, soaked into porous bone and stone.  But layered under it, the sour rot of marrow‑drain, like spoiled fat, and a thread of something sweeter, cloying.  Myrrh.  The expensive kind.  The kind they used to burn in brass censers above my altar so the donors wouldn’t have to smell their own terror.  

My stomach tightens.

“Here,” Lysa says quietly.

She stops near where the alley walls narrow to a throat.  The cobbles slope down toward a clogged drain, its iron grate bristling with old offerings and trash—wilted bone flowers, mold‑slicked votive candles, a tangle of wax‑sealed ribbon once meant for a god’s ear.  

The blood is a dark, almost black smear on the wall to the left.  At first glance, it looks like any other slaughter in this city—a splash pattern, a vertical streak where something slid down, handprints smudged into nothing.  

I know better.  

Even before Lysa starts unpacking her tools, my bones are humming.  

She sets her satchel on a relatively dry bit of stone, kneels, and pulls out a small glass vial filled with something that looks like liquid moonlight.  Lumen‑dust, suspended in alchemical binding.  Tribunal forensics meets old thaumaturgy.  

“Vesper’s people didn’t use any?” I ask.

She shakes her head, pulling off one glove with her teeth.  “He doesn’t have the clearance. Or the budget.” A flash of teeth around leather. “Perks of knowing where the Marrow Archives hide their toys.”  

She unstoppers the vial.

The air sharpens.

“Light,” she says.

I oblige.

A thought, a flex of something that lives in my blood and bones, and the small lumen‑orb embedded in my bracer flares to life, throwing a cold, steady glow down the alley.  Shadows leap back, crowding up the walls.  The bloodstain shines dully in the new light, ugly and mundane.  

Lysa rises, moves close, and with a delicacy that would make a surgeon’s hands look clumsy, she flicks the lumen‑dust toward the wall.  

For a heartbeat, nothing happens.

Then the dust catches.

It clings to the stone like frost finding a window.  Thin tendrils of silver filament stitch themselves along lines I hadn’t seen, creeping over the dark patch in precise, deliberate arcs.  What looked like a smear resolves into structure.  

“Come on,” she murmurs. “Show yourself.”  

The dust obeys.

An outline blooms in pale, shimmering lines.  Circles within circles.  Spokes radiating out like a fractured sun.  Curved, hooked sigils nestling between each line like teeth.  

My knees go loose.

No.  

It isn’t possible.  

But there it is.

A harvest binding sigil.  Temple design.  Very specific.  Very old.  

I know it because it lived in my bones before it ever lived on this wall.  Because Varos carved its sister into my chest the first time he tethered my blood to his altar.  

My vision tunnels.  

The smell of myrrh roars up, drowning the alley’s mildew.  I taste cinnamon and ash on my tongue, phantom wine so vivid I almost gag.  

“Cyran.” Lysa’s voice is distant. Muffled. Like she’s speaking from the bottom of a well.

I take a step back.

The wall looms.  

Another step.  

My back hits cold stone.  The opposite side of the alley.  There is, quite literally, nowhere else to go.  

I am pinned between my past and my present, and the air is too thin.  

“Cyran.” Closer now. Sharper. “You’re in the Lower Ribcage. With me. It’s year eighty‑four. Look at me.”  

I drag my gaze off the glowing sigil.

It feels like ripping a hook out of my own flesh.

Lysa stands a few paces away, angled half‑between me and the wall, blocking the worst of the light from my direct line of sight.  Her hand is outstretched, fingers steady despite the tightness in her jaw.  Leather glove back on.  

“May I?” she asks.

Two words.

Anchors.

I seize them.

“Yes,” I rasp.

She closes the distance in three firm steps and wraps her gloved hand around mine.  Her grip is strong, no careful, fragile touch—she holds like she means it, like she expects me to try to slip away and has no intention of letting me.  

The leather is rough under my palm.  Warm from her skin beneath.  It gives me something immediate to catalogue: texture, pressure, the tiny squeak as her thumb tightens over my knuckles.  

“Breathe,” she says quietly. “In. Out. Count.”  

I obey.

One.  Two.  Three.  

My fangs are down; I can feel the ache in my gums.  My heart—such as it is—pounds a weird, stumbling rhythm against my ribs, loud enough that it drowns out the drip of water from the clogged drain.  

“They used that,” I say finally, nodding toward the shining pattern without really looking at it. “On harvest nights. To pull the heart‑blood up from the god’s bones. To… arrange the offerings.”  

“Harvest binding,” she says, voice tight. “Like in the Archivist’s notes.”  

“In the—” I choke. “You’ve seen this before?”  

“Sketches,” she says. “Solen’s hand. Faded, half‑erased. I thought it was theoretical. An extinct practice.”  

“Extinct,” I echo, a bitter scrap of laughter tearing itself free. “He’s never been good at staying dead.”  

She squeezes my hand.

“Who?” she asks, but it isn’t ignorance. It’s invitation.

“Varos,” I say.

The name tastes like poison and incense.

Saying it aloud in this alley feels like opening a door I have spent two centuries bricking over.

“He’s alive,” I add needlessly.

The glowing sigil pulses faintly, lumen‑dust catching the damp air.  It looks like something breathing.  

Lysa’s mouth flattens.

“We don’t know that yet,” she says, automatic lawyer’s caution. “This could be a copy. A—”

“Look at the lower ring,” I cut in.

She shifts, stepping a little to the side so we can both see without me having to let go of her hand.

The lumen‑dust has traced every line the blood once drew.  Along the bottom edge of the main circle, a smaller, overlapping pattern emerges—nested triangles, a jagged three‑sided mark punching through a ring.  Unlike the smooth, sacred curves of the temple script above, this is all edges and angles, a cipher meant to be quick to carve into wax or flesh.  

I don’t recognize it.  

Lysa does.

“That’s not temple,” she whispers. “That’s a Contract Lord’s cipher.”  

I turn my head, slowly, to look at her.

Thessen’s smirk in the Sanctum.  His perfumed, lavender rot.  “Careers end for less.”  

“His?” I ask.

She hesitates. “I can’t be sure without the full ledger key,” she says. “But the shape—Lord ciphers all use the same structure. Triangles for Contract authority, circles for bound assets. This says a Contract Lord has rights over whatever this binding is doing.”  

“Of course it does,” I breathe. “Why steal a god if you can’t put your name on the receipt?”  

My laugh comes out wrong.

Too high.  Too sharp.  

“They didn’t just survive separately,” Lysa says slowly. “They’re working together. Varos for the old blood magic. Thessen for the legal cover.”  

A neat little circle.

Like the one glowing on the wall.

Sacrifice in the alleys.  Panic in the markets.  Bodies on my patrol routes for plausible cause.  Tribunal scrambles to contain the scandal.  Thessen steps in with a solution: take the dangerous Relic off the streets, put him back under ‘proper’ temple care, monetized this time.  

New master, same tricks.  

The alley lurches.

For a moment, I’m back under the Skull dome.  Censer smoke in my lungs, command phrases threading my spine.  Varos’s voice layered under Priest Kelton’s.  

Always a pleasure, Father.  

“Cyran.” Lysa’s voice slices through the echo like a knife. “Here. Now. With me.”  

She steps closer, closing what little space remained between us until my shoulders are flat against the bone wall and she is almost pressed to my chest.  The scent of her—ink, soap, rain—cuts through the myrrh like fresh air.  

Her free hand comes up, hovers for a second, then rests very deliberately on my chest, over the place where a heart should beat.  

“Breathe,” she says again. “You’re not on his altar. You’re in a filthy alley with terrible lighting and worse jurisdiction.”  

Despite everything, a hoarse sound escapes me that might be a laugh.

“Hardly flattering,” I manage.

“Good,” she says. “I’m not trying to flatter you. I’m trying to keep you here.”  

I look down at her.

At the resolve carved into every line of her face.  At the damp hair plastered to her forehead.  At the smudge of lumen‑dust on her cheekbone, catching the faint light like a tiny star.  

I realize, with a sudden, sick clarity, just how much danger she’s in by standing next to me in this alley.

Varos doesn’t break his toys.  He repurposes them.  Thessen doesn’t discard assets.  He punishes thieves.  

And Lysa?  

She walked into their ledger room and set fire to the margins.  

“I should leave,” I say. The words tear at my throat. “Get out before they tighten the noose. You can still claim you tried. That you fought. That you lost.”  

Her eyes flash.

“There it is,” she says softly. “Your favourite solution. Run. Alone. Let them have you quietly so no one else gets hurt.”  

“It’s worked before,” I say bitterly.

“No,” she says. “It hasn’t. You’re still in a cage, Cyran. They just changed the paint.”  

She doesn’t raise her voice.

She doesn’t have to.

Each word lands like a gavel.

“You leave,” she continues, “they win. Varos gets his Relic back. Thessen gets his profit margin. And the Prime Contract keeps bleeding this city dry. You stay, we make this hurt.”  

We.

My hand clenches around hers.

“Lysa,” I say. “If we take this to Voss now—if we prove Varos is alive—the Tribunal won’t see a monster risen from the grave.” I jerk my chin at the sigil. “They’ll see a lapsed property claim. They’ll drag me into a sanctum and argue over who owns me.”  

“I know,” she says. “We’re not taking it to Voss. Not yet.”  

The certainty in her voice is terrifying and exhilarating in equal measure.

“Then what?” I demand. “Frame it? Hope nobody else thinks to use lumen‑dust and stumble over the same truth in a week?”  

She releases my chest, but not my hand.

Her expression hardens.  The same look she wore in the Sanctum when she told Voss no.  

“We take the record,” she says, reaching for the leather‑bound notebook at her hip with her free hand.  “We document everything. Placement, structure, the Contract cipher. Then we leave it. Let them think it’s just another random atrocity until we’re ready to make our move.”  

“You’re asking me to walk away from this,” I say.

“I’m asking you to live long enough to make it matter,” she shoots back.  

Her thumb brushes my wrist, a tiny, grounding stroke.

“Cyran,” she says more softly. “They have had centuries to perfect this system. Temple, Tribunal, Contracts—it’s one machine now. We can’t smash it in a day with one glowing wall. We need allies. Precedent. Proof that doesn’t hand you to Varos on a silver platter.”  

Her logic is brutal and precise.

She’s right.

I hate that she’s right.

“So we do nothing,” I say. It tastes like ash.

“We do not nothing,” she says. “We act carefully.”  She flips the notebook open with one hand, charcoal nub already between her fingers. “And we start by stealing back this knowledge.”  

Her hand leaves mine only long enough to sketch, fast and sure, the main circle, the harvest spokes, the jagged Contract mark below.  Her shorthand annotations crowd the margins: Archivist match, see Solen file. Cipher unknown—triangular, likely Contract Lord. Residual cold. Myrrh trace.  

I watch her work.

Despite the panic still beating under my skin, a thin thread of something like awe weaves through.

They carved this into me once.  Branded my soul with symbols meant to make me a tool, an object, a pretty, obedient engine of blood.  

Now she’s taking their script and rewriting it in her own hand.  

“Done,” she says, snapping the notebook shut. The sound cracks through the alley like a small shot.  

She looks up at me.

“We have what we need for now,” she says. “The rest stays here as bait.”  

I swallow.

“Bait?” I echo.

“For them,” she says simply. “Not for you. They already think they have you on the hook. Let them. It’ll make the snap that much more satisfying.”  

Varos’s sigil glows quietly on the wall, silver lines fraying at the edges as the lumen‑dust begins to settle.  

He’s alive.

He’s here.

He’s working with Thessen.

The frame‑up isn’t just politics.  It’s a bespoke trap built to slide me back under a knife.  

Fear claws at my throat.

So does something else.

Rage.

Not the hot, theatrical kind I throw at donors for show.  A colder thing.  Older.  It settles in my bones like a decision.  

I look at Lysa.

She holds my gaze, unflinching.

“Then we don’t let them,” she says. She squeezes my hand hard enough to hurt. “We’re leaving. Now.”  

There’s no room in her tone for argument.

Good.

I’ve had enough arguing with myself for one lifetime.

We step away from the wall together, fingers still linked.  The residual chill clings to my skin like frost.  Each step toward the mouth of the alley feels like walking out of a grave.  

The quarantine tape flutters ahead in the damp breeze.  Beyond it, the market hum swells, the ordinary filth and commerce of the Ribcage District waiting like nothing has changed.  

Vesper straightens as we approach.

“Well?” he asks, eyes flicking between our faces, then to our joined hands, then politely away again.

“Pattern’s the same,” Lysa says, slipping effortlessly back into her Tribunal cadence. “We’ll need to review our notes before we can draw any conclusions.”  

Translation: Not here. Not now.

Vesper grunts.

“City hall will want a statement,” he says to me.

“They can have one,” I say, my voice surprisingly steady. “After my lawyer looks it over.”  

His brows lift.

“Didn’t know you had one,” he says.

I glance at Lysa.

“I do now,” I say.

She doesn’t smile.

But something like it flickers, brief and dangerous, at the corner of her mouth.

We duck back under the tape, into the wet light of the Lower Ribcage.  The marrow‑stink thins.  The market noise floods back in—vendors shouting, someone arguing over the price of fungus, a wagon wheel hitting a rut with a crack.  

Behind us, in the cold throat of the alley, Varos’s sigil fades as the lumen‑dust settles.  

Ahead of us, the dead god’s ribs arch overhead, bone‑white against a sky the color of old parchment.  

My collar still smells like Lysa’s soap.  My hand is still wrapped around hers.  

The monster from my nightmares has stepped back into the light.

I am not going back into his cage alone.
