# Chapter 8 — *The Currency of Flesh*
### Lysa | POV

He is wearing the silk shirt again. The one that looks like it was woven from spiderwebs and bad decisions.

For one beat I just stand in his doorway, fingers still wrapped around the cool curve of the bone-handle, letting the air of his quarters hit me.

Last time I was here, the room pretended to be a boudoir for the gods: velvet over bone, candlelight on mirrors, fruit no one ate. Tonight, it has given up pretending and embraced it.

The fire in the carved bone grate burns lower, more ember than flame, just enough to paint everything in amber and shadow. Most of the candelabras are snuffed; those that remain throw soft, strategic pools of light across silk and skin. The drapes are drawn tight, blocking out the corpse-city’s sickly glow. Someone—him, of course it’s him—has refreshed the incense. It’s heavier now, less sandalwood, more something languid and expensive that clings to the back of my throat.

This is not a patrol debriefing room. This is a stage.

Cyran stands by the fire like he’s been placed there by a careful hand. Black trousers. That cream silk shirt, open a little lower than regulation modesty would tolerate, collarbones and the top curve of a sigil-etched chest catching the light. His hair is artfully tousled, deliberate disarray that says I woke up like this and we both know that’s a lie. The bandage I wrapped around his forearm is gone, skin smooth; the one at his ribs is hidden, if it’s there at all.

My goal tonight had been simple enough on paper: clarify his understanding of the audit’s scope before Voss and Thessen twist my report into a permission slip for the Gala. Set expectations. Build procedural tripwires.

I didn’t have a contingency plan for this.

“Investigator,” he says, and the word is softer than usual. Less mockery, more invitation. “You’re prompt. I appreciate that in a woman coming to ruin me.”

“There is a specific kind of silence that happens before a man sells his soul,” I think, stepping inside. “This feels like standing in it.”

Aloud, I say, “You sent word that we needed to talk.”

“I did.” He reaches for the decanter on the low table by the hearth and begins to pour before I’m halfway to him. Dark red, full-bodied, the same vintage as last time. “To survival,” he adds, lifting one glass toward me in a mock-toast.

I don’t take it.

“We need to talk about the report,” I say.

He watches my empty hand for a breath too long. Then he lowers the glass, mouth curving.

“The report is finished,” he says. “Voss is happy. Thessen is happy. What more could we possibly have to discuss?”

“I’m not happy.”

The words come out flatter than I intend. Too honest.

He laughs once, under his breath. “No. You’re the difficult one.”

“I prefer thorough.”

“Of course you do.” He sets his own glass down with exaggerated care, the crystal making a neat little click against the wood. The movement is deliberate. Placing props before a performance.

Something in my spine tightens.

“We can sit,” I say. “This doesn’t need to be—”

“Formal?” he supplies, taking a step closer. “Oh, I agree. We’ve gone well past formal, haven’t we?”

Heat licks, uninvited, along the back of my neck. Bone-dust storm in a shrine alcove. His arm braced against the ceiling as debris fell. His cloak heavy around my shoulders. The solid line of his thigh against mine, the warmth of his body bleeding slowly through layers of fabric in the dark.

You kept me warm in the tunnel, he says now, as if catching the memory out of my head and turning it in his hands. “Or was that a hallucination induced by hypothermia?”

“That was survival,” I say. My voice is tight. “For both of us.”

“Was it?” He takes another step.

He shouldn’t be able to move like that after hours on patrol—loose and languid, weight rolling onto the balls of his feet, every line of him saying predator and every little hesitation saying please don’t run. The firelight licks along the edge of his jaw, catches in his eyes. The skin along his throat looks newly unmarked where a priest once carved prayers into him.

The room is smaller than I remember. Or he’s larger. Or I’m too aware of the distance closing between us to trust my own measurements.

“I know how this works, Lysa,” he says.

My name in his mouth is not the same as Investigator. It lands lower. Closer.

My pulse stutters. He’s avoided my name until now, using titles like armour. Hearing it now feels like he’s slipped a hand under that armour without asking.

“Do you,” I say, because I need the words to be steady and cool, not responding to the way he says it like a promise.

“You want compliance,” he says, very softly. “I want… not to be destroyed.”

His hand lifts.

Not fast. Not sudden. The opposite. Knuckles first, fingers curled in, the back of his hand brushing up along the air between us as if asking permission before it touches anything real.

When his knuckles graze my cheek, the contact is feather-light. My skin jolts anyway, every nerve under it going painfully alert.

He smells of polished bone and expensive wax and the faint metallic ghost of blood that never quite leaves him, no matter how much he scrubs. Up close like this, I can see the small flecks of darker colour in his pale irises, the micro-scars along his lower lip where someone’s teeth once broke skin. His Marks prickle faintly against my awareness—the carved sigils under his shirt reacting to the room’s thick incense, to the remembered storm, to me.

I tremble.

Not much. But enough that he feels it; his fingers twitch, the back of his knuckles pressing a fraction more firmly into my cheekbone. I hate that my body answers like this. I hate that he feels it.

“We both know what I’m good at trading,” he whispers.

The words are ice water.

Not because they’re false. Because they’re true.

I see suddenly not just him—the gorgeous, dangerous creature in front of me—but the line leading up to this moment: temple rites, donors in masked revels, priests making bargains with other mens bodies. Thessen in the briefing room, rolling a grape between his fingers. Wealthy patrons like to see what their contributions support. The file’s bland little phrase Subject responsiveness to instruction satisfactory. Capacity for independent function limited.

They taught him, very early, what his currency was. They’ve been spending it ever since.

My stomach turns. Something like grief slams into the want simmering under my skin and tangles with it.

I grab his wrist.

My fingers close around the thinness there just above the bone, stopping his hand half an inch from sliding into my hair the way I know, with a sick certainty, he has done for donors and priests and anyone else who paid the right price.

“Stop,” I say.

He freezes.

For a heartbeat, his eyes go very young. Not in the way we use that word for immortals—eternal, ageless—but in the way of someone who has been caught mid-gesture too many times and had pain follow.

“Am I reading the signal wrong?” he asks, brittle lightness limping back into his voice. “You want this.”

I want you.

The thought is immediate. Hot. Inarguable.

I want the way your mouth curve when you’re not performing. I want the sound you make when a child runs out of danger and you realize you didn’t die for them. I want the way you went very, perfectly still in that alcove so I could sleep against you and the storm couldn’t touch me.

“I don’t want to buy you,” I say.

The words land between us like a dropped stone.

His wrist flexes under my fingers. For a second, I think he’ll pull away, jerk back, snarl. He doesn’t. He just stands there, my hand clamped around his, every line of his body waiting.

“You’re offering yourself like currency,” I continue, quieter now, because the anger has coalesced into something sharper. “Again.”

“How else would I offer?” he says. There’s no humour in it. Just bewilderment and a flicker of offence. “It’s what I have. It’s what they want. You want my compliance, my charm on display in those dreadful little forms. I want your kindness on record. This seems… efficient.”

There’s the lie: that this is about efficiency. Not survival. Not fear.

I let go of his wrist. Deliberately. My fingers leave little pale dents in the skin that fill in as blood—or whatever passes for it in him—returns.

“I won’t be one more name on the list of people who took you like a favor,” I say.

His face shutters.

Truly shutters. I didn’t think there were more doors left to close in him; I was wrong. Whatever expression he’d been wearing—seductive, coaxing, hopeful in a way that made me want to set the world on fire on his behalf—vanishes as if someone blew out a candle behind his eyes.

He takes a controlled step back.

“A moralist,” he says, tone suddenly sharp and crystalline. “How tedious.”

“It’s not morality,” I say. My hands are very still at my sides now. If I move them, I’m afraid they’ll reach for him again. “It’s respect.”

“Respect.” He laughs.

It’s a brittle sound, like glass cracking in a fire. The glamour of the room tears a little at the edges with it. For a heartbeat I see not the polished predator but the exhausted blade. The tired ornament someone propped back up for one more show.

“Respect doesn’t keep me alive, love,” he says. The pet name is a weapon now, not a caress. “Usefulness does.”

“Then find another use,” I snap.

The words come out before I can soften them, precise and hard.

His gaze flicks to my mouth, then back to my eyes.

“Such as?” he asks. “Perhaps I could take up knitting. Or learn to balance Tribunal ledgers with my teeth. Tell me, Investigator—what other talents do you think the Vigil cares about in their pet monster?”

The phrase pet monster hits something under my own breastbone.

“You’re not a pet,” I say. “You’re a person they turned into a thing. That’s already an obscenity. I won’t compound it.”

His lip curls.

“You keep saying that,” he says. “As if there’s a difference that matters. As if any of them”—a little flick of his fingers takes in Voss, Thessen, unnamed donors, the whole invisible crowd—“look at me and see anything but a very pretty ledger entry that kills things on command.”

“I see you,” I say, and it feels pathetic, inadequate, against the weight of what’s been done to him.

“Congratulations.” He inclines his head, mock-courtly. “You and the storm. Both of you very moved by the tragedy.”

The memory of his shoulder under my cheek in the dark alcove punches through me again. The way he’d sat so utterly still, not daring to breathe, as if my sleep were some fragile holy thing he might break by existing too loudly.

“You kept me warm,” I say. “In that tunnel. You didn’t ask for anything for it. Not then.”

His mouth twists. “You shared a cloak and didn’t immediately stab me while I was vulnerable. In my experience, that’s foreplay.”

“This is not foreplay,” I say.

“It could have been.” His expression flickers, pain showing through in a sliver before he slams something down over it. “It was supposed to be. That’s how this works. A few near-death experiences. Some shared confessions in the dark. Then we go to bed and you stop being dangerous.”

It takes me a second to catch my breath.

“I am not dangerous to you,” I say, very quietly.

He stares.

“You’re holding my future in your pretty, ink-stained hands,” he says. “You will decide, in some tasteful, neutral prose, whether I am fit to continue being carved up for Marks or if I should be… retired. You have no idea how dangerous that makes you.”

“I know exactly how dangerous that makes me,” I reply. “That’s why I’m here, not in your bed.”

Something like hurt flashes too quick to name.

Then his shoulders relax. Not real relaxation. The performance of it. His whole posture slides back into the charming monster I first met on the cistern ledge.

“Very well,” he says lightly. “Consider the offer rescinded. Terribly unprofessional of me to have made it in the first place. Voss would be scandalized.”

“Voss would be thrilled,” I say. “So would Thessen.”

At Thessen’s name, the corners of his eyes tighten. Just enough.

“You think this is about them?” he asks.

“I think they taught you that this is the only leverage you have,” I say. “And they’re counting on you using it until there’s nothing left of you to audit.”

“Then we are all doing our jobs excellently,” he says. “You refuse to abuse your power; they abuse theirs; I continue to be very good at dying for people who can’t even spell my name without a file in front of them.”

“Stop.”

The word comes out sharper than I intend. He goes still anyway. Old reflex.

“You joke about being property like it’s clever,” I say. “It’s also killing you.”

He stares at me.

“You want my cooperation,” he says slowly. “You want me to parade in front of donors and not bite any of them and file my reports on time and not upset your statistics. You want me to sit very prettily in my kennel while they decide whether I get to be a man again before the bones of this city finally give out.”

“Yes,” I say. It feels like a confession. “I want all of that. And I want you alive at the end of it.”

“And yet,” he says. “Here we are.”

“Here we are,” I agree.

The space between us feels like a bone-chasm.

I can feel my own eyes burning. I will not cry here. In this room. In front of this man. Not about this.

“If you want something from me,” I say, “ask for it plainly. Not like this. Not as payment. I won’t—” My voice catches; I force it even. “I won’t take you like a favour.”

He lets out a breath that might be a laugh and might be a wound.

“I don’t know how to do that,” he says. “Want without owing. Want without buying. You keep saying no, and I keep thinking, eventually, you’ll remember that’s how this city works and stop.”

“Then let me be the exception,” I say. “For once.”

The words hang there, naked.

His jaw works. Something like hunger and something like terror flicker in his expression, warring.

He steps back.

The distance between us opens like a cut.

“Get out,” he says.

He says it calmly. No raised voice. No theatrics. That, somehow, makes it worse.

My throat tightens. “Cyran—”

“That’s an order from a dangerous man to a dangerous woman,” he says, with a curl of his lip. “You respect those, don’t you? Go. Before I say something we’ll both have to file as unbecoming conduct.”

There it is. The wall.

I nod once, because I don’t trust my voice, and turn.

The corridor air is cooler when I step into it. Less perfumed. More honest—bone-dust and distant damp and the faint copper ghost of the Ribcage’s arteries.

I close his door with care. The latch clicks home.

My legs carry me away on habit, past the neighbor’s door with its gossiping eyes, down the narrow stair that still smells faintly of the storm’s dust. My hands are shaking.

I press my thumb to my wrist, to the Tribunal mark there, the little circle of ink that says I am supposed to be the one who knows how to do this correctly. To love justice more than outcomes. To write what is true even when it hurts.

Behind my ribs, something feels like it has been scraped raw.

I have just hurt him. Deliberately. Strategically. To keep from hurting him worse later.

It doesn’t feel like victory. It feels like loss.

I am attracted to him. I am not going to pretend otherwise, not here, alone with the god’s bones and the echo of his fingers on my cheek.

I am not going to use him. Not as currency. Not as consolation for a life spent writing other people’s pain into neat lines the system can ignore.

Someday, if we survive this, if he survives this, if he learns how to want without selling and I learn how to want without martyring myself on my ethics, I might say yes.

Tonight, the only honest word I have is no.

It echoes all the way back to the Shrine Hall in my memory: the man who screamed when they took his Mark and the line I wrote—Subject distress: High. It echoes forward to the Gala I can feel bearing down like another storm.

No to Thessen. No to Voss. No to the temple that made him think his only worth is what he can trade.

No to myself.

I keep walking. The city creaks around me. Somewhere behind one of these bone-walls, a Relic sits alone in a beautiful room, convincing himself that my refusal was judgment, not protection.

I will have to find a way to explain the difference.

Not tonight.
