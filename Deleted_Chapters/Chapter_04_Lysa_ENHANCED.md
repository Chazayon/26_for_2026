# Chapter 4 — *The Coin He Knows*
### Lysa | POV

I expected a barracks. I walk into a boudoir.

The door swings inward on silent hinges, and for a moment I just stand there with one hand still on the satchel strap, letting my eyes adjust from marrow-mist gloom to candlelight.

Bone walls, yes—this is still the Ribcage, the god's carcass doesn't let anyone forget that—but here they're smothered under velvet. Burgundy drapes fall in heavy folds from ceiling to floor, swallowing the curve of the ribs, tassels glinting dull gold in the low light. Tapestries hide the worst of the structural cracks. The air is thick with sandalwood and beeswax and something warmer underneath that I refuse, for now, to name.

Behind me, in the corridor, the Reliquary creaks on its old axes. Ahead, in this room, glassware clinks softly as Cyran crosses toward a sideboard like a man crossing a stage he's walked a thousand times.

I'm still wearing damp Ribcage air in my robes. The cistern's copper tang clings to the wool, thin as a second skin. The memory of his dagger at my throat ghosts there too, a phantom line of pressure, even though his hands are presently occupied with a decanter.

I am tired. My head aches faintly from punishment sigils and late hours. My goal, tonight, is simple enough to write down and somehow much harder to live: conduct a professional audit interview, gather clear answers on three civilian deaths, and leave without becoming one more variable in Thessen's personal experiment with his favorite weapon.

"Welcome to my humble kennel," Cyran says, not looking at me as he uncorks the bottle. "Try not to bite the furniture. It's on loan."

The room is warm enough that I feel it at once, seeping up from floor to spine. A low fire smolders in a grate carved into the bone wall, turning the carved ribs above it into something that looks disturbingly like a god's chest lit from within. Multiple candelabras have been lit despite there being more than enough from the hearth—one on the mantel, two on the low table, a cluster on the sideboard where he stands. Their flames reflect in the mirrors that punctuate the walls, multiplying light and movement until it feels like more than two people are in here.

Or perhaps that's just him.

He's stripped most of the leather away. No armor now, only black trousers and a silk shirt the color of old cream, sleeves rolled to the elbow, collar unlaced half a handspan down. His Marks remain mostly covered, but there are glimpses of carved sigils at his forearms, pale lines catching candlelight. His hair is damp at the temples, as if he ran wet fingers through it on the way up the stairs.

I catalog the rest because cataloging is what I do, and because if I name it correctly I can stop noticing it: the height of him, which the narrow cistern had obscured; cheekbones that catch the light as if they were designed for the purpose, cutting shadows into geometry; a mouth with considerably more architecture than it needs. And his eyes, when they lift from the decanter to find mine across the room—pale grey, the color of ice over water you can't gauge the depth of.

He is beautiful. Not the way the temple idols are beautiful—not symbolic, not ornamental—but the specific, inconvenient, embodied kind that you notice at the worst possible moments and regret for considerably longer.

Calculated disarray. The file called it unbecoming conduct. I call it deliberate.

"Red or white?" he asks. The decanter glugs, dark liquid sliding into a crystal goblet with an entirely unnecessary flourish of his wrist. "The red is… full-bodied."

Of course it is.

"Water," I say, crossing to the table. My boots sink half a finger's breadth into the carpet—the carpet, in the Ribcage, where most people are grateful if the floor has all its bone slats and no open marrow gaps. "Please."

He pauses, just for a second. Then he recovers, reaching for a cut-glass carafe I hadn't noticed beside the wine.

"Admirable restraint, Investigator," he says lightly. "You put the rest of us to shame."

"Someone needs to be able to write legibly when this is over."

I set my satchel down beside the table and take stock.

The room has been furnished with a single hard-backed chair and several things that are not chairs so much as suggestions of horizontal surfaces draped in fabric—the sort of chaise that invites draping, lounging, being draped upon. Cushions, overstuffed and embroidered, spill off a low divan. There is a plate on the low table stacked with fruit and cheese wedges artfully arranged, none of which look as if any teeth have ever actually touched them.

The only honest thing in the room is the chair.

I sit in it.

Cyran brings the drinks over on a small tray like a penitent altar boy, except altar boys usually aren't barefoot and half-unbuttoned.

He sets the goblet of wine down nearest to himself and the tumbler of water in front of me. The glass sweats faintly in the heat. My throat is abruptly, painfully dry.

I pick it up and drink. Not a cautious sip; my body doesn't care about appearances. Cool, metallic-tanged water knifes through the fuzz at the back of my tongue, cutting the cistern from my mouth. I keep my eyes on him over the rim. If he's watching my throat as I swallow, I don't give him the satisfaction of acknowledging it.

He doesn't sit. Of course he doesn't. He leans instead, one hip against the edge of the table so that he angles into my eyeline no matter where I try to rest it. The fire paints his profile in amber and shadow. The nearest mirror catches his reflection and throws it back—Cyran seen from the side, Cyran seen over my shoulder, Cyran pouring himself another finger of wine on the sideboard as if this is an evening among friends.

"So, Auditor," he says at last, lifting his glass. "What does the file say I am?"

He makes it sound like a flirtation. He makes everything sound like a flirtation. It's a weapon like any other.

I set my tumbler down, deliberately, careful not to let it clink.

"Effective," I say. "And expensive."

One of his eyebrows arches. "Flattering."

"Five thousand Merit Marks." I tick them off on invisible fingers. "Thirty years of service. Threshold recalculated four times, always in the same direction."

Something in his jaw twitches. It's very small. If I hadn't spent the last six years watching monsters' faces for the moment their claims of innocence cracked, I might have missed it.

"Ah," he says. "You read all the way to the end. Most people get bored around the part where they list my preferred weaponry and assume the rest is decorative markup."

"That's because most people aren't paid to be thorough."

"Is that what they're calling it now?" He traces the rim of his glass with one fingertip, a lazy circle. "I always thought you lot got paid to be merciful when it counts and blind when it doesn't."

"Mercy doesn't appear in my job description."

"But judgment does."

He says it lightly, as if we're discussing the weather. His eyes, in the nearest mirror, study my face like a reflection he doesn't quite trust.

The fire snaps. I find myself looking, involuntarily, at those mirrors too.

At what's in them.

He catches the light like someone placed deliberately in it. Men like Cyran are not designed for institutional chairs and audit interviews; they're designed for exactly this—amber candle-glow, velvet backdrop, the particular kind of beautiful that makes you feel that witnessing it is a privilege rather than an accident. The file mentioned temple-trained. Looking at him now, I understand that this is what that means. Someone taught him to stand the way light finds him. Someone taught his face what angles to hold.

I do not look like this. I know what I look like. The mirrors are quite thorough about it: indigo robes that want pressing, hair that was functional an hour ago and has since reconsidered, spectacles that migrate down my nose with the persistence of a junior clerk who thinks he knows better. Ink on the second finger of my right hand that I have been unable to fully remove for six years. A face that colleagues call *focused* and strangers call nothing at all, because it doesn't invite the kind of looking where you'd need a word for what you're doing.

Women like me don't get candle-lit rooms. We get the waiting corridor outside them. We keep the machinery running and then we go home, and we do not sit across from men whose faces were made as a deliberate argument against practicality and think—

I stop that thought with a precision that would embarrass me if anyone could read it.

He is performing. I know he is performing. Every practiced angle, every lazy smile, every deliberate lean into my eyeline—this is a man who learned long ago exactly what beauty does and how to weaponize it. It has nothing to do with me specifically. I am simply the nearest available surface.

That is fine. That is useful, in fact, because it means I don't have to be surprised by it.

"The file says," I continue, because naming the thing usually helps, "that your capacity for independent function is limited."

He laughs, sharp and bright, startling in the close air.

"Is that what they wrote?" he says. "How delightfully creative. I'll be sure to accost my next god-spawn with a supervisor present so as not to strain my… capacity."

"Is it accurate?" I ask.

He tilts his head, feigning thoughtfulness. "Which answer keeps me on the right side of your pen, love?"

"I'm not here to keep you on any side," I reply. "I'm here to record what is."

"Spoken like a true believer."

He pushes off the table then, sliding around behind my chair. I feel the movement more than see it; a shift in heat along my left shoulder, the faintest disturbance of air as his silk sleeve brushes the back of the chair. Every survival instinct in me wants to twist to keep him in front of me.

I don't. I make myself stay still. My spine straightens another degree.

"Do you mind?" I ask, conversational, eyes on my notebook as I unbuckle it from my belt and flip it open.

"Desperately," he says, voice nearer my ear now. "But I've learned to be patient with discomfort. It keeps one… flexible."

The scent of him folds over the sandalwood and wax. Leather, yes, underlaid with some expensive cologne chosen carefully to hit that impossible point between temple-incense and donor-drawing room. Under all of it, faint and dangerous, the iron warmth that is simply him.

"Tell me, Investigator," he murmurs. I can feel the words brush the short hairs along the nape of my neck. "What does unbecoming conduct mean in that pretty file of yours?"

"There are fourteen categories." My voice is steady. Too steady. "Which would you like to be charged with?"

"Let's start with all of them." His hand descends, very lightly, to rest on the back of my chair. "Then we can narrow the list together."

His fingertips brush my shoulder.

My notebook pen halts mid-stroke.

For one embarrassing half-second—less than a breath, less than a heartbeat—something in me does not process this as tactical. Something in me processes this as: he turned that toward me. Specifically. Not the room, not the role, not the Tribunal badge—*me*.

I know better. I know exactly better. The self-awareness comes instantly, a familiar catalogue: the ink on my fingers, the chair-backed posture, the spectacles, the audit ledger currently open in my lap. I know what I am in this room and what he is in this room and the structural distance between those two things, which has nothing to do with height and everything to do with physics. Men who look like him are not short of options. Men who look like him do not aim this at women who have been called *focused* their entire professional lives.

But the knowledge comes a beat after the reaction. That beat—that single, unguarded beat—costs me something I refuse to calculate.

It is the lightest possible contact, more suggestion than touch, but every nerve under my robes knows exactly how many finger-widths there are between my Tribunal mark on my wrist and where he's just laid his hand. My breath catches before I wrestle it back into regularity.

Most find the report writing easier after… relaxation, he might say next. Men like Thessen always couch it like that. For their sake, of course. It's easier if you don't fight.

"Cyran." I close the notebook. The sound of it snapping shut is louder than it has any right to be.

He stills.

Using someone's name has weight. In the Tribunal, in the Vigil's temples, in bed. Names anchor or bind depending who speaks them.

He lifts his hand away from my shoulder as if burned.

I stand, turning to face him. Too close now for comfort; the hard chair scrapes the carpet, pressing against the backs of my calves. We're nearly chest to chest. His shirt gapes at the throat, a pale triangle of skin exposed above the first sigil carved there. The firelight throws his scars into relief, catching in the fine lines cut into him like someone tried to write prayers on his body and forgot which language they were speaking.

His eyes are not on mine.

They're lower, measuring the distance between us, the tilt of my chin, the set of my mouth. His pupils are blown wide, but I know enough to read that as Thirst, not necessarily desire.

"Your body," I say, and I make each word precise, because sloppy words become sloppy ethics, "isn't payment I'll accept."

The air between us stretches.

He freezes in a way that has nothing to do with predatory stillness and everything to do with a system shorting. Glass in his hand, half-raised toward his mouth, stops midway. The reflection of us in the nearest mirror is suddenly less performance and more evidence: a Relic caught in the act of offering himself as coin, an Auditor standing toe-to-toe with him, refusing to take it.

Something fractured and ugly flickers through his expression. It's gone almost before I can catalogue it, smoothed over by a smile that tries very hard to be languid.

"Pity," he says. "I'm told I'm excellent payment."

He makes it sound like a joke. It isn't one.

Something loosens, unwanted, in my chest. Under the professional distance, under the firm steady lines I drew around this situation before I walked in the door, there is a person who heard *I'm told I'm excellent payment* and understood, for the first time, that he is offering because offering is the only move he has left, and that the offering has nothing to do with wanting.

The small, shameful part of me had wondered, for exactly as long as it took to draw one breath, whether it might.

I keep my gaze on his. I will not look away first.

"I'm sure you are," I say quietly. "But I'm not a merchant. And you're not a ledger item to barter with."

"Everyone else seems to disagree."

He says it too lightly. There's no real humor left in it now, only a hollow where humor sits when it's been used too often to cover the same wound.

I lower myself back into the chair rather than backing away. Retreat would feel like conceding ground. The wood is solid under me, wonderfully, blessedly plain.

"Sit down," I tell him. "We're going to talk about the deaths in Sector Four."

He studies me for a long moment, weighing something. Threat level, perhaps. Or the likelihood that I'm lying, that this is some elaborate new ritual he hasn't learned the steps to yet.

Then, slowly, as if it costs him something, he walks around the table and takes the opposite chair.

He doesn't lounge in it. There's no room for that. It's a straight-backed, serviceable piece of furniture that forces you to put your spine where it belongs. He folds himself into it like someone remembering how to inhabit a shape they were flogged out of long ago.

His hand, around the stem of his goblet, is no longer performing. The fingers are white at the knuckles.

I open my notebook again.

"Three bodies," I say. "Two weeks. All within ten paces of the cistern walkway. Walk me through each patrol rotation when those deaths occurred."

He exhales through his nose, a sound halfway between annoyance and resignation.

"You want me to recite the week in order?" he asks.

"Yes."

"Darling, I barely remember what I had for breakfast."

"You don't eat breakfast."

That gets me a flicker of something almost like amusement.

"Ah. You read the feeding logs too. I'm touched."

"I'm thorough," I repeat. "Start with the first night."

He drains half his wine in one swallow before he speaks.

The story he tells me is, on the surface, perfectly acceptable. A Relic's night on patrol—with the edges filed down, humor lacquered over the worst parts.

I was where they said I should be. I followed the prescribed route. I killed what needed killing. I filed the requisite forms.

"And the intervals where you're not accounted for?" I ask, watching my own pen as it moves. "Twenty minutes unlogged on the second night. Forty-five on the third."

"Maintenance checks," he says smoothly. "The cistern mechanisms are temperamental. Someone has to make sure they don't flood the district in its sleep."

"That's not in your mandate."

"It's in no one's, hence the problem."

His gaze slides, unthinking, to one of the mirrors as he says it. He catches his own eye and looks away at once, jaw tightening.

"Do you often stay alone with unsecured god-blood?" I press.

"Does anyone else volunteer?" he counters.

"Cyran."

He shuts his eyes briefly. The knuckles of his free hand flex on the table before he flattens them, fingers splaying.

"Sometimes," he says, more carefully, "the Thirst is louder down there. If I don't take a moment to… center… I am less use to everyone involved."

"There are sanctioned feeding houses for that."

"With waiting lists, rules, and charming little sign-in sheets." His lip curls. "What I carry around in my veins is worth more to some people than the lives in my file. They'd bottle me if they could. Thessen certainly has tried."

There it is. A crack in the markup, a glimpse at the machinery beneath.

"He filed the first complaint," I say.

"Of course he did."

"He alleges unbecoming conduct in relation to donors visiting the Vein Markets."

He laughs again, but it's a shorter sound this time, stripped of earlier theatrics.

"Is that what we're calling them now," he says, "donors?"

"What would you call them?"

He considers. The candle flames gutter in a brief draft from the chimney. In the mirror to my right, I watch his reflection watch mine.

"Patrons," he says at last. "Owners. Guests. High bidders. Take your pick, Investigator. You have the vocabulary."

"Do you want them there?" I ask.

His eyes snap back to mine.

"That's not the question you're here to ask."

"It's the one I'm asking."

Silence opens out between us, as tangible as the carpet, as the heavy drapes muffling the city's creaks. My pulse is uncomfortably loud in my own throat.

He looks down at his hand around the glass, following the veins for a moment as if they're lines on a map. When he speaks, his voice has shed most of its theater.

"I want to live," he says. "Whatever configuration of company achieves that on any given night tends to be the one I tolerate."

"That's not the same as wanting them."

"That's not a luxury I often have," he replies.

I write the words down. Not verbatim; I'm not here to give High Inquisitor Voss more poetry to ignore. But the meaning goes onto the page, neutral ink that will dry into something stiff and real.

"Are you this strict with everyone you audit," he asks after a moment, "or just the monsters?"

"I don't audit monsters." My voice is calm. It always gets calmer when I'm angry. "I audit cases. And people who happen to be in the middle of them."

"You don't think I'm a monster?"

"I think the word does more work for the Tribunal than it does for the truth."

His mouth curves, not quite into a smile.

"Careful, Investigator Vaskin. Someone might hear you say that and revoke your ink."

I ignore the way he says my name. I ignore the small, sharp satisfaction it gives me that he remembers it.

"Tell me about the child," I say. "The one in the cistern tonight."

His fingers flinch against the glass.

Of course he thought I hadn't seen. He moved quickly, efficiently, placing himself between the boy and the god-spawn like a wall of silk and steel. But fear has a scent, and children leave traces. The smear of small fingers on the pump housing, the quick, jerky motion in my peripheral vision as we left the chamber.

"There was a boy," he concedes, after a beat. "He followed the screaming. I sent him home."

"Without filing an incident report."

"Would that have improved his night?"

"It would have put him on someone's watch-list."

"He's already on someone's." His voice goes flint-hard for the first time. "You don't get bruises like that on your wrists from playing with chalk sigils in the gutter."

The image rises, unwanted, in my mind: the pressure-mark I saw when the boy's shirt hitched; the way he flinched at Cyran's touch and then allowed it anyway because the alternative was the monster.

"Did you feed from him?" I ask.

He bares his teeth at me. It could be a smile if you didn't know better.

"No."

"Have you fed from any unregistered civilians in the last month?"

"No."

"Have you fed from any registered ones outside of sanctioned parameters?"

He stares.

"Do you want the truth," he asks softly, "or the version that keeps everything tidy for your donors?"

"I write what you do," I answer, equally soft. "Not how you feel about it."

We look at each other. Something like an understanding brushes past us and is gone.

He exhales, long and slow.

"I take what I am allowed," he says. "No more. Sometimes less, when I can get away with it. Does that satisfy your conscience, Investigator?"

"It informs my report," I say. "My conscience will wait."

We circle there for nearly an hour. Questions, answers, half-answers. His charm waxes and wanes; he reaches for it reflexively when a question gets too close, then drops it again when I don't rise to the bait. Somewhere around the forty-minute mark, he stops checking his reflection in the mirrors.

The room grows hotter. Candle stubs drown in their own wax; he replaces them without thinking, a practiced gesture. My water glass empties. I don't ask for more. Showing need here feels too much like opening a door.

By the time I close my notebook, the plate of fruit and cheese remains untouched. The fire has burned low, embers a dull red echo of the god-blood's glow far below the streets.

He watches me tuck the book back into my satchel, his gaze narrowed, assessing. Not the leering once-over of a man planning his next conquest; the measuring stare of someone who has just seen his favorite script torn up and is trying to decide whether the new play is more or less dangerous.

"Same time tomorrow?" he asks, voice light again but edged with something sharp. "I promise to be twice as forthcoming if you reconsider your stance on acceptable payment."

"Tomorrow we walk the patrol," I say, standing. My knees protest dully; the hard chair has left its honest imprint. "Sector Four. The nights of the deaths. I want to see exactly where you were and weren't."

He tilts his head.

"In this?" He plucks at his shirt-front theatrically. "I'd hate to ruin the silk."

"Wear something practical," I say. "If you own anything that meets the definition."

His smile this time is smaller. Almost real.

"I'll rummage in the kennel," he says. "See if they've left me a collar that goes with sensible boots."

I pick up my satchel. My fingers have left faint damp marks on the leather where they pressed too hard.

At the door, I pause.

He's still seated, but he's not lounging now; he's upright, hands resting on his knees, the goblet abandoned on the table with a rim of dark wine still clinging to its sides. Without the performance, he looks younger. Not in years, but in the way people do when you take their roles away and leave them only their own bewilderment.

"Cyran," I say.

He looks up at once.

"If anyone else in this building offers themselves to me as leverage," I tell him, "I'll note it as unbecoming conduct. For them, not you."

Something flashes across his face too quick to categorize. Hope? No. Relief? Not quite. An easing, perhaps, in a muscle I can't see.

"Goodnight, Investigator," he says.

"Goodnight."

The corridor outside is blessedly cold.

The door shuts behind me with a soft, precise click, cutting off the heat and the scent and the sight of candles and mirrors and a man who tried to turn himself back into currency because that is the only way he knows to survive scrutiny.

I walk down the narrow stair, one hand skimming the bone rail, counting the turns. On the landing, a neighbor's door cracks open; an older woman in a faded silk robe peers out, eyes bright with hungry curiosity.

"Relic keeping you late, Tribunal?" she asks, voice papery with smoke. "They say he's worth losing sleep over."

"Go back to bed," I say, without pausing. "He's off-duty tonight."

She laughs, a soft, knowing sound, and closes the door.

At the bottom of the stairs, the Ribcage air hits me in the face—damp, metallic, reeking faintly of blood and grease and the Vein Markets' overripe perfume. I step out into it like plunging into cold water, drawing a long breath I didn't realize I'd been rationing.

My pulse is still too high. My skin feels too hot where his fingers brushed it, where his voice curled along my neck. Under the fatigue, under the professional satisfaction of having held the line, desire curls slow and traitorous.

He is beautiful. That has nothing to do with the file and everything to do with the man who walked into his own quarters already knowing which performance this night demanded of him.

For one shamefully human moment, I wish his offer had been made by that man wanting something for himself, not by the weapon trying to bribe his way into safety.

The moment passes. Of course it passes. I have excellent reflexes.

Except.

The Ribcage air is cold on my face and I let it be cold, breathing through the last hour, cataloguing what I actually allowed myself to feel in there. And I find, at the bottom of the pile, something I can't quite file under professional. Something that sat up when he said *let's start with all of them* and did not immediately sit back down.

I press my thumb to my Tribunal mark. The ink is cold.

I don't wonder what I would have said. I already know I would have said no. I've always been good at the correct answer.

I'm considerably less comfortable with the pause I took to get there—that single beat where *no* was forming and I was still, somewhere underneath it, standing at a door I had no business being near.

Redemption is not owed. Exploitation is not permitted. Both things can be true at once.

Tomorrow night, I will walk his patrol in the dark and stand beside him where the god-blood hums loudest, and I will find out which of the two he truly is.

And I will write it down, exactly as it is, no matter what it costs either of us.
