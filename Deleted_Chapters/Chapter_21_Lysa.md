# Chapter 21 — *The Doctor in the Dungeons*
### Lysa | POV

The cell smells of mildew and despair, which are technically two different scents. 

Mildew is damp stone and stagnant water.

Despair is old sweat and the sour metal tang of people who stopped believing anyone would come for them.

My head throbs in a rhythm that doesn’t match the city’s veins. The last echo of the Bells still lives somewhere in my teeth—an ache that flares when I move too fast. My wrists burn where the bone cuffs bit in; now they’re gone, traded for iron manacles with prettier sigils and worse implications.

Voss took my badge.

He did not take my brain.

I roll onto my back on the narrow slab they’re calling a bed and stare up at the ceiling.

Vein Prison.

Deep under the city, under the Tribunal courts, under even the Vigil barracks. The place you send problems you don’t want walking around where donors might see them. I’ve signed transfer orders to this place. I’ve toured the intake once, as part of a training rotation, made neatly shocked noises at the conditions, promised in a written memo to advocate for reforms. 

I’m here now.

That’s one way to assess whether the reforms took.

Spoiler: they didn’t.

I sit up.

The cell is four paces by four, if I shorten my stride. I know this, because I’ve already paced it enough times to wear a groove in the stone. Door: iron, inset with Tribunal sigils designed to reject tampering and unauthorized keys. Window: high and narrow, a rib‑slit cut into the wall, barred with bone rods thick enough that even a Relic would have to strain. Floor: damp. My boots left prints when they hauled me in. 

No obvious weaknesses.

No obvious doesn’t mean none.

I swing my legs off the slab and stand.

My head protests.

I ignore it.

Panic is useless.

Assessment is not.

I go to the door.

Run my fingers over the sigils, feeling for sloppiness. A mis‑etched curve, a line not quite straight, a warder who cut corners at the end of a long shift. Nothing. Each glyph is precise, lines sharp, angles exact. I recognize the rune‑cluster for Containment, another for Silence, a third for Failsafe—if the lock is destroyed without the proper key, it will seal permanently, fusing the seam. 

Of course they reserve their best work for cages.

I flex my fingers.

They didn’t check my hair.

Voss was angry.

Angry people get sloppy.

I reach up into the tight knot at the back of my head.

The pin is where I left it: a slender length of disguised metal, Tribunal standard issue for female Auditors who might need to both hold their hair and crack a lock on field evidence. Officially, it’s a grooming tool. Unofficially, it’s a quiet acknowledgment that sometimes doors shut between you and the truth. 

My heart stutters once when my fingers close on it.

Small victory.

I pull it free.

My hair falls in a rough curtain around my face. My neck prickles—the part of me that still believes neat presentation equals authority flinches at the disarray. That part can get used to disappointment.

I kneel by the lock.

The pin slides into the keyhole smoothly.

It meets resistance immediately.

There’s a layer beyond the normal tumblers—a thin membrane of magic, humming just under my nails. Not enough to shock. Enough to tell me that whatever clever mechanical work I do here won’t matter unless I deactivate the sigils. 

Breaking Tribunal wards from the inside requires tools and time I don’t have.

I sit back on my heels.

Think.

The Ceremony is at sunset.

I don’t know exactly what Thessen will do to Cyran between now and then. I do know he enjoys the process as much as the result. 

Every minute I spend admiring this lock is a minute I’m not spending ruining his evening.

Footsteps echo down the corridor.

Not hurried.

Not heavy enough to be a full Guard squad.

I tuck the pin into my sleeve and get to my feet just as the little hatch at eye level scrapes open.

A young face peers in.

Guards look younger every year.

This one can’t be more than twenty—jaw still soft under the helmet strap, eyes wide in that combination of fear and self‑importance you see in fresh recruits.  

“Auditor Voss,” he says.

“Former,” I correct.

He swallows.

“Prisoner Voss,” he amends, like the words taste wrong.

“Yes?” I ask politely.

“I have questions,” he says. He holds up a slate as if that makes it more official. “For the record.”

“For which record?” I ask. “Internal disciplinary? Criminal? Donor complaint? There are different forms. I can recite the distinction codes, if you like.”

He blinks.

“This is… an initial interrogation,” he manages. “Regarding your involvement in the attempted theft of Temple property and the assault on Tribunal Guards.”

“Assault,” I repeat. “Interesting word choice. Did the form also include a section on unauthorized deployment of Temple sonic tech in a civilian area?” 

His gaze flickers.

“We are not discussing that,” he says.

“Because you didn’t log it,” I say. “Because you know as well as I do that unregistered weapons on a public street violate three separate Tribunal statutes. Four, if we classify Cyran as a civilian asset instead of Temple property.” 

I step closer to the hatch.

He leans back, instinctive.

I modulate my tone to something almost conversational.

“Write this down,” I say. “Subsection Twelve, Article Three: ‘Any deployment of experimental or divine‑adjacent weaponry within city limits must be accompanied by a signed authorization from the High Council and reviewed by an independent Auditor within twenty‑four hours.’” I tilt my head. “Did you see any independent Auditors on that street?”

His throat works.

“That’s… above my pay grade,” he says.

“Of course it is,” I reply. “That’s the point. You’re the one they’ll blame later, when the donors’ lawyers come sniffing for liability. ‘We had no idea our Guard would use such crude measures,’ they’ll say. ‘A regrettable excess of zeal from a junior officer.’” 

His fingers tighten on the slate.

“I’m just doing my job,” he mutters.

“So was I,” I say, quiet. “Until the job stopped matching the law.”

Silence stretches.

He seems very aware, suddenly, of the distance between us: one door, three sigils, and the weight of an institution he thought would always point in the right direction.

“I’ll… come back,” he says.

He slides the hatch shut with more haste than necessary.

His footsteps retreat.

I let out a slow breath.

My heart is still hammering from the echo of the Bells, but my mind feels clearer.

Words are still a weapon.

They just don’t hit the target I want fast enough.

I go back to pacing.

Four paces one way.

Turn.

Four paces back.

The drip of water in the corner keeps time. There’s a faint, irregular thud somewhere beyond my wall—distant doors, or the heartbeat of the god’s veins moving sluggishly around this oubliette.

I catalog, again, everything I know.

Vein Prison’s layout. Intake on Level One, processing on Two, long‑term cells and medical on Three. Old maps showed a connection between the medical wing and the under‑temple tunnels before the Tribunal “repurposed” this place. They may have sealed it. They rarely erase anything fully. 

My palm brushes the wall on the next turn.

It feels… different here. Cooler. Less damp.

I stop.

Press my ear to the stone.

Hear nothing but the drip.

“You’re pacing too loudly,” a voice says.

Not from the door.

From the wall.

I freeze.

The voice is male, pitched low and smooth. Each word is precise, as if he’s laying them out on a table with a gloved hand, deciding which one to dissect first. 

“Most people go through the first three stages in silence,” he continues. “Shock, denial, bargaining. You skipped straight to agitation. Interesting.”

My pulse jumps.

“Who’s there?” I ask.

“Oh, very good,” he says, amused. “Straight to interrogation. Efficient. I approve.”

I move along the wall, palms flat, until I find it.

A vent—no bigger than my hand, a rusted grate set into the stone a little above eye level. It’s old: not part of the Tribunal’s neat Containment aesthetic, but something that predates their occupation. Air moves faintly through it, cool and dry, smelling of disinfectant and old bone. 

I crouch.

Peer through the slats.

At first, all I see is dimness.

Then a strip of light as someone on the other side shifts.

A white coat.

Pristine, in a room that looks otherwise neglected: cracked tiles, rusted hooks, a table stained a deep, permanent brown. The man wearing it stands with his back half‑turned, hands moving in deft, economical motions as he arranges instruments on a tray. 

Not Tribunal cut.

Older.

Colder.

Dr. Marrow, I think, with a jolt.

I’ve read his reports.

The ones that exist, anyway.

Most of his work is redacted down to polite euphemisms: “experimental intervention,” “outcome under review,” “subject deceased—no further data.” The Vigil forwards him their… problems. The Tribunal pretends not to know.

“Dr. Marrow?” I venture.

He pauses.

Looks over his shoulder.

I don’t see his face fully.

Just the edge of a profile: pale skin, sharp cheekbone, dark hair pulled back too neatly for a prison doctor, a glint of something like amusement in his eye. 

“And you,” he says, “must be the Auditor who blew up the Gala.”

I blink.

“Is that really the headline they went with?” I ask.

He chuckles.

“A working title,” he says. “Your little bone‑storm in the donor hall upset my schedule. I had three potential subjects on that guest list.” 

“I’ll try to be more considerate next time,” I say dryly.

“Please don’t,” he replies. “It was… invigorating.”

He turns more fully toward the vent.

Still in shadow.

Up close, his white coat is immaculate. Not a stain out of place. The instruments on his tray gleam: bone saw, scalpels, clamps. Beside them, a neat row of glass vials, each filled with something clear, something viscous, something that catches the light like captured veins. 

“I need out,” I say.

“I’m sure you do,” he answers. “That’s hardly a unique desire in this facility.”

“I have to get to the Temple before sunset,” I say, stepping closer to the grate. “A donor is about to turn a Relic into a public puppet. With Tribunal blessing.”

“You phrase that as if it’s a bad thing,” he muses.

“It’s an illegal thing,” I say. “Abuse disguised as ritual. You’d know something about that.”

He inclines his head, unbothered.

“Flattery will get you everywhere,” he says. “But not out of that cell. Why should I help you?”

Because Cyran is out there alone.

Because Thessen has six words and a schedule and no one standing between him and the altar.

Because I am so, so tired of watching this system eat the people it’s supposed to redeem.

“Because Voss is using unregistered sonic tech,” I say. “Temple‑grade. On the street. I saw the schematics. I heard the frequency. You want data. I have it.” 

He goes very still.

Ah.

There it is.

Curiosity.

“My, my,” he says. “They gave the Guards the toys before they gave them to me? How rude.” He steps closer, until his voice is almost in my ear through the grate. “Tell me.”

I exhale slowly.

“Quadruple‑nested resonance bells,” I say. “Brass casings, bone cores etched with pulse‑sigils. The primary frequency was… two hundred and twenty hertz, modulated with a high‑band overlay around eighteen kilohertz to target enhanced audioreceptors.” 

He hums, low and approving.

“And the damping range?” he asks. “How far before the wave decayed?”

“Two city blocks,” I say. “Based on the visible effects and the echo. Strongest within ten meters. After that, diminishing. It destabilized a Relic’s equilibrium almost instantly.”

“A Relic,” he repeats. “Which one?”

“Subject Nine,” I say.

He laughs.

“Oh, delightful,” he says. “We’ve come full circle. My predecessor would be tickled.”

Anger flares, sharp.

“Subject Nine has a name,” I say evenly. “Cyran.”

“Names are social constructs,” he says. “Numbers are data. But very well. Cyran. Did he bleed from the ears?”

“Yes,” I say, resisting the urge to dig my fingers into the stone hard enough to crack it. “Nose, too. Motor control impaired, but not eliminated. He was still trying to stand when they bagged him.” 

A small, satisfied sound.

“Their implementation is crude,” Marrow says. “But promising. I’ve been petitioning for access to Temple acoustics for months. Voss has been very stingy.”

“You help me,” I say, “I’ll write in my report that you should be given full access to the bells.”

“I don’t care about your reports,” he says, almost gently. “But I do care about frequencies. And about seeing what happens when someone uses them on a god’s heart.” He taps the wall lightly, once. “Acceptable terms. Hold out your hand.”

I blink.

“Excuse me?” I say.

“Through the grate,” he clarifies. “Unless Vein Prison has changed since this morning, I believe your cell geometry still allows for it.”

Carefully, I slide my fingers through the narrow gaps.

They scrape; the stone bites at my knuckles.

Something cool and smooth touches my skin.

“Mind the glass,” he says.

I close my hand around a small vial.

It’s stoppered with wax, the glass thick, the liquid inside a clear, faintly viscous shimmer that clings to the sides when I tilt it. 

“What is it?” I ask.

“Corrosive,” he says. “A little hobby of mine. It will eat most alloys, particularly those used in Tribunal locks. It will also eat your flesh, if you’re careless. Do try not to get it on your fingers.”

“Noted,” I say.

“And Lysa?” he adds.

Hearing my name in his mouth sends a prickle down my spine.

“Yes,” I say.

“Don’t die,” he says. “You’re interesting. It would be a waste of a good data set.”

Warmth does not reach his tone.

It doesn’t need to.

Interest is all he has to offer.

It’s enough, for now.

“Thank you,” I say.

“You’re quite welcome,” he replies. “Do hurry. I’d like to see if Thessen’s spectacle lives up to the advertising.”

The sound of his footsteps recedes.

I’m alone again.

The vial sits heavy and cold in my hand.

I tuck it carefully into the crook of my arm as I climb to my feet. My fingers shake once, then steady.

Door.

Again.

This time, I don’t bother with the hairpin.

I wedge the vial’s mouth against the seam where the lock meets the door, brace it with my other hand, and snap the wax seal off with my thumb.

The smell hits me first.

Sharp, metallic, like someone dissolved pennies in citrus and lightning. 

I tip.

Liquid kisses iron.

It hisses.

Smoke blooms up in a pale, acrid cloud.

I turn my face away, squinting as the vapor curls around my head. The air tastes like acid and old blood. The sigils on the lock flare once, outlines blazing in angry red, then stutter, their precise lines running like ink in the rain. 

The metal darkens.

Bubbles.

Liquefies.

It takes less than a minute.

Marrow doesn’t waste reagents.

When the vial is empty, the lock is a misshapen, oozing wound in the door. The smell is worse now—burnt metal, something almost organic in the undertone, like overcooked meat.

I set the empty vial carefully on the floor.

Wrap my sleeve around my hand.

Push.

For a moment, nothing happens.

Then the warped metal gives with a wet squelch, the bolt shearing through its own softened housing. The door shudders.

Creaks.

Swings inward a handspan.

My heart does something painful behind my ribs.

I slide through the gap.

The corridor outside is narrow, low‑ceilinged, lit by a single marrow‑lamp at the far end. Cells line one side, doors all shut, small hatches dark. On the other side, the stone wall is bare save for the occasional drip of moisture. 

No Guards in sight.

Not immediately.

They grow complacent on Level Three.

No one gets out of these cells, after all.

I move on bare feet; they took my boots.

The stone is cold under my soles.

I pass three doors, four, ears straining for any scrape, any shift. Someone coughs behind one. Someone else mutters a prayer in a language I don’t recognize. Chains clink faintly.

At the corner, I pause.

Peep.

A Guard sits on a stool half‑turned away, back braced against the wall, helm tipped forward just enough that I can’t tell if his eyes are closed. His spear leans against his shoulder, point wobbling with each slow breath. 

He is between me and the stairs up.

Of course he is.

I scan my surroundings.

No convenient bricks.

No abandoned weapons.

Just damp, bone, and my own two hands.

I pick up the empty vial.

It’s glass. Weighty. The edges where the acid ate at the mouth are rough.

I weigh it in my palm once.

Then I step around the corner.

“Excuse me,” I say.

He jerks.

In his credit, he’s fast.

His hand slaps for the spear, his other going to the whistle at his neck.

I throw.

The vial cracks against his temple with a wet, unpleasant sound.

Glass shatters.

He slumps sideways off the stool, spear clattering, whistle never making it to his lips.

I wince.

Advance.

Check for breath.

Still there.

Skull intact enough.

He’ll have a headache, not a funeral.

I strip his belt one‑handed—keys, a short truncheon, a knife that’s more decorative than practical but better than nothing. The keys might help if more locks aren’t eaten by Marrow’s pet acid. 

I spare a second to pull him fully into the shadow of the wall.

It won’t buy me much time, but every bit counts.

Then I run.

Stairs up.

One flight, two.

The smell shifts as I go: less mildew, more dust, the faint whisper of air that hasn’t filtered through stone for days. Voices, distant but distinct, float down—a Guard laughing, the clank of a bowl on metal.

Level Two.

Processing.

Too many people.

I don’t slow.

Not yet.

I need daylight.

On Level One, the light changes.

No longer the sickly, marrow‑lamp glow, but slats of honest sun knifing in through grates high up. The main corridor stretches ahead, leading to the intake hall. 

I duck into a side passage before anyone can see me from the desk at the far end.

Empty.

Storage rooms line this one—doors ajar, shelves stacked with confiscated belongings, bundled records, old armor.

Boots.

Blessed, glorious boots.

I grab the first pair that looks close to my size.

They’re a little loose, but better than bare feet on god‑bone.

I shove my hair back, twist it into a knot as tight as my fingers can manage without a pin, and scan the shelves again.

Coats.

A plain, dark one. Not Tribunal robe, not Guard livery. Civilian. Forgotten or seized, it doesn’t matter. I shrug into it. It hangs a little long, but it hides the worst of the prison grime, the fact that I’m not where I’m supposed to be.

Keys on my belt.

Knife at my hip.

Heartbeat in my throat.

Cyran in a Temple carriage, bag over his head.

I step back into the corridor.

Voices are louder now from the intake hall.

“—said the Auditor went mad. Tried to stab a donor.”

“Wouldn’t you? Those people—”

“Shut up, they’ll hear—”

I walk.

Not stealthy.

Not rushing.

Just a woman with a coat and a purpose.

At the corner, I pause long enough to gauge the room.

The intake hall is as I remember it: a counter, a ledger, a rack of shackles on the wall, a door to the outside watched by two bored Guards. Another Guard sits at the desk, quill in hand, recording the day’s miseries. 

None of them look at me twice.

Why would they?

People go out as often as they come in—clerks, medics, auditors, functionaries. The coat helps. So does the way I hold myself: spine straight, shoulders back, authority worn like a familiar cloak instead of a stolen one. 

I walk past the desk.

One of the door Guards glances at me, then at my neck.

No badge.

His brow furrows.

“Excuse me,” he says.

I smile.

“Late on my rounds,” I say briskly. “Prisoner transfer was… unexpectedly exciting. I still have to file a report before the High Inquisitor’s evening session. Do you want to explain to him why his paperwork is delayed?” 

He pales.

“Ah—no, ma’am,” he stammers. “Of course. Carry on.”

“Thank you,” I say.

I don’t look back.

The door is heavy bone, carved with the Tribunal’s seal.

It opens onto stairs up to street level.

I take them two at a time.

The Vein’s chill gives way to the city’s heat.

When I push out into the daylight, the light hits like a slap.

The sky is a bleached, washed‑out blue above the bone‑buildings, the sun sliding inexorably toward the line of ribs that will frame the Heart Temple tonight. 

Time is shorter than it feels.

I draw one deep breath.

The air up here smells of dust and cooking fat and a hundred mundane lives.

Under it, if I’m very still, I can almost taste the god’s heart preparing itself for the Pulse—a faint, anticipatory tremor through the stone.

I think of Cyran’s eyes, glassy with pain under the bag.

Of his voice in the alley: I’d rather be a man at your side than a god alone. 

Of the red words in the Archive, burning.

Obedience is the only Virtue.

No.

I step into the street.

I am no longer an Auditor.

No badge.

No rank.

No official authority.

What I have instead is a vial’s worth of stolen chemistry on my skin, an illegal escape behind me, a sociopath’s curiosity at my back, and a man I love waiting at an altar he did not choose.

That will have to be enough.

I start toward the Heart.

High up on the back wall, barely more than a slit. Barred with thick, vertical bones. A poor excuse for air and light. No view. Just a slice of stone corridor beyond, and more bone. Vein Prison is underground, coiled in the god’s lower ribs like a parasite.

“Who’s there?” I ask, turning toward the sound.

Silence.

The wall opposite the door is uneven, a bulge of older bone in the mortar, crisscrossed with hairline cracks. Near the floor, a rectangular patch of stone is a slightly different shade, like someone filled in an old vent and did a passable job of pretending they didn’t.

I crouch.

Press my palm to it.

Cool, but not as cold as the rest of the wall.

“Who’s there?” I repeat, lower now.

A soft sigh answers me. Not exasperated—more like a man forced to delay an interesting experiment for small talk.

Male, I think.

Educated.

Bored.

“Prisoners, yes,” he says. “They’re very loud. You’re different. Your footsteps have a pattern. Four paces, turn, four paces, turn. It’s irritatingly regular. I counted nineteen circuits before I decided you were not going to tire yourself out.”

My lips twitch.

It’s not kind.

Not cruel, either. More… delighted. Like he’s just discovered a new instrument on his workbench.

“People who think rules matter if you recite them loudly enough,” he says. “Do they?”

“Sometimes,” I say. “Sometimes they’re chains. Sometimes they’re scalpels. Occasionally, if you’re very stubborn, you can turn them into keys.”

“Oh, excellent,” he says. “Metaphor. It’s going to be a good afternoon.”

I press my ear to the patched stone.

“Who are you?” I ask again.

There’s a rustle on the other side. Fabric, not chains. Papers shuffled. A drawer sliding.

“I might ask the same,” he says. “But I already know. You’re the Auditor who upset such a very expensive party.”

Gala.

Word travels fast underground.

“And you’re the doctor,” I say slowly. “Assigned to the Vein medical block. Marrow.”

A pause.

Then, lightly, “Doctor Marrow, if we’re being formal. Though decorum feels wasted down here.”

My blood goes a shade colder.

I’ve read his reports.

Clinical notes turned in to the Tribunal with immaculate handwriting and no visible emotion, full of phrases like subject expired and bone harvest successful. Rumors filtered up with them: about a man in a white coat who treats prisoners like puzzles and gods’ leftovers like clay.

“What are you doing in a place like this?” I ask. “Shouldn’t a doctor of your reputation be in a clean laboratory somewhere, not… wherever that is?”

“Research,” he says. “The Vein offers such a wide variety of specimens. Mortals, immortals, something in between. Systems under stress. It’s fascinating.”

“I’m thrilled my incarceration is improving your publication prospects,” I say.

“Publication is vanity,” he replies, amused. “Data is the point.”

He lets the word hang.

Data.

Information.

Currency.

I sit back on my heels.

“I need out,” I say.

“Of course you do,” he says. “Everyone does. You’ll have to be more specific if you want my professional assistance.”

Professional.

Not altruistic.

“Voss used unregistered Temple tech in the middle of the street,” I say. “Sonic weapons. Bells on staves.”

There’s a small, sharp pause.

“Describe it,” he says.

Not a question.

An imperative wrapped in silk.

“I thought you didn’t do interrogations,” I say.

“I don’t,” he says. “This is consultation. There’s a difference.”

My heartbeat ticks up.

Every minute here is a minute closer to sunset.

But if he cares about the bells, that’s leverage. The Maestro was right: knowledge is the only currency anyone respects in this city. Blood just oils the gears.

“They were brass,” I say. “About this big.” I sketch the curve in the air out of habit. “Mounted on bone staffs. Temple sigils around the rim. Activated with a mallet.”

“Crude,” he murmurs. “And the effect?”

“Wide‑angle concussive wave,” I say. “Not directional. Enough to rattle bones. It hit everyone, but—”

“The Relic,” he supplies. “Your vampire. It affected him more.”

I grind my teeth.

“Yes,” I say. “He dropped screaming. Blood from the ears. Loss of motor control.”

“Hm,” he says thoughtfully. “So they finally tried to weaponize the harmonic resonance in a vampiric auditory system. I did suggest that in a memo, oh, twenty years ago. They sent me a very rude reply about ethics.” A soft, disdainful exhale. “How charming that they’ve come round.”

I swallow.

“You drafted the theory,” I say.

“Please,” he says. “No one in the Temple reads my work. They prefer miracles to math. But Voss—that’s different. He likes shortcuts. What was the frequency?”

“You assume they told me.”

“I assume you looked,” he says. “You seem like the sort who reads fine print even while bleeding.”

He’s not wrong.

My mind throws up the image of the bell as it swung up, light glinting off the carved sigils. Numbers banded subtly along the inside where only a close observer would notice. Old Temple habit: sanctifying a device by inscribing the ratio it relies on into its flesh.

“Four hundred hertz base,” I say slowly. “Boosted to…” I close my eyes, visualize it, the way the sigil lines crossed. “Four hundred eighty in the second harmonic. Layered with… I think it was a minor third above to create dissonance. And there was a subsonic pulse. Low enough I felt it more than heard it. Fifty‑five, maybe fifty‑six.”

Silence.

Then a low, pleased hum that brushes against the edge of a purr.

“Acceptable,” he says.

“That’s not exactly a peer review,” I say.

“It’s better,” he answers. “It’s interesting. Which brings us back to your original request. You need out. I need details. I’ve had my appetizer. What are you willing to trade for the main course?”

“An ethics lecture?” I suggest.

He chuckles.

“I do enjoy a spirited debate,” he says. “But time appears to be of the essence, and I find I want to see how your little drama plays out. Curiosity is such a dangerous indulgence.” Fabric rustles again. Glass clinks. “Fortunately for you, I indulge often.”

Something thin and cylindrical slides through the lower edge of the patched stone.

A section of mortar has crumbled more than I realized, leaving a finger‑wide gap along the floor. A small glass vial noses its way through, as if shoved by an invisible hand.

I catch it before it rolls far.

It’s clear, stoppered with wax, half‑filled with a viscous, faintly luminescent liquid that clings to the glass when I tilt it. It smells even through the seal: sharp, metallic‑citrus, like the acid I signed out from field supply a dozen times, but… stronger.

“What is it?” I ask.

“Corrosive,” he says. “An old Temple blend we were allowed to improve upon. It will eat iron and most warding sigils. Don’t get it on your fingers unless you’d like to help me measure the rate of necrosis in real time.”

The part of me that takes notes on everything files away the phrase old Temple blend we were allowed to improve upon for later.

The rest of me is already on my feet.

“Why?” I ask.

“Manners,” he chides. “The word you’re looking for is thank you.”

“Why help me,” I clarify, “when the people who sign your requisition forms will be very upset if I leave?”

“Because I’m bored,” he says simply. “And because Voss using my old theories without attribution offends me. And because I should like to see what happens when someone like you is loosed upon the Heart with nothing left to lose.” A pause. “Don’t die too quickly, Auditor. You’re the most interesting thing that’s happened to this block in months.”

The drip of water fills the space where my heartbeat used to be.

“Noted,” I say.

“And Lysa?” he adds.

The use of my name makes the skin along my spine prickle.

“Yes,” I say.

“If you survive this,” he says, “I’d like to interview your vampire. His nervous system must be fascinating.”

“I’ll add you to our list of terrible future commitments,” I say dryly.

“Delightful,” he murmurs. “Do hurry. The Great Pulse waits for no one.”

His presence withdraws.

The slight warmth through the stone fades, leaving only damp cool and the faint echo of my own breathing.

I stare at the vial.

I think of Cyran on the street, clutching his ears, blood leaking between his fingers.

I think of the bag over his head.

I think of Thessen’s small, satisfied smile.

I bite the cork out with my teeth, careful not to let it splash.

The smell hits like a slap.

Sharp, acrid, like citrus left to rot with metal shavings.

My eyes water.

I swallow down a cough, wrap the mouth of the vial in my sleeve to shield my skin, and approach the door.

The lock is a squat, ugly thing: iron reinforced with bone, sigils carved deep. I pour slowly, letting the liquid run along the seam where metal hugs metal.

It hisses on contact.

For a moment, nothing visible happens.

Then the line of acid goes from clear to black.

The metal beneath it bubbles.

The sigils flare violently, trying to throw up a ward, then gutter as the strokes that define them are eaten away. Smoke coils up in thin, bitter threads. The smell shifts from sharp‑citrus to burnt blood and old coins.

I step back, shielding my mouth and nose with the crook of my elbow.

The hiss becomes a steady, hungry sizzle.

My mind, traitor that it is, notes the rate of dissolution, the thickness of the lock, the volume of reagent, the efficiency of Temple chemistry when someone like Marrow is allowed to play without a conscience standing over his shoulder.

I count under my breath.

At forty‑five, the sound changes.

The sizzle softens, giving way to an occasional drip as metal, now liquid, runs down and patters onto the stone.

I test the lock with the toe of my boot.

It swings inward.

The metal around it has melted into a warped oval, edges jagged but soft enough that they smear under the impact.

I push the door.

It moves.

Slowly, but it moves.

Wards crackle, fizzle, and die as the sigils that fed them wash away.

Beyond, the corridor is dim—a narrow vein of stone lit by a single hanging lantern that swings slightly in a draft. Cells march along one side, doors like mine. On the other, the wall is solid until it curves out of sight.

Down at the far end, someone has left a stool under the lantern.

It’s empty.

Good.

There will be a patrol, though. There’s always a patrol.

I slip out, keeping to the shadow hugging the wall.

Every sound seems magnified: the whisper of my boots, the tiny clink of still‑soft metal flaking off the ruined lock, the drip of water, the distant murmur of someone crying two cells down.

“Hey,” a hoarse voice whispers from the nearest door. “You. They let you out?”

I glance toward the slot.

A pair of eyes peers back. Bloodshot. Young.

“No,” I say. “They didn’t.”

“You’re—”

“Quiet,” I cut in. “And move away from the door.”

He does.

I hear the shuffle of feet on stone.

Guilt claws at me.

I could do this to every lock in the corridor, in theory. One vial, many doors. Marrow gave me enough for more than one.

But every door I open multiplies noise, chaos, variables.

I am not here to reform the Vein Prison in one afternoon.

I am here to stop a weapon from being turned on the city.

“Please—” someone farther down starts.

I shut my eyes for a heartbeat.

“I’ll come back,” I lie.

Then I start down the corridor.

Halfway to the lantern, I hear it: the dull thud of boots from the far curve.

One pair.

Lazy rhythm.

A guard on routine, not yet alerted.

I move faster, keeping low.

There’s no time to get back to my cell and pretend nothing happened.

No time to hide.

I glance at the lantern, at the stool.

Option one: wait until he rounds the corner, hope he’s as inattentive as his footfalls suggest, and ambush him.

Option two: try to talk.

Negotiation rarely works on people whose entire sense of self is tied up in the weight of their keys.

Option one, then.

I slip up beside the lantern, press my back to the wall, just out of the pool of light.

The boots come closer.

A man emerges from the curve, humming tunelessly under his breath.

Mid‑thirties, maybe. Vein uniform—boneplate over dull cloth, truncheon at his hip, key‑ring on his belt. He’s scratching at his jaw with one hand, the other loosely wrapped around the lantern chain as he passes, giving it an idle nudge.

He doesn’t look at the cells.

Rote.

Bored.

He passes me.

I step out.

Hook a hand into his collar, yank him backward. At the same time, I swing my weight, slamming his head into the wall with a precise, ugly thunk. The sound vibrates up my arm.

He grunts.

Drops.

I catch him on the way down so he doesn’t hit the floor as hard, lowering him into a sprawl.

Mercy, habit‑deep even now.

He’s out cold.

I check his pulse.

Steady.

I pry his keys and truncheon from his belt.

The keys jangle; I flinch at the noise and stuff them into my pocket, fingers wrapped tight to muffle them.

His face is slack, eyelids fluttering faintly, a bruise already rising at his temple.

He’s some clerk’s son who thought Vein Guard duty would be an easy step toward a pension. His report will say he was assaulted by a dangerous traitor. They’ll give him sympathy and better training.

I don’t have time to feel bad for him.

“Sorry,” I murmur anyway.

Then I run.

The corridor branches twice.

I pick the way that slopes upward, lungs weeping against ribcage, every breath tasting of stone and old sweat. Vein Prison is a labyrinth, but like all bureaucratic structures, its chaos has a pattern. Intake toward the front. Holding cells near the ground. Medical and… other facilities down.

Up is administrative.

Up has doors that lead to streets.

Up has light.

Stairs appear, narrow and steep, carved straight into the bone.

I take them two at a time until my thighs burn.

At the top, another corridor. Another door. This one is heavier, reinforced with more sigils, but it has a keyhole. My stolen ring fits. Third key I try turns smoothly.

I ease the door open a crack.

Light knifes through, warm and bright compared to the prison’s dull glow.

Sound spills in: street noise, distant carts, a hawker’s shout. The world has not noticed the absence of one Investigator and one Relic.

Good.

I slip through.

The Vein’s main hall yawns ahead of me: a long, high‑ceilinged chamber lined with benches and clerk desks, its far end opening onto a set of broad stairs that lead up to the street. A few functionaries move between the desks, quills and ledgers in hand. A Guard stands at the main door, speartip resting on the floor, gaze turned outward.

No one is looking back here.

They assume the doors behind them only open inward, under supervision.

I straighten my stolen truncheon at my hip, smooth my hair back, and walk.

Not skulking.

Not sprinting.

Just another official in a borrowed shadow, crossing from one duty to another.

My heart hammers against my ribs hard enough to ache.

My palms are damp against the keys in my pocket.

No one stops me.

One clerk glances up, eyes sliding over the iron at my belt, my plain tunic, the lack of badge. She frowns, opens her mouth.

I meet her gaze.

“Transfer from lower block,” I say, voice clipped, annoyed. “They’re shouting for you about a lost form.”

Instant panic.

She blanches.

“Which—”

“Intake,” I say.

She goes white and bolts for the side door, skirts swishing.

I keep walking.

The Guard at the front spares me a glance, then looks past me to the street beyond.

I walk right by him, every nerve screaming, and step out into the afternoon.

The city hits like a wave.

Noise. Light. Smell.

The sun has tilted; the ribs cast longer shadows now. The air tastes of dust, roasting marrow, incense from some distant shrine. Over it all, a bassline hum: the dead heart starting to gather itself for the Great Pulse.

I stand on the Vein’s steps for a single breath.

The urge to collapse there, to laugh or cry or retch, is strong.

No.

Later.

I turn toward the Heart Temple.

It rises at the city’s center, a column of fused bone and scar tissue curling protectively around the massive, petrified organ at its core. Today, banners hang from its balconies, donor colors fluttering. Carts roll toward it bearing offerings, attendees, last‑minute decorations to make divine torture look like charity.

My feet move.

Fast.

The truncheon at my hip bumps my thigh in a steady rhythm.

Every step away from the Vein is a step deeper into open treason. I have no badge. No writ. No authority but my own.

Good.

The system made its choice.

Now I get to make mine.
