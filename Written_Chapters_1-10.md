
# Chapter 1 — *The Blood Relic's tithe*
### Cyran | POV

I stand on a rib. Not a metaphorical one, though the city does love those. A real, arched fragment of god-bone, curving over the street like the broken cage of some titanic chest, slick with condensation and the greasy sheen of marrow vapor. The wind—what little of it can be bothered to stir at this hour—slides over my face, cool and damp, bringing with it the distant echo of market bells, the murmur of sleepless sinners, and underneath it all, that low, constant creak of settling bones that means the Reliquary hasn’t yet decided to collapse in on itself and end our collective misery.

My boots are already ruined.

I tip my weight forward on the balls of my feet, leather armor creaking softly, and peer down into the fog-choked artery of the street below. Lamps burn low, swallowed in the marrow-haze so that everything glows faintly red and green, as if the city is holding its breath beneath stained glass. Somewhere out there in the murk, a feral god-spawn is chewing its way through the late-night population like a child loose in a confectioner’s.

My goal is elegant in its simplicity: find the beast, kill it before it kills anyone else, and make sure the right people notice so they carve a few more Merit Marks into my ledger instead of my hide. Freedom by inches, paid in blood and performance.

If I were a better monster, I wouldn’t enjoy this part so much.

A shriek knifes through the night—high, human, and far enough away that if I misjudge the distance, I’ll be peeling what’s left of them off the cobbles later. My jaw tightens before my thoughts catch up, body already shifting, weight gathering. The sound ricochets around the ribcage arches, but there’s a clarity to terror if you know how to listen.

Left. Two streets down, one level lower. Near the cistern access. Of course.

“Lazy thing,” I murmur to no one, straightening the line of my cuff where a scuff of grime mars the black leather. “Couldn’t have picked somewhere with better lighting?”​

The shriek cuts off.

That’s the clock starting.

I step off the rib.

For one suspended heartbeat there’s only air, damp and cold against my face, my coat flaring behind me like a curtain at the close of a show. Then my boots kiss bone again as I land on the sloped roof of a calcified townhouse, knees bending to take the force, fingers brushing stone for balance. Not a sound, not even the clatter of loosened grit. The Vigil likes its Relics graceful. Showy. If we must be monsters, we are at least expensive ones.

The mist licks at my cheeks as I run, low and fast along the roofline, shadows slipping past shadows. Rot rides the air first—a sweet-sour stench of old garbage and meat left too long at the altars. Then ozone, sharp and wrong, like lightning trapped in a jar. And underneath that, the bright, thin tang of fresh fear-sweat.​

Ah. There you are.

I angle my path toward it, boots finding the familiar rhythm of patrol along the ribcage’s spine: leap, land, slide, push off another spur of bone. Below, the street curls between towering arcs of rib, their inner curves strung with bioluminescent moss that throbs a dull, exhausted green. The light paints everyone who passes beneath it in the same sickly hue, no matter their sins. Convenient.

As I vault a narrow gap between structures—a vertebra-bridge, worn smooth by centuries of carts—my gaze snags on a shrine carved into the crook of a rib. The dead god’s face is stylized, all hollow eyes and bleeding sigils, mouth open in an eternal, silent command. Someone’s tucked a wilted bouquet of vein-flowers into its jaw, their petals bruised to black.​

I land beside it and, on impulse, lift two fingers to my lips, then tap the statue’s stone cheek in a parody of piety. “Your child’s misbehaving,” I murmur. “I’ll handle it.”

The mist shifts. A shape—too many limbs, too much joint where there should be smooth curve—skitters along the wall below and vanishes into the yawning circle of a cistern hatch.​

“Of course,” I sigh. “Sewers. My boots thank you.”

I drop.

The hatch rim is slick with grease and something that shines too darkly to be normal water. My gloved fingers bite into bone as I swing down, then release, letting myself slide feet-first into the tunnel. The world narrows to wet stone scraping my shoulders and the rush of air past my ears, the chute angling sharply until gravity does most of the work. The temperature rises by slow degrees, cold damp giving way to a cloying, humid warmth that settles under my armor like another skin.

The smell hits me halfway down.

Not the thin, bright ribbon of mortal blood—though that’s here too, smeared along the wall as if someone’s hand dragged in passing—but the thicker, older scent that lives down in the bones of this place. Iron and salt and something electric that buzzes against my tongue when I breathe through my mouth.

God-blood.

What’s left of the Hollow Titan’s heart congealed in cisterns and pools beneath the district, still pulsing on some ancient, stubborn rhythm that has nothing to do with living bodies. The cisterns are supposed to be sealed, monitored, taxed in whispers and fumes by the Contract Lords. They tithe power like everything else in this city—every prayer, every execution, every intimate little signature meant to feed the Prime Contract sleeping under our laws.​

I’m not meant to be here unsupervised.

The chute spits me out onto a slick stone ledge, boots skidding, arms briefly pinwheeling. I catch myself on a pillar, fingers digging into ancient chisel marks, and push upright. The chamber opens around me, vast and echoing. The ceiling disappears into shadow; the floor is more suggestion than certainty, broken by great, circular pools where the Titan’s blood has collected in slow, viscous lakes.

They glow. Faintly, but enough—a sullen, dark red that throbs in time with a heartbeat I can feel in the backs of my teeth.

I swallow. My own hunger stirs, lazy and interested, stretching like a cat before deciding whether the prey is worth the effort. The Thirst is never gone; it’s simply polite enough most nights to stay in the corner of the room, pretending it isn’t watching me.

Tonight, it leans forward.

“Well,” I say, voice sounding a touch too bright even to my own ears. “Isn’t this festive.”

A splash answers me from the far side of the nearest pool. Heavy. Intentional.

My hands know what to do while the rest of me prevaricates. They move to my thighs, fingers unbuckling the straps that hold my daggers in place. Bone hilts—polished smooth with use, carved with inlaid sigils that lick cold against my palms when I draw them free. Twin blades, perfectly balanced, edges catching the bloodlight in clean, pale arcs. I spin them once, more for the feel than the spectacle; even alone, I prefer to give myself a show worth watching.

Instead of creeping, I start humming. An aria I can’t quite forget, all long, tragic notes and foolish lovers. The sound threads through pillars and pump mechanisms, bounces off stone, announces me. If it’s thinking about ambush, it can think again.

Something skitters around a column ahead, claws scratching stone. I get a brief flash of movement: jointed limbs flexing too far, plates of bone where skin should be, a smear of black ichor along the path. Then it’s gone again, circling.

“You’ve been busy,” I call, stepping onto the narrow lip of stone that runs along the pool. The blood below glows dully, close enough that if I tilted a hand I could trail my fingers through it. I don’t. “Citizens are filing complaints. Noise, property damage, partial consumption.”

I pick my steps with care. The stone is treacherous, every surface filmed with condensation and bone-dust. I keep my weight forward and light, ready to shift. My eyes track the ripple of shadows ahead. The Thirst paces quietly, content for now to watch.

The creature shows itself at the edge of the light.

It unfolds out of the dark, too many legs splayed wide to brace its bulk between two pillars. Carapace slick and patchy, chunks of mismatched bone pressed into raw, pulsing flesh. Its head can’t decide on a shape—two jaws trying to occupy the same space, teeth jutting inward and outward at once, dripping slime. Four eyes, then six, then I stop counting, because they all land on me.

The smell of sulfur hits with its breath. My eyes water.

“Oh, that’s unfortunate,” I say. “Who let you out half-finished?”

It screams at me, a sound like boiling kettles and metal scraped over glass. The vibration shivers down my spine. Then it lunges.

Claws slam into the walkway where I’d been standing. Stone explodes into shards that ping off my armor. I’m already sideways, weight flipped, left foot planting, right heel sliding. The swipe hisses past my chest close enough that one hooked talon scrapes my coat.

Fast. Sloppy. All appetite, no finesse.

We circle each other on the thin run of stone. It clacks and hisses, limbs scrabbling for purchase. I keep sliding just out of reach, small shifts of hips and shoulders turning near-misses into air. Each pass, my blades test a different angle—nick at a joint there, slice across the soft tissue under a plate here. Black ichor streaks its flanks, hissing where it drips into the god-blood.

It shudders, shakes droplets everywhere. One spatters my cheek, viscous and hot. I feel the sting of acid start and fade as skin knits under it.

The beast rears higher, limbs spread to either side for balance, and brings both front claws down like hammers. The impact cracks a pillar. Dust and bits of bone filter from above, sprinkling the pool and my shoulders in fine white grit.

“Careful,” I say between notes of the aria, dancing back. “That’s load-bearing.”

It doesn’t appreciate the joke. Its next swipe comes in lower, a scything arc toward my knees. I jump, boots hitting the raised edge of a maintenance platform, stone slick underfoot. My shoulders slam into a rusted pipe. The monster’s claws screech along metal, sparks spitting.

The pipe judders. Old bolts groan. A trickle of something thin and cold leaks from a seam and runs down the back of my neck.

“Absolutely not,” I mutter, shivering.

I push off the pipe and drop back to the walkway as the creature recovers. It’s breathing harder now, sides heaving unevenly where I’ve cut into it. One leg drags, half-severed tendons flapping. It gathers itself for another charge anyway.

Behind it, in the shadows along the far wall, something small moves. A quick, jerky flinch.

I catch it out of the corner of my eye—just enough: pale face, wide eyes, body pressed into the dark hollow behind a pump mechanism. A child. Knees hugged to chest, fingers clenched tight in worn fabric.

For a heartbeat, my focus splits.

The creature smells the fear. It pivots so fast its bad leg nearly gives. Its head jerks, jaws yawning toward the pump, sulfur-breath turning in that direction. The sound it makes now is different; hungry, eager.

“No,” I say, and the word punches out of me sharper than any blade.

Playtime ends.

It surges toward the recess. I move. There’s no room for curve and flourish now, only straight lines. I sprint along the lip of the pool, stone slick under my boots, arms pumping. Every pulse of god-blood underfoot counts off distance: three strides, two, one—

The walkway ends ahead in a broken drop. I don’t stop.

I plant my last step on the edge and jump, letting the momentum carry me under the sweep of its next claw. The hem of my coat snaps, catching a few errant barbs. My body twists in the air; the world inverts, monster below, red pool sliding by in my peripheral vision.

For a fraction of a second, I’m above its back, weightless. Then gravity remembers me.

I come down hard, boots slamming onto the knotted ridge at the base of its neck. The impact drives its front end toward the stone with a shriek. I ride the drop, knees bending, and ram both blades down into the thick cluster of bone and gristle under my boots.

There’s resistance, dense and rubbery. I snarl, put my shoulders into it, driving until I feel something crack. A jolt runs through the creature—the shudder of a severed connection. Its limbs flail in a wild, weakening storm, claws carving gouges in the stone inches from the pump housing.

“You stay away from them,” I hiss through gritted teeth.

It twitches, keens, then collapses sideways in a heavy, wet slide. I leap off just before it crushes the walkway, land in a stagger, one hand skidding along the wall for balance. The whole cavern vibrates for a moment with the impact. Ripples shudder across the pools.

Then stillness, except for the ragged rasp of my breath.

The knives stay buried in bone until I’m sure it’s done. Its legs give a few last, meaningless spasms; ichor oozes around the hilts and drips into the blood below. When the last tremor fades, I grip the handles and yank them free, breath hissing between my teeth at the strain in my shoulders.

I wipe one blade on a relatively clean patch of my sleeve. The smear it leaves glistens black in the red light. My cheek throbs where acid splashed; when I touch the spot with gloved fingers there’s only a line of faintly warmer skin, already healed.

A small sound comes from the recess. Not a scream this time—more like air shuddering out of tight lungs.

I turn slowly. No sudden moves.

The child is still there, of course he’s still there. The city never misses an opportunity to improve its tragedies. He’s curled into himself in the narrow space behind the pump housing, as if the machinery might swallow him whole if he presses hard enough. Up close, he’s even smaller than that first blurred glimpse suggested—eight, maybe nine at most, all coltish limbs and sharp angles as if someone sketched a boy and forgot to fill him in.

His shirt hangs off one shoulder, seams blown and hastily mended in uneven stitches. The fabric might once have been blue; now it’s a tired, murky grey, stiff with old salt and newer grime. Bare knees jut from ragged shorts, knobbly and bruised, a fresh scrape glistening wet along one shin where he must’ve gone down hard. His toes are naked on the cold stone, nails bitten to the quick, skin gone blotchy with chill and filth.

His hair is the color of old walnut shells, hacked short with no attention to charm or symmetry, bits of it sticking up like he’s been running fingers through it for hours trying to wake from a nightmare that won’t end. It clings damply to his forehead now, sweat or mist or both. And his eyes—

His eyes are too big for his face, dark as spilled ink, whites showing all the way around the irises. They shine too bright in that grubby little face, catching the god-blood’s glow in a way that makes them look fevered. He stares at the corpse. Then at me.

For one, absurd instant I think, irrationally, that perhaps he’s not afraid. That perhaps the story his terrified brain will tell itself is simple: monster, savior, grateful tears, a lifetime of fond whispers about the Vigil’s pale hound who pulled him from the jaws of death.

Then he flinches as my eyes meet his. His fingers claw deeper into his own sleeves, trying to fold himself into nothingness. The motion pulls his shirt higher, exposing a flash of too-thin wrist ringed in the pale pressure-mark of some grown-up’s grip, already shading toward bruise.

Ah. There it is.

Not just fear, but the sharp, particular horror reserved for things that wear a man’s shape and move like something else entirely. For creatures whose eyes catch the bloodlight like a cat’s, whose teeth, when they smile, do not quite stop where human teeth should.

The echo of the kill is still singing in my bones. The god-blood’s hum threads through it, an undercurrent that whispers mine in a voice older than language. The Thirst prowls restlessly, annoyed at being denied playtime, pressing its tongue along my nerves.

I sheath one dagger, then the other, gestures exaggerated so he can follow every motion. Empty hands, open and away from my body. I take one step forward, then stop when his shoulders jump like I’ve fired a crossbow.

“Terrible place for a midnight stroll,” I say, voice pitching toward light. “The view is appalling, and the company’s worse.”

His gaze flickers to my face, then down to my boots, then back to the monster. His throat bobs. When he finally speaks, the word scrapes out high and raw, as if it’s been dragged over stone on the way up.

“I—” He swallows, tries again. “It grabbed my cousin. On the street. I followed.”

Of course he did. People do foolish things when someone they love screams. Children do them faster.

“Is your cousin here?” I ask.

He shakes his head once. Quick, sharp. His fingers dig into his own arms until the knuckles pale under the grime. No need to ask more. The answer is written in the way his gaze skitters away from the heap of flesh at my feet and then back as if he can’t help looking, trying to recognize a shape that isn’t there anymore.

My tongue finds the small cut I bit into it during the fight, and I taste my own blood, copper-bright against the lingering god-iron in the air. Hunger prowls in acknowledgment, but it’s a distant, manageable thing now the danger’s passed. I swallow it down.

“I’m sorry,” I say simply. No bow, no flourish.

The words hang there, heavier than a joke would have been.

His gaze snaps back to me, surprise cutting through fear for a heartbeat. He’s close enough now that I can see the tear-tracks on his cheeks, carving clean lines through the soot and tunnel-dust. One has picked up a rusty smear where it crossed the scrape on his shin, tinting it pink.

“You…” His eyes narrow, studying. “You killed it.”

“Yes.” I tip my head toward the carcass. “One unauthorized god-spawn, neutralized. I’ll be sure to file the requisite forms.”

“You’re Vigil,” he says, like it’s both accusation and relief. His gaze tracks along my arms as he speaks, squinting at the glimpses of carved sigils showing where my bracers have slid back. “One of the… Relics.”

“Cyran,” I supply. “I find things that eat people and make them stop.”

He thinks about that, chewing at the inside of his cheek. His eyes keep bouncing between me and the dead monster, as if trying to slot us into separate categories and failing. The boy and the beast and the thing that killed it, all crammed into the same trembling moment.

“It was going to get me,” he whispers.

“It didn’t.” I let a small, sharp smile show. “Awful at follow-through, that one.”

Silence coils in again. The low thrum of the pools fills it, each pulse nudging at the edges of my awareness. The blood feels closer now, as if my own killing has tuned me to its frequency. If I stand very still, I can almost imagine words forming in the beat.

I don’t stand still.

“There’s a maintenance stair that way,” I say, nodding toward a narrow flight of stone steps winding up the wall nearby. “It comes out by the lower tooth-market. Do you know it?”

He nods, a little too fast, like he’s agreeing with a test he’s not sure he studied for.

“Good. Take it. Go home. If any officer tries to fine you for being down here, tell them Relic Cyran will happily educate them on proper priorities.”

The corner of his mouth twitches, a ghost of something that might one day remember how to be a smile. “Is that allowed?”

“No,” I say. “But it’ll be memorable.”

I step aside, making a clear path between him and the stairs. He hesitates another moment, pinned by the weight of everything down here—the corpse, the pools, me. Then he uncoils from the little trench of shadow he’s made for himself, one careful step at a time.

As he comes fully into the bloodlight, the hollows under his cheekbones look carved. Someone has not been feeding this child enough. His shoulders are too sharp for his age, collarbones like handles jutting under the stretched neck of his shirt. There’s ink smeared along one forearm in clumsy loops, a child’s attempt at a sigil that never quite took—playacting contracts in chalk and gutter water.

As he passes within arm’s reach, the iron tang of old blood and grime and unwashed fear clings to him. His bare foot slips; he yelps, catching himself on the pump housing.

My hand moves without thinking. Fingers close around his upper arm, steadying. His skin is hot against my glove, the rapid thud of his pulse a frantic drum under too-thin flesh.

He freezes, eyes snapping up to mine. Up close like this, I can see the fine tremor in his lashes, the way his pupils blow wide, swallowing the brown until they’re almost all darkness. He smells of alleyways and cheap incense and the faint, stale sweetness of some stolen street-stall pastry on his breath.

“Easy,” I say quietly. “I’ve got you.”

For a breath, we stay like that—me anchoring him, him searching my face for… something. Promise, threat, explanation. Whatever it is, he doesn’t find enough of it to be comforting. I keep my expression level, the performance stripped back to something bare and simple. No teeth. No jokes.

He eases his arm out of my grip. I let go immediately.

“Thank you,” he says, voice barely more than air.

I incline my head. “You’re welcome.”

He retreats toward the stairs, never quite turning his back on me until the angle of the wall forces it. At the first step, he glances once at the monster’s body, once at me. Fear wins. He bolts up the staircase, small feet slapping stone, footsteps echoing until they fade into the city’s deeper creaks.

When the last patter dies, the cistern feels bigger. Emptier.

Just me, the corpse, and the god-blood.

The pools’ slow pulse presses harder now the noise is gone. My Marks prickle faintly under my skin, the carved sigils along my forearms responding to the kill, to the blood, to the whole closed system that turns death into numbers on someone else’s ledger. The whisper that haunts this place brushes my thoughts again, almost-words forming below hearing.
​

I ignore it in the only way I can: by working.

The Tribunal will want proof. I kneel beside the monster, boots squelching in congealing ichor, and set one knife to its ruined hide. It’s tougher now that life has stopped, flesh fighting the blade with stubborn resistance. I pry plates apart, dig beneath them, searching for the branded knot of flesh that bears the Merit sigil.

There. A ring of tight, scarred tissue tucked under an overlapping ridge of bone, lines of an ugly circle burned deep into it. I cut it free with quick, efficient strokes and hold it up. The sigil glows faintly, answering the lines carved into my own arms. Another tidy entry on my invisible account.

“Congratulations,” I tell the dead thing softly. “You just helped redeem the monster that killed you. Circle of life.”

The joke falls flat in the damp air. I shove the strip of flesh into a pouch at my belt. Its heat seeps through the leather, a low, unpleasant throb.

The pools answer with a pulse of their own, stronger, as if in appreciation. The whisper under the beat spikes—my name, almost, shapes pressing at the inside of my skull. Heat licks the back of my neck.

Not tonight.

I straighten, sheathe my knife, and turn my back on the blood. The hum of it clings to my spine as I cross to the stair, boots leaving sticky tracks on stone. Every step up is away from that pull and toward another kind of leash—reports, audits, ledgers. A different pulsing heart in another part of the corpse, this one made of ink and signatures instead of congealed divinity.
​

By the time I push the hatch open, the marrow-mist is rolling thick through the Ribcage again, cool and greasy against my face. The bone-arches loom overhead, ribs of a god long past saving.

Somewhere above, the Tribunal’s towers watch. Somewhere below, the Titan’s heart keeps beating, patient as a debt.

I adjust my bracer where the leather’s twisted, flick a last smear of ichor from my sleeve, and step out into the street, letting the mist close around me. Another hunt done, another Mark earned, and a little boy who will remember that when the monster died, something worse stood in its place and bowed.







# Chapter 2 — *The Audit of the Blood Relic*
### Lysa | POV

I have audited three hundred monsters, and every single one of them claimed to be innocent.

The Spine District smells of ink and judgment, and today, judgment is loud.

I'm standing in the observation gallery above the main hall when it happens—the moment the Magistrate raises his rod. Below me, the ghoul-kin drops to its knees before the whole assembled floor of scribes and clerks and minor officials who have nothing better to do than watch. The hall is vast, ribbed with pale vertebra-stone that curves upward into cathedral arches, and the sound of the creature's wail fills every hollow of it. It bounces off the bone and comes back wrong, smaller somehow, more frightened. I keep my face neutral. I adjust my spectacles.

The sigil burns green.

I write in my ledger: *Procedure 77-B administered. Subject distress: High. Duration: twelve seconds. Mark successfully revoked.*

My quill scratches the parchment and I focus on the scratch, on the precise drag of the nib, on the fact that this is my job—recording what happens, not what I feel about it. Below, the ghoul-kin is hunched over something that used to be its arm, cradling the space where the merit-mark lived. The skin there is blackened, crater-smooth. It isn't bleeding. Merit Marks don't bleed when they go. They just—unmake. That's somehow worse.

I touch the inside of my left wrist through my sleeve. The Tribunal mark sits there, smaller than theirs, inked rather than carved. Different. I tell myself it's different.

I write: *No procedural irregularities noted.*

Three rows of benches below the gallery, the assembled observers shift and mutter. A pair of clerks in junior grey robes are whispering behind their hands. Someone actually laughs—a short, startled thing, quickly swallowed. I look down at them and they don't see me looking and that is, I think, precisely the problem with this place.

The shoulder-tap comes just as the Magistrate begins his closing statement.

"Investigator Voss wants you."

I close my ledger with a clean snap.

***

The walk through the halls of the Spine District is never short, and today it feels longer. I fall into step behind the clerk—a boy barely old enough to be carrying messages—and I note, in the half-attending way that's become habit, the state of things. A cracked tile near the east corridor junction, the third this month. Bone-walls developing stress fractures in the upper archive wing, grey-green with damp. The infrastructure of the Tribunal is failing in increments that the annual maintenance reports describe, cheerfully, as *minor superficial wear.* I know better. I've read those reports three times.

I've written notes in the margins. No one has asked me about the notes.

The clerks' room is full when I pass—the scratch of forty quills, the soft pneumatic *whoosh-and-thunk* of the message tubes moving overhead like mechanical veins. The smell of iron-gall ink and cold tallow candles has been the backdrop of my working life for six years. I barely notice it anymore. What I notice instead is the half-eaten apple someone has balanced on top of the Relic-case file cart—red and browning at the bite, cheerfully oblivious to the weight of what it's resting on—and I'm angry about that in a way I cannot entirely explain. I straighten a stack of leaning case files as I pass. Small corrections. The only kind available to me.

Voss's office is on the upper floor of the seventh vertebra-tower, which means it has a view. That's intentional. Everything about High Inquisitor Voss is intentional.

The boy knocks, opens the door without being invited to, and leaves me standing on the threshold while Voss finishes something at his mahogany desk. The desk is polished to a shine that feels aggressive in this building—everything else is dusty and practical, and then there's *this*, sitting in a pool of grey light filtered through tall bone-windows. On the credenza behind him, an oil lamp and two volumes of the Contract Code and a pot of expensive tea steeping in a cosy. The tea smells like bergamot. It smells like money.

He doesn't look up.

I wait. I clean my spectacles with the cloth I keep in my front pocket. I wait some more.

"Investigator," he says, finally, and slides a file across the desk.

It lands near the edge. Red leather binding. Heavy. I step forward and pick it up and my hands register the weight before my mind does—this is a full-cycle audit file, not a summary, not a preliminary. Someone has been building this for a while.

The name stamped on the spine reads: *CYRAN. DESIGNATION: BLOOD RELIC. VIGIL ASSIGNMENT: RIBCAGE DISTRICT.*

"Sit," Voss says, still not looking at me.

I sit. I put the file on my knees. I do not open it yet.

"There have been complaints," he says. He folds his hands on the desk—small, careful hands, ink-free, which tells me everything I need to know about what kind of work Voss actually does. "Unofficially, at this stage. Dead civilians, three confirmed, two unconfirmed. Missing time during patrol rotations. Reports of *unbecoming conduct* from contract holders in his quarter."

"Unbecoming conduct," I repeat. "That could mean any of fourteen things under the current code."

"Yes," he says. "That is why we need an auditor."

I open the file.

The first two pages are standard intake—classification, original contract date, issuing authority. Thessen's seal is at the bottom of the issuance page, wax the colour of old blood, the sigil pressed so deep it's nearly worn through the parchment. I look at it for a moment longer than I need to. Then I turn to page three, and I find the first redaction—a thick bar of black ink running through a full paragraph under the heading *CREATION CIRCUMSTANCES: SEE APPENDED RITES DOCUMENTATION.* There is no appended documentation.

Page five has another one. Page eight has three.

Between the redactions, the language is extraordinary in its emptiness. *Temple rites, performed per standard protocol. Subject compliance: satisfactory. Disposition during assessment: amenable.* Amenable. I glance up at Voss, who is watching me now with the particular attentiveness of a man who wants to see what I'll do with information before he decides whether to give me more of it.

I keep my face neutral. I turn to the Merit Mark summary.

"Five thousand marks," I say. "That's significant."

"He's been active for—"

"Decades. Yes, I see that." The date of first service is nearly thirty years ago. The date of expected personhood threshold has been recalculated four times, each time pushing the goalposts further out. I read that entry twice to make sure I'm reading it correctly. I am. "He's been recalculated."

"Threshold adjustments are within the Tribunal's discretionary authority," Voss says.

I write: *Threshold recalculated x4. Review basis: unstated.* My quill presses harder than it needs to, and I ease off before I tear the paper. "You want me to audit the city's poster boy."

"We want you to conduct a thorough, impartial assessment of a Vigil protector whose conduct has come into question. Yes." He lifts his tea and takes a precise, controlled sip and sets it back. "With dispatch. The donor community is watching this closely. We want it resolved quickly and quietly before—"

"Before donor relations sour," I finish.

He looks at me with the flat patience of a man who expected this and has already decided it doesn't matter. "Before the situation escalates."

There's a difference. I don't say that out loud. I write it in smaller letters at the bottom of my page instead, pressed so soft no one but me would read it. I flip to the section marked *COMPLAINTS: ORIGINATING PARTIES* and find, under the first grievance filed, another seal. Thessen's. Again. A different seal this time, not the creation-authority, not institutional—this is personal. A Contract Lord filing a personal complaint against his own asset.

Something cold and alert wakes up behind my sternum. I've seen this pattern before, buried in old case files from three cycles back, cases that quietly closed without resolution. I recognized the shape of it the way you recognize a word in a language you've only half-learned—not perfectly, not fluently, but well enough to know what it means.

Someone wants something from this audit. And it isn't the truth.

"I'll be thorough," I say.

Interesting.

“That’s where the marrow-thieves hang, if you ever want to audit someone with even less career stability than I have,” I say. “They’re very enterprising. Steal holy sludge, sell it cut with god-knows-what to idiots who think drinking it will make them immortal.”

I grin. “If it did, do you think I’d still be working for the Vigil?”

It’s an almost musical little murder.

I stop.

For a beat, everything hangs there: the loaves, them, Lysa’s gaze between us.

“Adoring fans,” I say, loud enough that the nearest one flinches. “Truly, fame is a burden.”

Steps beside me. The scratch of quill on paper.

Of course she’s taking a note.

“What devastating observation are we inscribing now, love?” I ask, starting forward again. I nudge one of the loaves with the toe of my boot, sending it into the gutter. “Public terror? Relic as civic eyesore?”

“Citizen response to your presence,” she says. “Useful context for any claims about community safety.”

“Fear is safety,” I say lightly. “Ask the Contract Lords.”

“I’m not auditing them,” she says. “Yet.”

Now that’s a delightful little word to drop into a conversation. Yet.

We move on.

The closer we get to the Vein Markets, the louder the city hums. The city notices her, too.

A cluster of teenagers loitering under a crooked rib—bone-dice clicking between their fingers, cigarettes smoldering—fall abruptly silent as we pass. “Comforting to know I’m not the only one ruining evenings,” I murmur.

“That implies you’re ruining mine,” Lysa says. “You’re not.”

“You wound me,” I say. “Again.”

Her mouth quirks, very slightly. It’s gone in a heartbeat, but I see it.

The market square opens up ahead, a sudden widening of the bone street into a rough circle ringed in stalls. Ribs arch overhead like the cage of some colossal amphitheater. Lanterns hang from their inner curves—glass globes filled with slow-burning phosphor fungus, casting a jaundiced light over everything.

Silence doesn’t fall all at once. It unravels, thread by thread, picked loose by my presence until the whole plane of noise sags. The hissing of the gristle coals sounds suddenly loud. Somewhere a bone-bird on a rooftop gives one harsh, startled caw and then is quiet.

The hairs on the back of my neck rise.

It’s not the silence. I like silence. Silence is an old lover; we understand each other. This is different. This is absence.

My hand goes to my daggers before I decide to let it.

Behind me, Lysa stops.

“Stay here,” I say, already moving forward into the square’s center.

“You don’t give orders to Tribunal investigators,” she says crisply.

I half-turn in time to see her lift something from the inner fold of her robe—a compact baton of dark metal, etched with faint sigils along its length.

Well.

“Absolutely not,” I say. “Put that away.”

“Absolutely not,” she echoes, and that’s the first time I’ve heard steel in her voice aimed at anyone but an absent superior.

“You’re here to observe, not get eaten,” I say. “Let me do my job, darling, or my Merit account is going to get very odd remarks attached to it.”

“If I see a threat, I respond,” she says, stepping to the edge of a fruit barrow. “That’s my job.”

A scavenger-beast. Half-feral god-spawn the Contract Lords insist aren’t their problem because they didn’t make this one on purpose.

People scream.

There’s always a moment, right here, that decides how bad it gets. Fight, flight, freeze. The beast lunges toward the nearest dense knot of bodies—the fishmonger’s stall. I move.

The world narrows to angles and distance. Cart, stall, open lane. Beast velocity versus human panic versus my own speed. The air tastes metallic, thick with adrenaline and the tang of its musk.

“Over here, you half-cooked mistake,” I call, drawing one dagger, then the other. The bone hilts are reassuringly solid in my palms, sigils cold against my skin. “Wrong buffet.”

It snaps its head toward me at the sound. Good. Come on, then.

“Sloppy!” I sing, stepping in and letting one blade kiss along the joint of its front limb. Black ichor wells, hisses where it falls.

It bellows, a sound more steam vent than lung, and swings sideways. I duck, feeling the rush of air as claw passes an inch above my spine. The square constricts around us—stalls, walls, people. No open room for the pretty, sweeping spins I could manage down in the cistern. We’re pressed in close here, fighting in a bone cage.

Surprising. Most Tribunal types start quoting codes when frightened. She’s giving orders that might actually get someone out alive.

The beast swipes again. I pivot, weight on the balls of my feet, letting the claws whistle past. Another nick along its side, another gout of smoky gore. We make a circle of it, the two of us—monster and slightly worse monster—turning around a shared center.

“Do not engage,” I throw over my shoulder. “You’ll only encourage it.”

“I am not—” she starts.

Something small darts at the edge of my vision. Not the beast. Lower.

A child, perhaps eight, maybe nine, darting from beneath a toppled stall. Wrong place, wrong time—little body jolted loose from wherever it was hiding by the shudder of our dance. He—or she, it hardly matters now—sprints three steps, then freezes.

Right in the scavenger’s next path.

For one suspended second, all the lines change.

I can’t get there with enough momentum for a killing blow before those claws come down. I can see the geometry as clearly as if Thessen had chalked it on temple stone. The beast is coiled, limb already in motion. The child is a soft, bright mark on the map, eyes wide, feet rooted.

If I dodge, I live. The child doesn’t.

If I keep circling, maybe I get a cleaner opening. Later. After.

My body decides before I do.

I lunge.

The child’s eyes are huge and dark, whites bright against marrow-dusted skin. For a lunatic heartbeat, my mind overlays another set of eyes there—the boy in the cistern, wrists bruised, back pressed into machinery.

No.

Claws hit.

Good. Stay that way.

The beast tries to follow through, to tear and rend. I jam my left arm forward, let the claws bite there instead of deeper, flesh shredding, and use the proximity.

The right-hand dagger goes up.

Under the jaw is a soft spot, if you know where to feel for it. Even rushed, even half-blinded by pain, my hand remembers.

Someone whimpers. Someone sobs. A vendor starts swearing in a stream so inventive I almost applaud. The smell of burned gristle, fish, and ruptured god-flesh sits heavy on the air.

“Tis but a scratch,” I say automatically, leaning a shoulder against the nearest intact crate. My legs have gone slightly unreliable. “Adds character.”

The look she gives me could sour holy wine.

“Sit,” she says.

“I am standing with flair,” I correct. “It’s different.”

“Sit.”

I do not.

Ah.

Not fear of me, then. Fear for me.

That’s… new.

“There was a child,” I point out, a little bewildered by this line of argument. “Small, squishy, breakable thing? You might have missed him while you were composing your incident report.”

Her gaze snaps up to mine. The tremor in her hands has moved to her jaw.

“I saw him,” she says. “I also saw the angles.”

Of course she did. She’s been mapping my patrol routes in her head since the audit started.

“You made a choice you didn’t have to make,” she continues. “You spent your body like it was disposable.”

I blink at her. There’s a short, bizarre moment where I genuinely don’t understand the problem.

“That’s what it’s for,” I say, slow. “What else am I supposed to spend?”

She looks at me for a long heartbeat that feels like being laid on an altar again, examined for flaws.

Two words I never expected to hear from anyone doing anything to my body: is that acceptable.

“Yes,” I say, because it is, and because the alternative word—no—hasn’t always been believed, but I have the strangest suspicion she might actually listen to it.

She peels the torn leather back with clinical care. There are four long, parallel gashes raked across my flank, shallow but bloody, oozing more for show than for any real damage. The Marks under my skin tick with the effort of knitting; it will scar faintly, if at all.

She cleans these, too, with methodical thoroughness. The alcohol stings. I grit my teeth and make some flippant comment about liking it rough, because that is what I do when anything gets close to resembling genuine care.

She ignores it.

Her hands still shake.

“You know,” I say, as she winds the second bandage around my ribs, fingers brushing bare skin with each pass, “I don’t bite without invitation.”

The words come out more defensive than I intend, a half-remembered script. Offer reassurance, make yourself the safe predator. It’s almost funny.

Almost.

Her hands stop.

The bandage hangs loose for a beat, her fingers fisted in the linen. When she looks up, her eyes are very, very bright.

“Then consider this a permanent lack of invitation,” she says, each word enunciated with surgical precision.

Ah.

That stings more than the claws.

I laugh, because I don’t know what else to do. It’s a short sound, pressed out of me.

“Cruel,” I say. “I might weep.”

“You might learn,” she counters. “Not everyone wants what you’ve been taught to offer.”

Her voice is softer on that last part, almost too quiet to hear over the returning noise of the market. Almost.

She ties off the bandage in a final, neat knot and sits back on her heels, putting a fraction more space between us. The contact breaks. The warmth of her fingers lingers against my skin longer than it has any right to.

I look down at the white loops around my forearm, the tidy stripes around my side. They’re competent work. Not Temple-tidy, no ornamental flourishes, but solid. Caring, in a way that has nothing to do with artistry.

“Thank you, Investigator,” I say, because the words are required, and because my old teachers would flay me for forgetting manners.

“You’re welcome,” she replies. Then, after a heartbeat, “Try not to make me do that again tonight, please.”

“No promises,” I say. “The monsters here have no respect for my need to appear effortlessly invincible.”

She huffs. It is not quite a laugh. But it isn’t not one, either.

The wardens arrive in a clatter of boots and spears, all breathless importance and the lingering smell of cheap tobacco. One of them—a woman with a nose crooked from too many breaks—eyes the carcass, then me, then the bandage with a mix of respect and irritation.

“Could have waited for backup, Relic,” she says.

“Could have,” I agree. “But then your charming jawline might be decorating the cobbles, and the Tribunal frowns on that sort of rearrangement.”

She snorts. “Fair enough. We’ll get the remains to the rendering pits. Contract Lords’ll want their tithe.”

“Careful,” I murmur. “This one bites.”

She grins and goes to shout orders at her squad. The square begins to lurch back toward a semblance of normalcy. People peel off in twos and threes, muttering, clutching their salvaged goods. Someone rights the fruit cart I vaulted; bruised produce rolls, some of it crushed under boots.

Lysa stands. When she offers me her hand, it’s practical, palm out. No ceremony.

I take it.

Her grip is firm. She hauls me up with more strength than her frame suggests, then releases my fingers at once, as if remembering who I am. Or who I’m supposed to be.

We stand there for a second, too close, the smell of blood and burned gristle between us.

“You’re bleeding through,” she says, nodding at my arm where red is already seeping faintly into the bandage.

“I’ll live,” I say.

“Yes,” she says quietly. “You will. I’m not finished auditing you.”

We start walking again. The square falls behind us, the sounds of cleanup fading into the deeper creaks of the Reliquary’s bones.

She says nothing. I say nothing.

The silence between us is heavier now, thick with things not said. She saw me choose the hit. She saw me bleed. She bandaged me with hands that shook not from revulsion, but from the possibility of loss.

And then she told me, again, that my body is not hers to take.

I don’t know what to do with that particular equation. The temple never taught me this math.

Ahead, the Ribcage tunnels away into shadow and mist, patrol routes sketched in my muscles. Beside me, practical boots whisper on bone. Somewhere behind us, in a different part of the corpse, a file with my name on it is waiting for her next annotations.

The city watches.

The god’s heart beats on.

I adjust my stride, just enough that her shorter legs don’t have to work as hard to keep up.

She doesn’t comment.

We walk.

It’s messy. Loud. Imperfect.

It’s honest about the ways people use each other and the ways they don’t.

I toss back the rest of my glass. The whiskey burns, but it sharpens the edges instead of blurring them.

I pour one more measure. I don’t drink it.

On the table, beside my hand, the condensation ring my first glass left has already dried into a faint, uneven circle. Imperfect but present. Evidence that I was here.

I take three coins from my pouch and leave them under the bottle’s base—more than the remainder of the whiskey is worth. Payment for the space, perhaps. For the reminder that there are places in this city where people are allowed to choose their own chains.

Cyran has never had that.

When I stand, my legs are steady. The room tilts for a heartbeat, then rights itself.

On my way out, I pass close enough to the bar to hear Cairn say, in that gravelly deadpan of his, “If you fall off that table and crack your head, I’m not cleaning it up.”

“Liar,” Seraph purrs. “You’d mop up every drop.”

I step back into the street before I can hear his answer.

Vein-smog curls around me, thick and warm. Above, the dead god’s ribs arc overhead, strung with lanterns just starting to flare brighter as the afternoon leans toward night. Somewhere up there, beyond layers of bone and bureaucracy, the Spine’s windows reflect a different kind of light.

I draw my satchel strap higher on my shoulder and start walking toward the transport capsule.

To save Cyran, I can’t be the person who shouts in meetings and gets escorted out. I have to be the one who smiles politely, writes contextual, and quietly builds a cage of words that Thessen can’t see until he crashes into it.

I speak the language of neutral bureaucracy. Today it tastes like ash.

Tomorrow, I’ll make it taste like justice.

I do not breathe. Which is convenient, because I forget how.

Her hair brushes my throat, a few stray strands escaping the neat braid. It smells faintly of sage and ink and whatever cheap soap the Tribunal issues its scribes. There is a streak of dust on her cheek that the cloak has not quite wiped away.

I do none of those things.

I sit as if carved from the same stone as the alcove, every line of me held rigid so as not to disturb the poor, foolish Tribunal woman who has thought it safe to sleep against a monster.

Her head grows heavier against my shoulder as she slides a little bit more into the warmth between us. A soft, unconscious sound escapes her on an exhale—something between a sigh and a hum.

My throat tightens.

She jerks awake a moment later, of course. Because the universe is cruel and never lets good things linger.

Her head snaps up. The sudden loss of contact is a physical thing, like someone has pulled a blanket away.

“Sorry,” she says, voice rasped with sleep, straightening her spectacles with an automatic gesture. “I didn’t mean to—”

“Sleep, Auditor,” I cut in, more brusque than I intend. If I let her finish that sentence, I might say something unsalvageable. “I’ll watch the rocks.”

“That’s not…” She trails off, studying my face. Whatever she’s looking for there, she either finds or decides it’s none of her business. Her shoulders drop a fraction. “You need rest too.”

“Need is a strong word,” I say. “Want, perhaps. But I’m used to not getting that.”

Her mouth tightens. “You’re healing.”

“I’ll be fine by dawn,” I say. “Go on. Consider it part of my community service.”

She gives me a look that suggests she knows there’s more under that sentence than I’m willing to unpack in this alcove. Then, cautiously, she lets herself lean back against the wall again. Her head tips to the side once more—but this time she catches herself before it finds my shoulder, angling away so there’s a handbreadth of space between us.

It shouldn’t sting. It does.

She sleeps.

Eventually. Her breaths even out, her muscles slacken, the little frown between her brows smoothing with each exhale. Up close, in the thin not-light of the blocked tunnel, she looks younger. Or perhaps it’s just that the armor of professionalism has finally gone off-duty.

I watch her.

Not because I am a romantic fool—which I am absolutely not—but because the alternative is staring at the rock wall and imagining every small shudder is the next collapse that will finally bury me properly.

Under the cloak, her hand rests near her knee. Ink stains the side of her smallest finger. The Tribunal mark at her wrist peeks out where the robe has ridden up, circle of ink dark against pale skin.

Without thinking, I mirror the position with my own hand, fingers curling against the wool a hairsbreadth from hers. Not touching. Close enough that if either of us shifted in our sleep—not that I will be—our skin might brush.

Outside, the bone-storm rages itself hoarse. Gradually, the ping of shards against bone grows less frequent. The wind drops from a scream to a low, tired moan.

Dawn is still a long way off. The tunnel will need clearing. Wardens will dig us out eventually, or I will dig us out myself when the dust has settled enough not to flay us from the inside.

For now, there is this: an alcove carved into ribs, a cloak that smells faintly of me and now of her, a woman asleep at my side who has told me no more honestly than anyone ever has and still chosen to share warmth with the monster she’s auditing.

I sit very still.

I do not kiss her hair.

I watch the rocks. And, when it gets too quiet, her face.

The word hits me like a slap.

Please is… bewildering.

“I might kill you,” I whisper.

There’s no joke left to hang it on. The Thirst is pressing up against the inside of my teeth now, panting. It knows exactly how much better she will taste than any dosed, drunk temple donor, and it wants. It wants in huge, heedless swallows.

“I trust you,” she says.

Three words. No adornment. No manipulation. She’s not saying it for my benefit; she’s saying it because it’s true, and she thinks I need to know that before I tear her open.

“Not up to you,” she cuts in. “This is my choice. Not yours. Not theirs. Mine.”

The Thirst throbs at that, strangely moved. Choice. The idea of blood not demanded, not tithed, not tallied in temple ledgers. Given.

My hand finds her waist.

“No payment,” she says. “No ledger. Just this.”

“Just this,” I echo.

The Thirst howls.

“Cyran,” she gasps.

The Thirst goes wild.

More, it demands. More more more—

My jaw tightens.

“Cyran.” Her voice again, quieter now. The word buzzes against my teeth where they’re still sunk into her throat.

Her free hand finds my face, palm cupping my cheek. Her thumb strokes once under my eye.

“That’s enough,” she whispers.

The Thirst screams.

She is the most devastating thing I have ever seen.

“Hi,” I say, brilliantly.

It comes out on a shaky exhale. My voice sounds wrong in my own ears—rougher, younger. Less armour on it.

She huffs something that might be a laugh if it weren’t weighed down by adrenaline.

“Hi,” she says.

I realize, abruptly, that I am high.

The wound is gone.

“Efficient,” I say faintly. “We should put you on the roster.”

She swats my shoulder. It’s not hard. It still rocks me a little; balance is not my friend right now.

“Don’t make jokes about this,” she says. Her voice wobbles on this.

I catch her wrist before she can pull back, fingers circling the delicate bones there. I’m careful with my grip. My hands feel too strong, too sure now. I could crush marble.

“You stayed,” I say.

“That was the entire point,” she replies, but she’s looking at me like she’s never quite seen me before.

“Most people—” I start, then stop.

Most people would have fainted. Or panicked. Or demanded something in return. Temple donors always did; if not in words, then in the way they looked at me afterward. Like they’d paid for an experience and wanted to make sure they got their money’s worth.

Lysa’s eyes hold none of that.

“You stayed,” I repeat, because I need it anchored somewhere outside my own head, where the blood-high might be fabricating kinder realities.

“I stayed,” she says.

We sit there like that for a long, strange moment. Knees tangled. Her hand in mine, my other still curved, unconsciously, around her waist. Our faces close enough that I could lean forward and…

No.

Not this time.

“This wasn’t payment,” she says quietly, as if reading the direction of my thoughts. “You don’t owe me for it.”

“I disagree,” I say. “Vehemently. But I also know better than to argue consent law with a Tribunal investigator when I am this intoxicated.”

She snorts.

“Cyran.”

“Yes, darling?”

“That,” she says, tapping my chest lightly with one finger, right over the freshly mended flesh, “was blood given, not taken. Try to remember the difference.”

The words land somewhere deep.

Given. Not demanded. Not tithed through a contract sigil. Not coerced as duty or bought as entertainment.

Given, because she chose it. For me.

“I will,” I say.

The Thirst prowls contentedly at the back of my mind, sated but curious. It has never had this, either. It is as bewildered as I am.

“You should lie down,” she says, glancing around the ossuary. “You’re still pale.”

“I am always pale,” I remind her. “It’s part of the aesthetic.”

“Pal-er, then.”

She starts to shift away. Panic spikes—irrational, sharp.

I tighten my hand on hers.

“Stay,” I hear myself say.

She looks back, surprised.

My mask is nowhere to be found. I can feel the absence of it, like a missing tooth my tongue keeps prodding. There is no easy joke lined up, no flirtatious deflection.

“I—” I swallow. “Not for… that. Just… stay.”

She studies my face.

Whatever she sees there must pass her internal ethics test, because after a heartbeat she nods.

“All right,” she says.

She adjusts our positions with practical efficiency, levering me sideways so that I end up half-stretched along the wall, legs out, back supported by old bone. She settles beside me, shoulders touching, our joined hands resting on my thigh. The ossuary is suddenly very quiet.

Outside, the storm mutters. Inside, the dead keep their counsel.

I listen to the sound of her breathing. To the steady, strong rhythm of her heart, a little slower now that the worst is done. To my own, matching it for the first time in… I don’t remember.

Pain is a familiar room. Tonight, for the first time, there’s something new in it.

Trust.

Then I look at him, really look at him—at the way he’s standing slightly between me and the open street like it’s reflex, at the faint nervous flex of his fingers where they rest on the scroll, at the way his eyes soften for a heartbeat when they meet mine.

“What are we to each other now?” I think, and don’t say.

For now, we are this: an overworked scribe with a head full of case law, and a weapon who doesn’t yet know what he wants besides survival, stepping out of a forgotten ossuary and into a city that will not thank us for what we’re about to do.

“Ready, Relic?” I ask.

He offers me his arm, lampooningly courtly.

“After you, Investigator,” he says. “Let’s go teach your masters the difference between devotion and debt.”

I take his arm.

Together, we walk into the bone-light.
